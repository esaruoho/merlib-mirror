<p>Several means are known for obtaining light emission out of fluorescent tubes and lamps. Some are more convenient than others and however similar their outcome may look they only share part of the phenomena involved.</p>
<p>Ever since 1970, when John M. Anderson disclosed his own invention regarding (<a href="./pdf/US_patent_n_3500118_John_M_Anderson.pdf" target="_blank">US Patent #3,500,118</a>) an <em>"electrodeless gaseous electric discharge device utilizing ferrite cores"</em>, there has been a proliferation of similar devices, culminating in what they are now known as <em>"induction lamps"</em> and some manufacturers even call them "RF lighting devices" .<br />
<br />
According to the internationally recognised classification, the RF lighting devices are those <em>devices capable of producing light by using RF energy to stimulate gases contained inside a lamp</em> and not the ones having just circuitry dealing with radio frequency electric signals.<br />
Therefore, it must be stressed that none of them can technically be defined as being radio-frequency lighting devices and by no means they should be confused with our products or viceversa.</p>

<td width="800"><p><strong>Until the year 2003, the radioluminescence effect in the shortwave spectrum of the radio waves (HF) was</strong> <a href="http://www.radiondistics.com/" target="_blank"><strong><em>RADIONDISTICS'</em></strong></a> <strong>most tightly kept secret</strong>, this because the exploitation of this phenomenon in connection with readily available linear fluorescent tubes was and still is the only means to visually investigate the <em>radio-electric transduction</em> mechanism and the behaviour of radio waves being radiated from radio antennas. (see: <a href="/detector_it.htm" target="_blank"><u>A secondary ionization fluorescence hertzian radiation passive detector</u></a> )</p>
<p>The radioluminescence stimulated by radio waves direct bombardment of fluorescent tubes is much more than just a means for detecting radio waves emission. Infact, <strong>the ionizing power of the radio waves in the shortwave spectrum (HF) can advantageously be exploited to energize any fluorescent tube to provide artificial light at a fraction of the energy that traditional electric discharge methods would require</strong>, including those systems powered through an electronic ballast or even modern <em>"magnetic induction lamps"</em> (aka: "induction lamps" for short).</p>

<p>I tubi fluorescenti sono le fonti di luce bianca più efficienti, di gran lunga superiori a qualsiasi dispositivo a LED finora sviluppato.<br />
<br />
Tuttavia, a causa della bassa efficienza nella conversione energetica esibita dai metodi di accensione generalmente impiegati, quali quello galvanico e quello induttivo, l'efficienza generale degli impianti diminuisce drasticamente.<br />
<u>Pertanto, va chiarito che non sono i tubi fluorescenti i responsabili della gran parte del loro consumo energetico, ma i dispositivi, i circuiti o i metodi adottati per la loro eccitazione.</u></p>
<p>Le luminarie basate sui tubi fluorescenti al neon come corpi luminosi hanno mantenuto per decenni il primato del rendimento energetico, dell'efficienza luminosa e della resa cromatica rispetto al resto delle fonti d'illuminazione artificiale elettriche disponibili.<br />
Con l'avvento delle nuove tecnologie il primato plurimo dei tubi fluorescenti è stato attaccato su diversi fronti, non solo su quello del rendimento energetico, ma anche sul fronte dell'estetica e della miniaturizzazione.<br />
A tutt'oggi, però, nessuna nuova fonte di illuminazione artificiale li batte in termini di resa cromatica. Per questo importante motivo, l'industria ha investito notevoli risorse per far riguadagnare terreno ai tubi fluorescenti sul piano del rendimento energetico e delle loro dimenzioni, con notevoli successi anche per quanto riguarda la possibilità di controllo della loro luminosità - cosa questa impossibile da ottenersi coi vecchi tubi e vecchi circuiti elettrici d'innesco.<br />
Tuttavia, i tubi fluorescenti rimangono penalizzati dal fatto che il fenomeno elettrico impiegato per dare origine alla loro accensione, noto come scarica ad arco (attraversamento galvanico dei gas ad opera di corrente elettrica), è intrinsecamente oneroso e necessita di una quantità di energia sotto la quale non è tecnicamente possibile scendere e di conseguenza i valori di assorbimento elettrico delle luminarie convenzionali a tubi fluorescenti non sono più sostanzialmente migliorabili.<br />

<p>Lighting is a major energy consumer. For many buildings, such as commercial and public sites, lighting uses up more energy than anything else.<br />
<br />
The need for artificial lighting is a large and rapidly growing source of energy demand and greenhouse gas emissions. At the same time the savings potential of lighting energy is high. New energy efficient lighting technologies are coming onto the market. Currently, more than 33 billion lamps operate worldwide, consuming more than 2650 TWh of energy annually, which is 19% of the global electricity consumption.<br />
It is estimated that energy consumption for commercial lighting accounts for something in region of 25% to 40% of their entire power requirement and substantial savings can be made in this area by designing and installing the right units and controls based on the most efficient equipment available.<br />
Substitution of standard or obsolete lighting units by high efficiency lighting units can typically generate savings of between 20% and 70%.</p>
<p><strong>Today, fluorescent lamps light over 80% of the building floor space.</strong><br />
<br />
In conventional discharge lamps, the energy required to supply the light source is provided by direct or low frequency alternating electric currents, which require the presence of electrodes within the lamp to trigger and maintain the discharge. The presence of electrodes places severe restrictions on lamp design and is a major cause of failure, hence limiting lamp life. Fluorescent lamps operate with thermionic cathodes; that is to say, an electron emitting material (such as barium oxide) is coated onto the electrode. The inevitable evaporation of emitter material during life causes unsightly darkening of the end of the lamp and results eventually in lamp failure. Conventional fluorescent lamps have rated life of around 5,000-20,000 hrs, while electrodeless fluorescent lamps are rated at 100,000 hrs.<br />
The prospect of developing "electrodeless" lamps has challenged the lighting industry for more than a century, since <em>Tesla</em> demonstrated in 1891 that light could be produced in the presence of a high frequency electric field. The introduction of lamps based on the electrodeless principle for general purpose lighting has been hampered by the lack of readily available, compact, cheap and reliable driving electronics. However, in the last decade, progress in semiconductor electronics and power switching technology have made this approach commercially feasible.</p>
