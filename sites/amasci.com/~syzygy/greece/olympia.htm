Olympia (Ολυμπία)

Olympia is of course famous for being the site of the original Olympics.  The first official records of the winners of the Games date from 776 BC, but by this time they were well established.  All Greek citizens (but not women) were able to attend the Olympics and compete in them.  While the Games were in progress, there was a sacred truce across all of Greece; no cities were allowed to fight with each other.

The Olympics were no longer held after 393 AD, due to a ruling by Theodosius the Great which forbade all "pagan festivals."

Nike of Paionios
Now in the Archeological Museum at Olympia, this statue's base contains an inscription which states that it was dedicated by the Messenians and Naupactians to commemorate their victory over the Lacedaemonians (Spartans).  This battle apparently took place in 421 BC.
Hermes of Praxiteles
Praxiteles was a famous sculptor in ancient Greece and this is one of his masterpieces.  The god is depicted holding the infant Dionysus.  In his right hand he would have been offering something to Dionysus, probably grapes.
Temple Of Hera
The Temple of Hera, or Heraion, dates from the 7th century BC, making it one of the oldest monumental temples in Greece.
Olympic Stadium
The Stadium had no seats except for a few for judges (visible on the right embankment).  Spectators would sit on the grassy hills.  You can see a man about to cross the finish line in the photo.
Lyn at Olympic Stadium Entrance

Fallen Column Drums From Temple of Zeus
The Temple Of Zeus was a massive Doric temple and is considered to be one of the definitive examples of the Doric style.  Its massive foundation remains, but the columns have been toppled due to an earthquake in the 5th century AD.

The Temple contained a huge gold and ivory statue of a seated Zeus.  Sculpted by Pheidias (who also made the statue of Athena in the Parthenon), it was very famous and was considered one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World.
