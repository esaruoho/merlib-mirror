 Commercial Influence in Public Schools 

 C ommercial presence did not
 arrive at the invitation of this policy. Channel
 One , which broadcasts infotainment, Sponsored
 Educational Materials (SEMs), some fundraisers and
 bookcovers all provide a conduit for bombardment of
 commericalism in public education. Darnell Edmon, a
 Seattle highschool student, asks "Shouldn't schools be the
 one place we can get away from advertisements?"

 Channel One: 

 Channel One is a twelve minute commercially produced TV
 infotainment show with two minutes of ads geared toward
 teens. Each principal decides wheter to sign a three to
 five year contract with Channel One. The contract
 stipulates that the school must show the program in 80% of
 the classrooms 90% of all school days. Teachers may not
 turn the sound down. Schools with Channel One sacrifice
 six taxpayer-funded school days per student each year.
 K-III, the owner of Channel One, is a media conglomerate
 which according to the Wall Street Journal "controls a
 large proportion of the media going into U.S. classrooms"
 and "is plowing ahead with an aggressive strategy to
 profit from molding young minds." The chairman of K-III,
 William Reilly, calls schoolchildren "a good niche
 market".
