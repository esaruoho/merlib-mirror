Wishlist!

All that stuff I want, but haven't gotten around to (or can't afford to) buy myself.

Actually, there isn't really all that much here. I guess I'm not that material these days, but we'll see. I'm also not going to list computers, or stereo equipment, or any of that sort of thing here—I'm pretty picky, and most everything I'd want would be too expensive to ask for from someone else.

  • Amazon.co.uk

The Levenger Editor's Desk

I think these desks look pretty useful. Of course I really needed one last year, but I hope and expect to continue to work on written material, and I can see a role for one well into the future.

It looks like they've replaced the large desk with a folding model, which is US$189. (Plus $16 for a writing pad, which seems like a good idea.)

Ergo Desk makes a similar, more adjustable (and more expensive—US$199, in alderwood), desk. I'm no longer really sure which desk is better—the adjustibility of the ErgoDesk seems like a nice feature, but I don't think they look quite as nice. I've actually seen them, in a Relax the Back Store in Burnaby.

Books

You almost certainly want to check my Amazon lists for books, but the following are some highlights.

Jess: A Grand Collage, 1951–1993

I've been looking for this book since when I was really poor, couldn't find it when I had money, nor when I didn't again, and now I've given in and ordered it from Canada. I saw this exhibit at the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art, and it was awesome. Jess does collage—and not little, dainty collage, but big, robust, complex collage. I'm talking about human-size Tarot cards made with thousands of tiny pieces of newpaper and magazine advertisements. Awesome stuff.

Christopher Alexander (et al.)

I have long lusted after A Pattern Language: Towns, Buildings, Construction (Oxford University Press, 1987. ~US$50.00). What can I say—I love architecture, and I love thinking about what kinds of buildings I'd design, if anyone let me.

In the end, I bought A Pattern Language for myself. But Alexander has a new book—The Nature of Order, a summary of his life's work in four volumes—out from Oxford University Press.

Charles Dickens

No, really! I hated reading Dickens when I was in school—Great Expectations, Oliver Twist, The Mystery of Edwin Drood (okay, okay, I voluntarily read the last one). But I recently read A Tale of Two Cities, and (aside from some of the characterizations being a bit annoying) it was good.

Anyway, the winner here is undoubtedly the 21-volume Oxford Illustrated Dickens, hardcover books with the original illustrations that appeared with the serializations.

Apparently my mother and brother have gone in on these, but they haven't actually arrived yet....

John McPhee

I only have six John McPhee books—Looking for a Ship and The Deltoid Pumpkin Seed (airships!) in trade paperback, and Annals of a Former World in hardcover (which includes Basin and Range, In Suspect Terrain, Rising from the Plains, and Assembling California). McPhee is amazing—he can make pretty much anything interesting (to be fair, he tends to pick quirky enough topics that they'd be interesting anyway).

The books that sound especially interesting to me are

  • The Control of Nature (big engineering projects, such as controlling floods on the Mississippi)
  • Encounters with the Archdruid (Charles Park, Charles Fraser, Floyd Dominy, and David Brower)
  • Heirs of General Practice (doctors in family practice)
  • La Place de la Concorde Suisse (the Swiss militia)

The Readers probably aren't worthwhile, as they consist of excerpts from the real books, and I'm perfectly happy reading the real books.

Lea Hernandez

Graphic novels that look pretty cool and are also fairly cheap:

  • Cathedral Child (US$10.95)
  • Clockwork Angels (US$10.95)

Both of these can be had for US$22, signed!

I also really like the looks of Rumble Girls: Silky Warrior Tansie, but I think I'm willing to wait 'til there's a graphic novel. Not that I'd say no if someone bought me all the back issues and got me a subscription for the rest....

Movies

Although I couldn't really believe the hype about DVD when it first came out, we watched a couple of DVDs using M's new iBook, and I saw the light. At this point I can't see any point in buying movies on VHS—DVD is so clearly better that it's worth waiting for DVD releases or forgoing them altogether.

  • Until the End of the World on DVD. (See also The Strange Case of Until the End of the World.)

    My favorite movie, for lots of reasons. I already have the US release on VHS, and a framed poster, too. But Wim Wenders has been showing a 5.5 hour director's cut for years, and the DVD, which they're supposedly working on, is going to be that cut. The tape's only 2.5 hours, so I'm really looking forward to seeing the longer version!

  • Kiki's Delivery Service. Amazingly cute Miyazaki film, in which a young witch is sent into the world to find her way. After some tough times, she discovers that she can deliver packages (with the help of her broom!), meets a boy, averts disaster, and winds up pretty happy. (There's even a dirigible!) [Not available on DVD, as yet.]

Wacky Stuff and Other Ideas

The Brunton Pocket Transit. I don't know, I just think these are cool. I don't have a good reason to have one, and they're wicked expensive (~US$240), but they're fantastic compasses. Blame John McPhee—the Brunton Pocket Transit is the geologist's best friend.

I'm also fascinated by the work of Flushing, Queens, New York, artist Joseph Cornell (maker of Cornell boxes), and have so far failed to acquire any books about him and his work.
