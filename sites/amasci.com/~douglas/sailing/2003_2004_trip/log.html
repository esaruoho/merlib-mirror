04/27/03 (Annapolis)

With less than a week to go before my scheduled departure on May 1st, things
are pretty much as you would expect; totally out of control. The to-do list
keeps getting longer, and the days-left-to-do-it list keeps getting
shorter. But, the important things are getting done. Hey, does anyone leave on
a cruise with everything checked off their to-do list? I know I have never
done it. Seems a little on the cool side to start a sailboat trip, but I suspect
that 'cool' will be the name of the game this year. Nova Scotia ain't the
Caribbean. I would like to look forward to the start of the trip, but so far I
haven't had time. Maybe I should add that to the to-do list?

05/01/03 (lst day )

Spirit is off and running. In addition to headwinds all day, I had some fog/haze this morning out on the Chesapeake. I was only seeing about 3/4 mile, though fortunately my radar was seeing much further than that. This was Spirit's first sail since late June of 2002. She remembered just what to do, but I am still re-learning some of the finer points of single handed sailing. I am pretty worn out this evening. It feels like I have just been through the first day of sailing boot camp. The wind showed some genuine enthusiasm in the second half of the afternoon. I had hoped to make it to Solomon's Island this evening, but I had to abort that plan and puylled into the Little Choptank River to anchor. It is quite a convoluted approach to get in here but the short line to the south is protecting me from the worst of the wind and waves tonight. I would never have made it nearly this far if not for the new sail my Dad bought me. The old genoa had a 150 degree tacking angle but the new sail is closer to 120 degrees; big difference. We are "off and running".

I was motoring out of Fishing Bay near Deltaville, VA this morning,

quickly guess whether to move closer to the buoy or farther away.
Interestingly enough, I was able to get into the Severn this afternoon
without ever touching bottom. Go figure.

05/07/03 (Mobjack Bay)

I'm still anchored in Mobjack Bay, enjoying the seclusion of a rural
anchorage and trying to catch up on my to-do list while I wait
for a crew member to join the boat. The single sideband email is
proving to be an interesting gizmo. You compose the email
pretty much like any other mail software, but the interesting part
comes when you get ready to send it. First, you have to hook up
your computer to the radio. Then, you go hunting ... frequency
hunting. Each radio station that has the potential for receiving your
email can operate on several different frequencies. In addition,
since the radio signal generally has to bounce off the ionosphere a
few times between my boat and the receiving station, and since the
quality of the bounce varies with frequency, time of day, and
sunspot activity, there are generally only a few really promising
frequencies for me to try at any particular time of the day. But,
there's more! Like all of ham radio, these frequencies are shared,
so someone may already be using the frequency you want. So,
here is how the 'hunt' goes. You use a propagation software program
(beats me how it works) to pick out some promising
frequencies for some different stations. Next, you go to one of the
frequencies and listen to see if someone else is already using it.
If so, try another frequency. Once you find an unoccupied, promising
frequency, you hit the 'send' button on your email program.
Sometimes, it STILL doesn't work, so you do the whole drill again
with some other promising frequency. Eventually, you usually
get lucky and find a frequency that works. The single sideband email
stuff is so slow that you have to keep the messages short.
That means text only; no graphics. I also have a cell phone on the
boat that is capable of connecting my laptop directly to the
internet, but so far the single sideband has a greater success rate
than the cell phone, which is worthless unless you happen to be
close enough to a cell tower.

05/12/03 (New York City)

Woke up this morning at anchor in Sandy Hook. The fog had lifted a bit
after yesterday's crazy approach to the anchorage in 1/8 mile visibility.
Now, we can actually see something! Looked like some kind of park ashore.
We had a leisurely morning waiting for favorable tides, then headed for
the 'Big Apple'. There is a big stretch of open water between Sandy Hook
and New York City, and half way across we again ran into dense fog. Sailing
through New York City in 1/8 mile visibility was just not an option, so I
was very glad when the fog began to lift. Our first indication that New
York City was no longer missing was when we saw the top of the Veprazano
Narrows Bridge above the fog as we passed just west of Coney Island (which,
by the way, is not an island at all). As the fog disappeared, New York City
appeared just where it was rumored to be. A fairly powerful cold front was
due to pass through with big winds. These winds slammed us just north of the
bridge. After quite a few reefing drills, life started to resume a more
normal pace when the next crisis surfaced. I discovered one of the steering
cables had come loose. While I was below fixing the problem, Fred tried to
keep us out of trouble with the barge traffic. Later, we paid our respects
to the Statue Of Liberty, then headed up the East River. This is an
interesting river as rivers go. One end is the Atlantic Ocean and the other
end is Long Island Sound. The current runs like hell through the East River,
reversing directions every 6 hours. It gave us a great ride through New York
City. We slipped under the Brooklyn Bridge and watched Manhattan zoom by to
port as the current and sails carried us along. We needed diesel fuel, and I
was amazed to find out there was practically no place along the East River
for a boat to get fuel. We found one place, but the gas dock was closed until
morning. Since we need fuel badly, we will stay here overnight at the adjoining
marina ( $1.50 per foot of boat length ) and hopefully take on fuel tomorrow
morning. Next to the marina is New York's Shay Stadium and La Guardia Airport.
Tomorrow, we're off to Long Island Sound.

05/14/03 (Fishers Island)

The last 2 days have seen a lot of broad reach and down wind
sailing. The wind has really been cooperative. However, my
12 volt charging system has not been very cooperative, and my
refrigeration has become down right uncooperative. The
alternator is just not putting the amps into the batteries
that it should, and I don't think the 110 volt battery charger
does much better. Fred and I brain stormed the problem today,
and I looked at the user manuals and also at the wiring
diagrams I have made over the past couple years. I think I
found at least one wiring problem. The regulator uses 2 little
wires to monitor the state of the 2 battery banks. But, some
prior owner of the boat had run both wires to the starter,
which is normally only connected to the engine start battery
bank. I rewired today to fix that problem, but the charge rate
remains too low. Fred and I will do more troubleshooting when
we reach Mystic tomorrow. The refrigeration gizmo is running,
but not cooling; sort of like an office worker who is always
busy but never really does anything. We suspect a possible
lack of freon, but the user manual says you should never have
to add freon. However, sometimes user manuals can be very
optimistic. We will spend the rest of the night at anchor here
on Fishers Island, then head for Mystic in the morning. A big
ass storm is due to visit the area the following day.

05/22/03 (Gulf of Maine)

date = 5-22-03
subject = Gulf Of Maine
We had a double problem last night just after dark. I don't
like problems, and I REALLY don't like double problems.
The steering wheel suddenly became much harder to turn, and
the compass light started to periodically go out. I started
snooping around in my steering system and isolated the problem
to the steering pedestal in the cockpit. That thing is as
old as the boat, so any changes to it will involve replacement
of the whole thing rather than replacement of a part. It
is just about impossible to get inside the thing to tweak this
or that, so about all you can do is jam grease into any
moving parts and hope for the best, which I did before leaving
the Annapolis area. Last night, I ended up disconnecting
the steering chain and spinning the wheel round and round until
it loosened up. God was I glad that the problem was NOT
a crab pot line tangled in the rudder like I ran into in the
Florida Keys. While Lionel put the steering system back
together, I attacked the now-you-see-it-now-you-don't compass
light. I did continuity checks with the multimeter until
finding the problem, which was a soldered wire that had come
undone. I fixed that with my butane soldering tool, and we
were back in business. The winds died out around midnight and
we have been motoring at 5 knots ever since. Looks like
Shelbourne, Nova Scotia is likely by tomorrow afternoon. East
winds start to get rowdy by Saturday night, so we may be
stalled in Shelbourne for a bit.

05/23/03 (Cape Sable)

date = 5-23-03
subject = Cape Sable
Brrrrrr. A cold, cloudy morning just south of Nova Scotia. Our
delightful friend Mr Fog is once again with us. With a
little luck, we should get into Shelbourne late this afternoon.
I thought we were going to get through the day yesterday without
anything breaking, but noooooo. One of the lifelines broke.
Fortunately, no one's life was depending on it when it broke. The
lifelines on this boat are the standard, wire covered plastic that
you see on boats. I had just plain rope lifelines on my last boat,
and I think I prefer that for 2 reasons. For one thing, you can
SEE when the rope is getting worn and needs replacing. With the
usual yachtie lifelines, you don't know if the wire is good or
ready to break; that little detail is covered up by the nice white
plastic. The lifeline that broke today was clearly rusted and
corroded to the point where it had only a few wire strands
remaining; everything else had rusted away. But, that only became
obvious AFTER it had broken. The second reason I prefer rope
lifelines is that they do not inspire confidence. People in general,
and beginners in particular, do not realize how flimsy the typical
boat stanchion is (the stanchion is the metal post that holds the
lifelines). Those sturdy looking plastic covered wire lifelines
inspire a level of confidence that the stainless steel stanchions
can generally not support. Three of them have broken on my boat
since I have owned it. I think the more flimsy looking rope life
lines encourage a level of skepticism in the lifelines that is
much deserved. There is also the added plus that modern rope is
so strong that the stanchions are going to break long before the
rope does.

05/25/03 (Shelbourne)

date = 5-25-03
subject = Shelbourne
Wow, am I glad I am NOT out in the coastal Atlantic. Wet
and windy today as we wait out the weather here at the Shelburne
town dock. They dredged here last fall, put in a breakwater, and
built new docks. No water or electricity or showers or toilets
or internet connection, but no charge for staying here either, so
that works for me! We were met yesterday by Harry O'Connor,
who is the ambassador for the local yacht club. He was very
helpful in showing us where to tie up and telling us a little
about where stuff was located in town. We called in to Canadian
Customs, expecting to do the check-in over the phone. Instead,
they decided to send 2 officers down from Halifax (2 hour drive)
to check us out. I guess the days of clearing customs by phone
are not as common as they use to be. The 2 officers were very
friendly, asked us a bunch of questions, looked in all the
lockers on the boat, and then cleared us into Canada. Lionel
and Jeff headed back to Boston. They were great ship mates and
I hated to see them go. Now it's just Fred and I on the boat.
We both slept like the dead last night. My propane heater finally
ran the bottle dry this morning. We will refill on Monday when the
stores open up. As far as BTUs produced and fuel consumed, I
don't think my propane heater is as good as a big diesel heater
for serious cold places like Nova Scotia. However, I am very
impressed with the propane heater I installed on the boat. It
uses much less propane than I had assumed it would, and if it is
left on the high flame setting, it makes a major difference in
the quality of life inside the cabin. I think I may shop around
for an extra propane bottle when we get to Halifax.

05/28/03 (Port Mouton)

Spirit played 'bash the wave' all day, with the help of the 80 hp Ford diesel,
until the diesel decided it did not want to play any more. We had to shut down
the engine when it overheated. I thought that the problem was a loose belt, but
even after tightening the belt it overheated again and had to be shut down
long enough to cool off. When it got tired of overheating, it suddenly quit all
together; air in the fuel line. I could bleed the air out of the fuel line, but
the overheating was going to require some time to figure out.
So, we then played 'bash the wave' using only the mainsail and staysail.
Strange that a sailor's last resort is to actually use the sails. We
aborted earlier plans to get into Port Mouton, and opted instead for an
unplanned visit to Port l'Hebert (pronounced 'le bear'). Our guide book
let us down and we ran aground in what should have been deep water. We
backed off, dropped the hook, and I headed for the engine room.
Unfortunately, Spirit headed for the shallows. Aground again, which
set a new trip record for groundings (twice in one day). Removing several
bushels of seaweed from the raw water intake took care of the overheating,
but we were aground for the duration of the next tide cycle. By midnight,
Fred and I were trying to sleep in beds that were tilted 38 degrees (do not
try this in your own home). A friendly fisherman pulled us off the sand bar
in the morning. I'm astounded the deck cleat survived the experience. Fred
and I then motored upwind until we finally arrived at our next anchorage
at Port Mouton. I'm now sitting 3 inches away from the
propane heater Ah, the yachting life...

05/29/03 (Le Have islands)

date = 5-29-03
subject = Le Have Islands
It sounded like the winds would not get too serious until later
today, so Fred and I got up early, pulled the anchor, and headed
up the coast. We were able to sail on tail winds most of the day.
We ended up in an area called the Le Have Islands. The recommended
anchorage between Le Have Island and Cabbage Island was not going
to give us a lot of protection from the stronger southerly winds
that are currently in the forecast, so we sent the anchor down at
a spot where Fred had anchored on his last sailing trip. We are
anchored along the northeast shore line of Bell Island, which
should give us reasonably good protection from the winds. Today
did not have the nasty cold that yesterday afternoon seemed to have,
though it is still cool, overcast, breezy, and occasionally foggy.
I like it here. It reminds me of some of the isolated anchorages
along the west coast of Vancouver Island. The shore line here has
that same rocky, pine tree covered look that I enjoy. I'm looking
forward to doing some kayaking here if the weather cooperates. I
think we will remain 'on the hook' here for at least a few days.
The only crisis today was at the end of our sail when I pushed the
starter button for the diesel engine and nothing happened. I wiggled
the wires on the neutral safety switch, tried again, and she fired
right up. I think my brother was right; that neutral safety switch may
be more hassle than it's worth. It's easy enough to bypass the
switch. Fred and I both agree that my Dad's propane heater is one of
the best things on the boat. Being warm (at least occasionally) is
a good thing.

05/30/03 (still at La Have)

The fog is playing hide and seek with the shore line.
Now you see it, now you don't. The sun is trying hard
to make an appearance, but the fog seems to generally
be winning the atmospheric tug of war. The big winds
that the weather guessers were forecasting never developed,
which is okay with me. Fred's daughter was not able to get
away today, so I think she and her family will try to visit
us on the boat on Saturday. She has to be back home for church
stuff on Sunday, so it may be a quick visit. I guess we will
do some kind of dinghy taxi thing when she gets here. Fred is
really looking forward to
seeing his grand kids again. We did boat chores this morning.
A few days ago, and also about a year ago, the engine
stopped because air had gotten into the line between the
port side fuel tank and the fuel filter next to the engine.
That fuel line rises 20" after leaving the fuel tank, then
drops down toward the filter. My theory was that this 20"
rise was not a good thing, especially when the boat was
bouncing around in choppy water. This morning, I rerouted
the fuel intake and return lines so as to eliminate that
20" rise, in hopes that it might cut down on the air in
the fuel line problem. Time will tell, I guess. I certainly
don't think the change will do any harm. I also tightened
the stuffing box, replaced a broken bonding wire, glued
a piece of insulation back onto the ice box lid, and
connected the radar wire to the CORRECT circuit breaker.
Fred and I may do some serious goofing off this afternoon.

06/01/03 (Riverside, N.S.)

6-1-03
Riverside, NS
The weather guessers were talking about gale force
southeasterlies on Sunday, so Fred and I moved the boat
late Saturday afternoon, seeking more protection from a
southeasterly blow. Entering the La Have River turned out
to be an interesting navigational experience. At Cockawee
Shoal, you either have to go left or right to avoid the
shallows. We were confused when the buoys told us to go
right, since the deeper channel was to the left. Our first
attempt resulted in us accidentally trying to do a little
of the 'left' and then a little of the 'right', which took
us right toward the middle of the shallows. We backed up,
consulted charts, computers, and 2 gps receivers, and
decided to abandon the buoys for a while and stick to the
deeper water, which worked out fine. We looked for a place

create a route from a series of waypoints, and occasionally try to
figure out why the gps did not give the expected answers. In addition to
being one of the few remaining boats with no electric windlass, I am also
increasingly in the minority by not having nav software on my computer. I
don't think this yet qualifies as cruel and unusual punishment for the
navigator, but the trend is in that general direction. My lack of a dvd
player for evening movie entertainment may also eventually get me in trouble
if the crew decides to unionize one day. In the end, the morning navigators
were victorious and the sun came out to reward them for their labors. We
even got to sail the last few miles to Lunenburg. The town of Lunenburg is to
Nova Scotia as St Michaels is to the Chesapeake, or as Friday Harbor is to
Puget Sound. Their main industry has switched to tourism as fishing and boat
building gradually become extinct. We went ashore for showers and had a good
meal ashore before returning to Spirit, where she was tied to an overnight
mooring ball. We heard a severe thunderstorm watch on the vhf. Has summer
finally found Nova Scotia?

08/11/03 (La Have)

= 8-11-03
subject = La Have

I don't think I'm getting any better at sailing, but I am getting better at motoring.
We were leaving Lunenburg around mid morning on a quiet, cloudy day. Patrick was
attacking my dirty dodger windows with cleaners and rags, Max was latituding and
longituding the day's waypoints, and I was looking at the fog waiting for us in the
distance. I glanced at the engine temperature gauge, which has become an increasingly
interesting instrument over the past few months, and noticed that it had started it's
upward climb toward meltdown once again. I killed the engine, turned the boat over to
Max and Patrick, and headed toward the engine room. This time, instead of being clogged
at the thru-hull, it was clogged in the strainer and inside the hose between the
thru-hull and the strainer. It was a different kind of sea weed, too. This stuff looked
like the magnetic tape used in cassette tape players. After removing enough for at least
a couple salads, I pulled out the impeller and found that it had survived this particular
incident. I even managed to remember to turn on the thru hull valve this time before
restarting the engine. By the time it was all over, the engine temperature gauge was happy,
I was happy, and it was foggy as hell. But hey, two out of three ain't bad. The wind and
swells were in our face and our boat speed was less than spectacular, so we called it
quits for the day around lunch time. We anchored off Bell Island near the mouth of the
La Have River. Fred and I had anchored at this same spot a few months earlier. It was
just as pleasant as last time, but a damn sight warmer.

08/13/03 (Port Mouton)

= 8-13-03
subject = Port Mouton

It's been a bad year for fog. Or, it's been a good year, depending on how you
feel about fog. The locals have been telling us for weeks that this is the most
fog they have seen in years. My friend Doug in Halifax cut his summer cruise short
because he saw no point in sailing through an area that you can only see on radar.
In Lunenburg, I glanced at the newspaper and noticed an article about some crop in
southern Nova Scotia's Annapolis Valley that had been ruined by too much moisture.
I don't think the crop dried out much the last few days. We left the La Have area
yesterday morning in rain and no wind, which quickly turned into fog and rain and
no wind. We motored for 8 hours without seeing more than a quarter mile in any
direction. Our entry into Port Mouton was somewhat theoretical, since the depth
sounder and radar said there was land out there, but all we could see was the
occasional buoy. We motored over to the spot where Fred and I had anchored a few
months ago, and we could still not see land. I pointed the bow towards land and
crept in at 2 knots. The depth sounder began falling from 35 feet to 25 feet, we
could plainly hear the waves breaking on the sand beach, and we STILL could not
see land. We almost set a new trip record by dropping the anchor without ever seeing
the shore line, but after I had put the engine into reverse to back off into deeper
water, we could just barely make out a ghostly shore line ahead. I left the spreader
lights on overnight to lessen our chances of being run down by another boat. Today,
the forecast is (you guessed it) fog.

08/14/03 (Shelbourne)

date = 8-14-03
subject = Shelburne

There is a strange bright thing in the sky this morning. Instead of a comforting
gray blur around us, there is blue liquid stuff beneath us and green trees in the
distance. It is all very frightening. I would like to get back into the fog as
quickly as possible, since I have forgotten how to navigate with more than a quarter
mile visibility. We used the south exit from Port Mouton yesterday morning, which
I had not used before. Visibility was slightly better than when we groped our way
in the previous afternoon, but the fog swallowed us again as we headed offshore.
As we approached the Shelburne area, the fog started to break up. By the time we
tied up to the marina dock it was pretty damn pleasant. They had done a lot of work
on the marina since Fred and I passed through there a few months ago. There use to
be a rather thin and slim wooden plank that connected the dock to shore. Walking
along it was a little like walking on a diving board, with similar results if you
fell off. Now, however, the plank has been replaced by a nice stainless steel
walkway. Inside the marina building, instead of a bunch of carpenters pounding
nails and sawing wood, there is a cafe, toilets, showers, laundry machines, pay
phone, and the marina office with internet access. However, instead of staying
overnight for free, it now cost about $30. We walked around town a bit, shopping
for groceries, foraging for food, and getting a free tour of a strip canoe
shop that was just getting ready to open up for business. Max took the helm this
morning as we headed back out toward the wide open spaces.

08/16/03 (Argyle Sound)

date = 8-16-03
subject = Argyle Sound

Yesterday, we walked (and walked and walked) into one of the many Pubnicos.
On our side of the bay there is Lower West Pubnico, Middle West Pubnico, and
West Pubnico. On there other side of the bay there are more Pubnicos. It is
unclear where one stops and the next one starts, but you can do a lot of
walking trying to figure it out. This morning we not only had sunshine, but
wind as well. It's a real shame there were no fishermen watching us leave the
marina this morning, because it was quite a unique departure. The wind pretty
much takes control of my boat at slow speeds, and today it positioned the boat
sideways in the exit from the marina. Max said we had 2' of clearance from the
fishing boats in front of us, and Patrick reported 2' of clearance from the
fishing boats behind us. There was no room to maneuver, so I just tried to
keep the boat between the fishing boats and we drifted sideways out through
the narrow entrance into more open water. I suspect we were the first boat
to depart in such a manner. Once outside, we motored to open water and then
put the sails up. We tacked upwind for a while to round Pubnico Point, then
turned downwind. Patrick was at the helm, and made it a point to break the 7
knot barrier of boat speed. It was a grand day of sailing, although we were
only sailing for 2 or 3 hours before we arrived at Argyle Sound in Lobster
Bay. We slipped through the rather narrow entrance to the Sound, then put the
anchor down and attacked lunch. It's cloudy right now, and we may pick up a
little rain tonight. But this afternoon we will concentrate on just being lazy.

08/17/03 (Deep Cove )

date = 8-17-03
subject = Deep Cove

The Argyle Sound entrance had all the ingredients for a disaster yesterday,
with a hidden underwater rock in the approach that forced us
uncomfortably close to a lee shore. No way I was playing that game again
this morning on the way out, especially with the low tide. In much improved
weather, Patrick set out in the kayak with a leadline to sound the channel
depths while Max and I wrestled with the ground tackle. Patrick never was
able to find the underwater rock on the chart. In the end, Patrick paddled the
kayak through the deepest part of the channel, while Max and I followed
close behind in Spirit. Max said that Patrick could now add 'pilot' to his
resume. After a few hours of motoring, we reached Deep Cove Island in Nova
Scotia's Tusket Island chain after zooming through Schooner Passage with
the flood tide at 7 knots. On the chart, it looks like some giant paw has
clawed parallel north south waterways throughout the area. It is beautiful
here. Though there are a few houses here, all but one are deserted now. A
few sheep roam a mostly treeless island. Off in the distance are green
islands, blue waterways, and a nice white lighthouse. There are 10 foot
tides here, so we spent a lot of time trying to tie up properly. After that,
I decided to sit down with my cruising guide and learn something about the
Maine coast. However, Maine sailors Steve and Sue came over from their
sailboat Neshuma, and in 30 minutes they laid out our anchorages in Maine
so well that I could throw away our cruising guide. It was a delightful
day at our last stop in Nova Scotia.

08/18/03 (Gulf of Maine)

date = 8-18-03
subject = Gulf of Maine

Wow, there are some serious tides in this part of the world. We were guessing
that yesterday's tides changed roughly 11 feet, which is a lot if you are not tied
to a floating dock (we were not). We said farewell to Nova Scotia this morning.
The forecast is not great, with the wind blowing generally from the direction
we want to go. But, no nasty weather in the forecast, so we decided to go for
it this morning. We left Deep Cove Island, which is a little south of Yarmouth,
and we will try to make Bar Harbor in Maine by Tuesday evening. This is about
100 miles, so we will be out overnight. Right now, Max is at the helm and doing
about 5 knots under full main and staysail. With everything close hauled as close
as we can haul it, we are south of where we would like to be but sailing roughly
parallel to where we would like to go. I enjoyed my visit to Canada. We managed
to pick a year with more fog than usual, but better that than a year with more
hurricanes than usual. Ya pays yer money and ya takes yer chances. All things
considered, I think my visit to Canada went fairly well, with the exception of
the day that I managed to sail my beautiful sailboat into an underwater reef. The
plan right now is to clear US customs in Bar Harbor, then spend a week or so
sailing down the Maine coast to Portland. My trip will end in Portland. I'll put
the boat on dry land and make arrangements to have it shipped overland to Seattle.
But, there are a lot of miles between here and Portland, and we will try to
enjoy the remainder of the trip.

08/19/03 (Bar Harbor )

date = 8-19-03
subject = Bar Harbor

We had a pretty good crossing of the Gulf of Maine. After a day of sailing
close hauled under mostly sunny skies, the wind slowly began to die. By
sunset we were motoring over small, glassy swells that played constantly
with the light purples of the evening sky. I spent most of the night in my
bunk listening to the 80 horses in my engine room, then came on watch at
3am. We had a half moon smiling down at us from a mostly clear night sky
full of stars, and I noticed a breeze had sprung up from the southwest. Max
helped me raise the sails and then he went to bed for a while as I sailed
through the moonlight until dawn. The morning light revealed that we had
entered an obstacle course of buoys that I assumed were lobster pot buoys.
Max came back on deck and took the helm to thread our way through the buoys
and their nasty underwater propeller snagging lines as the coast of Maine
began to take shape ahead. We were tied to a mooring buoy in Bar Harbor by
mid morning. I went ashore later by myself, armed with everyone's passports
and the official ship's papers. The actual customs check-in, located about
a half hour's walk from downtown, was somewhat of an anticlimax. All the
customs man wanted was some paperwork filled out with names and addresses.
He didn't even look at the passports. I went back to Spirit to collect my
crew, who I found in the middle of a boat cleaning frenzy. We were all
tourists for the rest of the afternoon, and Max and Patrick spoiled me by
buying me dinner ashore. The fish and chips were good, but not up to
Canadian standards.

08/20/03 (Bar Barbour)

date = 8-20-03
subject = Bar Harbor

We remained in Bar Harbor an extra day to take advantage of an unlikely opportunity.
Max, who lives in Tennessee, actually knew a man who now lives near Bar Harbor. Steve
came by later in his car and picked us up in town for a tour of Mount Desert Island
and a stop for lunch before dropping us off at a grocery store back in Bar Harbor. As
you might have guessed by now, Bar Harbor is on a rather largish island, connected to
the mainland with a road over a causeway. The island boasts the only fjord in the
lower 48 states, but the fjord was sort of a joke compared to the magnificent fjords
of Newfoundland. The pronunciation of 'Desert' is regularly abused by both tourists
such as myself and even the weather bureau when they do their forecasts. The locals
pronounce it with the accent on the second syllable, as in 'a chocolate desert'. The
rest of us foreigners tend to pronounce it with the accent on the first syllable, as
in 'the hot sandy desert'. I guess if nothing else it helps the locals immediately
identify the hordes of outsiders who invade their beautiful little island home every
summer. But, it's not a bad relationship since the tourists help support their economy
before winter evicts all the sunblock covered crazies. In the evening, we rowed over
to 'Fiona' for evening cocktails, appetizers, and conversation. The skipper, Eric, is a
most extraordinary individual. He has made 2 circumnavigations in his home built Westsail
sailboat, and has done both the Panama Canal thing and the Cape Horn thing. Not only
that, but he collided with an iceberg at night and lived to tell the tale.

08/21/03 (Frenchboro)

date = 8-20-03
subject = Frenchboro

We had a great mooring ball in Bar Harbor. We were only a short row into the
dinghy dock. In addition, we had a front row seat to watch the coming and going
of various big ass power tour boats, the big ass 4 masted tour sailing ship, and
the 6 thousand lobster boats that pass through the mooring field 24 hours a day.
Fishemen give new meaning to the phrase 'go to work early'. As a matter of fact,
it is not at all obvious to me that they ever sleep when there are marine
critters to be harvested. I'm not sure why it is necessary for a fisherman to
get up at 2am to check his lobster pots. My guess is that the winds tend to be
calmer overnight and early morning, but it is also possible that some component
of a fisherman's (or 'fishers' as they say in Canada) DNA does not permit sleep
between sunset and sunrise. I slept on deck last night, and when I was woken up,
I could go back to sleep by either counting sheep or counting the passing fishing
boats. We motored over to the little fishing village of Frenchboro today. This
place is located on a small island a couple miles off the coast. They have no road
connection to the mainland, but they do have ferry boat service. We picked up a
mooring ball for $20 and had a nice time here. The harbor has a mix of sailboats,
power yachts, and work boats. Ashore we found a combination museum, gift shop, and
library with internet access. We walked down a road that started out as a concrete
street in town and ended up as a foot path in the woods. For dinner, Max and I stared
in amazement as Patrick enthusiastically massacred a boiled lobster.

08/23/03 (Camp Island)

date = 8-23-03
subject = Camp Island

A busy day at the office. By midmorning we had generated enough enthusiasm
to motor away from our mooring ball and raise sails. We were soon bashing
upwind with the staysail and 2 reefs in the main. Max and Patrick did a
great job to steering the boat clear of the lobster pot floating obstacle
course, but I had the helm less than 5 minutes before I snagged one on the
rudder. We dropped sails and I went over the side in my drysuit. The lobster
pot line had managed to get into the tiny opening between the bottom of the
skeg and rudder, and I freed the line without too much trouble. We then
continued on toward our destination, with lots of reefing and unreefing
drills along the way. The anchorage on the north side of McGlathery Island
had been recommended to us, and our friends who had recommended it were
anchored there. Our first anchoring attempt held well, but left us pretty
exposed to the wind. We pulled up the bruce and tried anchoring in more
protected waters with the delta. This anchor eventually started to drag,
and we had a scramble to get all our ground tackle back aboard. We were
leaving the anchorage to find something better when we heard a mayday on
the vhf. Our friends at anchor had drug ashore and were now on the rocks.
We returned to help out if we could. Patrick got in the kayak and prepared
to paddle a long tow line to our friends. But, a fisherman saved the day and
pulled the sailboat off the rocks, then towed it toward a near by boat yard.
We motored over to another anchorage we had picked out on the chart which
turned out to be a good home for the night.

08/25/03 (Rockland)

date = 8-25-03
subject = Rockland

Today was an unqualified success. I made it through the whole day without snagging any
lobster pots. It's amazing to me that I could sail through 10 million crab pots in the
Chesapeake without any trouble, then snag 2 pots in 2 days up here on the Maine coast.
We motored past North Haven in the morning. I think there were more boats in the town's
mooring field than there were people ashore. Boaters must come from miles around just to
put their boat on a North Haven mooring ball. Once into West Penobscot Bay we ran up the
main and genoa and sailed the remaining short distance to Rockland. The bay in front of
Rockland was amazing. I have never seen so many big wooden sailing schooners in one place
at one time, all under sail. A passing yachtie explained the phenomena. Apparently, the
fleet of tour boat schooners go out for a one week cruise during the summer, and they
all leave on Monday morning. So, we accidentally timed our entry into Rockland just right
to see the show. We also saw one bad ass no nonsense zillion dollar high tech racing sloop.
Max and I both figured it was one of the 12 meter Americas Cup boats, but Max hailed the
skipper as it passed by and apparently it was some other class of racing boat ( UPPER class,
I do believe ... I would have to save a long time to be able to afford one of it's winches ).
Rockland also has a healthy population of mooring buoys, and we grabbed one for the night.
To pay the fee, the guide book said to look for the obvious white 3 story building. It was
neither 3 story nor obvious, but we eventually found it and enjoyed some shore leave.

08/26/03 (Port Clyde)

date = 8-26-03
subject = Port Clyde

We tried something a little different today. Since we had another relatively short
distance to cover, we made a lunch stop. Max steered us into Tenants Harbor after a
successful morning of lobster pot buoy dodging. We grabbed a vacant mooring buoy and
enjoyed a nice lunch on a wonderfully warm sunny day. There were plenty of other boats
on mooring buoys to gawk at, a nice mixture of pleasure yachts and lobster boats. There
didn't seem to be much of a town ashore, but there was a nice collection of houses
along the tree covered shore line and the usual fishing piers piled high with empty
lobster pots. It was typical, delightful, coastal Maine. There was enough of an afternoon
wind to sail, which is exactly what we did. Max and Patrick helped me do some experiments
with genoa fairlead positioning and tacking angle, but the wind was so variable that the
results were pretty blurred. As this activity went on, Patrick continued to dodge lobster
pots. The concentration of lobster pot buoys on the Maine coast is absolutely astounding.
I'm not just talking about the shallow coves and coast lines. I mean EVERYWHERE. It is a
full time job for the helmsman to constantly dodge these things. One of the highest
concentrations of buoys was out at the 100' depth line. If you plan to cruise in this
part of the world, think about skipping Maine and going to Nova Scotia. But, by all means,
come back and visit by car. It is an exceptionally beautiful coast line. We played with
the genniker again before the wind died in the late afternoon, then motored into lovely
little Port Clyde for the night.

08/27/03 (Ebencook)

date = 8-27-03
subject = Ebencook

Maine is really spoiling us with this weather. Today was another gorgeous day
of warm sunshine and blue skies. The wind was on the nose once again, but once
again we did not have far to go. The result was several hours of tacking upwind,
which only someone as crazy as a sailor can appreciate. We didn't see the
outrageous concentration of lobster pots today that we have seen in the past,
although there were still plenty to dodge. One reason was that our
course took us well offshore at times today. We passed mostly south of Muscongus
Bay and took the longer route south of Damariscove Island before bearing off into
the Sheepcot River and our eventual anchorage in Ebencook Harbor. Lots of
interesting names on the charts of these Maine waters. We tried to set the
genniker again late in the day, but I botched the job of fully closing the
snap shackle at the head of the sail and the whole thing fell down just as
it was almost hoisted. Fortunately, the sail was still in it's sock so it was
relatively easy to retrieve from the water. There was the blur of a rain shower
off to the west, and Max made an exceptionally accurate forecast when he estimated
30 minutes for the rain to get to us. Patrick spotted a suspicious patch of sea
weed near our first anchoring spot, and some friendly cruisers came over in their
dinghy to confirm the fact that there were rocks hiding under those weeds. So, we
reanchored. We had just enough time to set the bruce, stow the soggy genniker,
and retrieve the hailard from the masthead before our very brief wind and rain
shower, followed by a very nice rainbow.

08/28/03 (Sebasco)

date = 8-28-03
subject = Sebasco

Another day of magnificent summer weather, good winds, and long offshore tacks
with the genoa and a reefed main. Things were pretty routine until we got close
to our destination of Sebasco Bay. Our angle of heel caused a glass bowl to
commit suicide, and some of the broken glass got on a fresh batch of my oatmeal
overload cookies, which were cooling on the stove top. I decided to throw them
out, rather than take a chance on someone accidentally eating broken glass. The
crew and I were all heartbroken at the loss. We grabbed a mooring ball and headed
ashore in the dinghy, but were sidetracked by a friendly pair of gentlemen on a
sloop with a diesel engine that refused to budge when they pushed the start button.
The skipper showed me where he thought the starter was located, but I had to tell
him he was pointing at the alternator. I was delighted to find somebody who actually
knew less about diesel engines than I did. I used his multimeter to check the voltage
at the battery and the starter, which were both a healthy 12.5 volts. I then put the
voltmeter on the connection that lead from the starter to the starter solenoid, and
asked the skipper to hit the start button. The voltage jumped, but was far less than
12.5 . However, interesting enough, the engine began to turn over a moment later. The
skipper then went through his pre-heat process, and was able to start the engine. The
bad news is that I had not done a damn thing, and the starting problem could easily
come back at any time (very possibly corrosion in the wire terminal connections). But
hey, you can't argue with success.

08/29/03 (Potts Harbor)

date = 8-29-03
subject = Potts Harbor

We had a shorter distance to cover today than yesterday, and tomorrow's run into Portland
will be shorter yet. This condition lends itself well to late starts, which is exactly what
we did. After dropping the mooring ball, we motored the short distance to the channel and
then ran the sails up. It was good that we had such a short distance to go, because tacking
with the genoa is not the way to get somewhere fast. We checked one of our tacks and found
it to be 150 degrees, which was roughly the tacking angle of my old genoa. I need to drag a
sailmaker out for a day of sail trim analysis. I keep wondering why my upwind performance is
so pathetic. But, pathetic or not, we had time to burn so we left the genoa up and enjoyed
the sail. We later turned downwind as our destination approached. It seemed a good time to
play with the genniker again. We got the sail up okay, but it was reluctant to do a proper
jibe in the light winds, and it was even more reluctant to come down. The hailard block at
the head of the mast had jammed once again, requiring me to eventually go aloft once again
to get he sail down. When I headed up the mast it was getting a little bumpy on the water,
and a little bumpy on the water translated to a whole lot bumpy 62 feet above the water.
As the mast did it's best to catapult me into the great unknown, I resolved to either install
an elevator on the mast or replace the genniker hailard block before setting the genniker
again. The sail was eventually wrestled into submission and we headed into Potts Harbor to
grab a mooring buoy for the last night of the cruise.

08/30/03 (Portland)

We were practically within spitting distance of Portland this morning, so there was even
less motivation than usual to get up and get going. We eventually managed to generate
enough enthusiasm to drop the mooring and motor offshore under sunny skies and calm winds.
Patrick found some sailing wind along the way, right on the nose as usual. By the time we
reached the channel that leads toward Portland the sun had disappeared but the wind had
not. We began a series of short tacks up the channel as Max and Patrick wrestled with the
sails and navigation. The galley supplies gauge was just about on empty, but I managed to
dig up enough for a brief lunch, consumed in the midst of a flurry of tacking activity.
Tacking the genoa requires rolling in the sail in order to get it around the staysail stay.
Then, the big sail has to be unrolled on the other side and sheeted in tight. The entire
activity is very calorie consuming, and a pretty good workout for the arm muscles. We
eventually surrendered the sails to the engine, and motored the remaining distance into
Portland. The city seemed to be a nice one, roughly the size of Halifax. There were 2 huge
oil drilling platforms tied to a pier, and the Coast Guard sailing ship Eagle was in
town. This marks the end of my sailing adventure, but not the end of my adventures in
general. The next adventure will be sending the boat to Seattle via truck, and the following
adventure will be rescuing my savings account via employment. Remember Sanderson's third law:
'shit happens, don't wait for retirement'. Best wishes to all from Spirit and me.
