How to Escape the Chinese Finger Trap: A manual for changing

your life

by Tim Dunn, M.A.

Green Lake Counseling Center

2319 N. 60th. St., Seattle, WA

(206) 525-0829

Chapter 1:
Human Nature

When I was a boy, there was a cheap toy that was very popular. It was called a Chinese finger trap. It was a tube, perhaps a half-inch in diameter and about 5 inches long. It was woven of the stuff that baskets are made of. I can still smell the harsh, hay-like smell of mine. It was dyed yellow and green, with some cheap dye that came off on my sweaty hands.

You were meant to stick your fingers into it at each end, and then try to pull them out. Then the loosely woven tube would contract, and trap your fingers. There was only one way to get your fingers out of the trap. You had to push towards the center of the trap with your fingers, to relax the weave. Then you could easily pull them out.

Human nature is like that trap. This is why so many people have personal difficulties that they seem unable to resolve. It is this simple fact about human nature that is the source of difficulty in changing our negative moods and emotions, our negative perceptions about ourselves and others, and our behaviors that are counter-productive for us. Instead of coping with these problems, people escape them.

People find countless diversions. They try alcohol, sex, clinging relationships, drugs, recreations and pastimes, and every pleasure or escape known to the endlessly fertile imagination of mankind. They use (or abuse) meditation, religion, the power of positive thinking, the consolation of philosophy, political power, wealth, social status, and everything else that mankind has hungered for throughout the ages. In the end, however, they are still caught in the Chinese finger trap.

Buddha said, life is suffering. In the end, all attempts to escape the fact that life contains suffering are doomed. A gloomy philosophy, you say? Not entirely bleak, I would respond. Because, THERE IS AN EXIT. Don't try to escape it. Go towards it. Seek out what hurts and frightens you. Push your fingers towards the center of the trap.

I will confess, this sounds crazy or perhaps macho or masochistic. IT IS NOT. It is the way out, as surely as pushing your fingers towards the finger trap is the only way of getting your fingers out of it. This is a simple idea. It is a great idea. Nobody likes this idea. This idea works. If you can believe this and put it into practice, you don't need to read the rest of this book. You can just get on with it instead. If you don't believe me, or understand me, keep reading.

I have read that in India, they catch monkeys by putting food in a jar. The mouth of the jar is big enough to admit the hand of a monkey when the hand is open, but too small to admit the hand of a monkey which has been balled into a fist. According to the story, the monkey will not open his fist and let go of the food in order to escape from the jar. He closes his fist on the food, and won't let go. I don't know if this really works, or is just a parable. I think it is supposed to be a moral tale which cautions against excessive greed.

I take a slightly different moral from the story. That moral is, you can be trapped by wanting something which you cannot get. If you stubbornly refuse to be disillusioned by your experiences, but instead cling to a futile hope of getting some impossible prize, you will be trapped like the monkey.

In the first case, it is seeking to avoid something which cannot be avoided which keeps you trapped. In the second, it is vain hopes which keep you trapped. These two stories illustrate a simple principle of human nature. People seek to avoid pain, and to experience pleasure. Freud called this the pleasure principle.

Freud called people's tendency to keep pulling to get out of the jar and the finger trap the pleasure principle. By this he meant that people seek immediate pleasure and immediate avoidance of pain, without considering the long- term costs that such a policy might bring. It is the pleasure principle that causes people to fool themselves. It is fooling themselves that gets people into trouble. It is mindlessly pulling away from pain and mindlessly seeking pleasure that causes people to greatly increase the troubles that life brings them.

The alternative is to acknowledge the internal reality of one's feelings and perceptions, and the external reality of facts and consequences. This Freud called the reality principle.

An excellent example of this sort of thing is the famous newsreel clip that most of us have seen of Neville Chamberlin, the British prime minister in the late 1930's. In the newsreel we see Chamberlin getting off of an airplane waving a document and declaiming, "Peace in our time". He had just had a summit meeting with Adolph Hitler. Hitler had gone on rampage in Europe already, but had not yet attacked Britain.

Chamberlin advocated a policy of appeasement. He was deluding himself, and anyone else who would listen, that Hitler would be content with swallowing up pieces of Europe. Since anyone who was paying attention to the things the Nazis were saying and doing would have known otherwise, it is pretty clear that this was a masterpiece of self-delusion.

Chamberlin was a liberal (in the British sense) and a pacifist who wished to believe the best of human nature. I don't know why he had the need to pretend that people never deliberately do awful things, but he did. We all know what came of his policy of appeasement. Chamberlin's position exemplifies the pleasure principle.

I have an excellent drawing of Freud in my office. He is holding a cigar in his right hand. He is balding and bearded, and has a sour and disagreeable expression on his face. One might say that Freud's view of psychotherapy was that one should substitute the "reality principle" for the "pleasure principle". I have to admit, I think that he was right.

This may lead you to suppose that I am a sour and disagreeable person. Personally, I don't think that is the case. I think that life can be lots of fun. It's just that I don't believe that life can be nothing but fun. Like Neville Chamberlin's successor, Sir Winston Churchill, I believe that life sometimes demands of us a willingness to contribute "Blood, sweat, and tears". In marked contrast to Neville Chamberlin's position, Winston Churchill's attitude exemplifies the reality principle.

I am a psychotherapist. People come to me because they are unhappy or because they are having problems with their relationships. My wife, Merry-Rae Dunn, does the same thing as well. I decided to write this book because we see how futile people's lives are when they get "caught in the Chinese finger trap". We spend an awful lot of time trying to convince people to push towards the trap, instead of pull away from it. Some people never do, and we fail to change their lives. Others do, and then there is a startling revolution. They are freed, and they begin to build new and better lives.

They do this by ceasing to do the obvious, logical thing. Their goal is pleasure, or at least the escape from personal pain. They succeed by going TOWARDS the pain rather than AWAY from it. It seems like an absurd paradox. To go escape their pain, their fear, their sorrow and depression, their anger and futility, they delve into it. When they do this, they relax the weave of the trap which they have built for themselves, and they can begin to escape it.

I am not advocating taking vows of poverty, chastity, etc. I am not saying that pleasure is bad and pain is good. I am talking about facing yourself, not turning from life's pleasures. You may protest that you already know yourself and your feelings. You may even be offended by my presumption in suspecting that you don't. If you are a happy person with a history of happy and successful relationships, if you lead a fulfilling life, if you are setting goals and meeting them, then maybe you are right.

On the other hand, you may just be like the rest of us. You may be full of longing, anger, bitterness, anguish, hurt, humiliation, sorrow, and despair. Even if you think that you are not, you may be wrong. People fool themselves. It is all part of the Chinese finger trap.

Freud and his disciples discovered a fantastic array of ways in which people fool themselves. He gave them complex technical names. He called them defenses, and claimed that years of study were needed to comprehend them. He may have been right about that, but anyone can understand that people fool themselves. Even if you have never studied psychology, you probably have watched people fool themselves. Did it ever occur to you that you might be one of them?

I don't mean to be offensive. I'm no different than you, or anyone else. I am talking about human nature. Your nature, my nature, your friends' nature, your mother's and father's nature, your brother's and sister's nature, your spouse's nature, and your children's nature. We all fool ourselves, because it is the easy way out. The problem is that we pay a price for doing this. This price is to stay stuck in the Chinese finger trap.

Like ostriches, we stick our heads in the sand. Like ostriches, we get eaten by the wolves that we don't want to see. There is a direct connection between the things that we hide from ourselves, and the painful and distressing experiences that keep cropping up. That is because we cannot really make feelings and perceptions go away by pretending that they are not there. They seep out, like buried toxic wastes, and pollute our emotional environment. They influence our moods, perceptions and behavior in ways that we don't even see. Freud called our emotional toxic waste dump the unconscious.

Our lives are being poisoned by our emotional toxic waste dumps. There is only one answer, and that is to clean them up. Cleaning up actual toxic waste dumps is an expensive and difficult proposition for the country. Cleaning up our emotional toxic waste dumps is an emotionally expensive and difficult task for us personally. In each case, however, the price of not doing the chore is much worse.

This is not happy news. It is surely no wonder that Freud bashing has become a popular sport in the "New Age". Slaying the bearer of bad news, as some emperors of old did, is also part of the Chinese finger trap. Slaying the bearer of bad news is really just the same thing as sticking your head in the sand. It doesn't make the bad news go away, but we don't have to hear it any more. Maybe you think that I am a bearer of bad news. As I write these words, I wonder how many people are going to bury heir heads in the sand by putting this book down, and forgetting about it. I hope that you are not one of them.

People who bring good news are always welcome. Television evangelists, New Age gurus, positive thinkers and the like are always welcome. There is a school of psychology, quite reputable in academic circles, called Cognitive Behaviorism. Cognitive Behaviorism says that you can just substitute positive "self-talk" for "negative self- talk". Cognitive Behaviorism is really just dressed up positive thinking.

Perhaps you have heard of "affirmations"? They are quite the thing these days. Yet another example is those subliminal audio-tapes that people keep buying. Listening to them is supposed to make you feel happier, more positive, more assertive, more confident, etc.

These people tell you that you can make the bad feelings and thoughts go away by just thinking or believing something nice, instead. They are finding fame and fortune by telling people to bury their heads in the sand. All of these people are just fooling themselves. If you let them, they will fool you too.

I'll illustrate my point by briefly discussing two common problems that people experience, depression and anxiety. Depression, mysterious and seemingly insoluble, is a common price to pay for burying one's face in the sand. Why are you down, people ask? You don't know, you reply. Its' just everything, or nothing. It is the pull of the planets, political happenings, mysterious bodily malfunction, or the nature of life and the universe.

I have seen lots of depressed people, and I have been one. I have NEVER seen a case that was not attributable to specific identifiable causes. There are essentially two classes of depression.

In the first kind, people are angry with someone, but won't admit it. Instead, they turn their anger inward, against themselves, and become depressed instead. They do this for various reasons.

They do it to protect the other against their anger. They don't think they have a right to be mad at that person for that reason, because someone told them so. They don't think they ever have a right to be mad at anyone, for any reason, because someone told them so. They don't dare alienate the person they are mad at, because they are dependent on them. Sometimes, they want to protect their idealized image of that person, so they deny the whole situation that is making them mad.

In the second kind of depression, the depressed person is like the monkey with his hand in the jar. He wants something or he feels he needs something which he cannot have. He refuses to let go of it, and so languishes. Often, it is too sad to remember what it is that he wants but cannot have, so he forgets why he is so depressed.

Frequently, the reason he doesn't know what it is that he wants but cannot get is that he is busy trying to pretend that he has it, or that he does not really want it after all. In his heart of hearts, he won't admit that he really can't get the thing he wants. He doesn't really give up and get on with his life. He has just pretended to.

In my experience, it is a lot easier to get people to let themselves be angry and get over "angry" depression, than it is to get people to really give up on something that they deeply yearn for. There are countless millions of people who don't know that they are bleeding inside because their parents didn't love them, or love them enough, when they were children. When later relationships go wrong, they think that the failure of that later relationship is why they are so miserable. This is not really the case. If you truely feel that your parents deep down loved you, the failure of subsequent relationships is easily gotten over.

When people know what it is that they want but cannot get, when they have recovered the memories and connected them to the feelings, then they are no longer depressed. They are sad. Being sad is different from being depressed. When you are sad, you can begin the process called mourning. You can begin to feel grief at your loss, and eventually let go. Then, unlike the monkey with his hand in the jar, you can pull your hand out and go on your way in life.

If you are experiencing the angry kind of depression, you are like the child stuck in the Chinese finger trap. You are stuck because you won't let yourself be angry. If you go towards the trap, and let yourself be angry, you stop being depressed. Then you are out of the trap.

As with depression, there are two kinds of anxiety. The first is, once again, disguised anger. I see this kind a lot in people who have been neglected or abused by their parents. Physical, sexual, and emotional abuse leave deep scars. People who are not angry about having been mistreated in these ways will usually be either depressed or anxious.

People who are anxious who have been abused may be denying their anger for the same reasons listed above. That is to say, they may have had such deprived childhoods that they cling to their illusions to avoid the pain of knowing how alone and unloved they were. People who have been actively abused, however, have another, more potent motive for denying their anger. They are afraid that their abuser will kill them if they ever dare let themselves get angry with them. This is often true even when the abuser is dead.

The second kind of anxiety might be called "existential dread". This kind comes from receiving inadequate, possessive, or ambivalent mothering at an early age. People who feel this way usually feel very dependent on close relationships, and frequently have difficulty asserting themselves. "Women who love too much" and "relationship addicts" are common but rather pejorative terms for people who feel a central core of dread and despair that they seek to escape in clinging relationships.

As with depression, the first kind of anxiety, caused by the refusal to be angry, is easily gotten over. You let yourself be angry, and if you have correctly identified who you are mad at and why, then viola! You are not anxious any more. You have escaped the Chinese finger trap.

The second type, existential dread, is much harder. This is because you must suffer much, much longer. The sufferer is striving to avoid almost overwhelming anxiety, dread, helplessness, rage, and depression. The exit is the same, that is to say that one must face the feelings, experience them, and act despite them. Unfortunately, this is a big job that takes years of hard work. With agonizing slowness, you escape the Chinese finger trap.

Phobias are unreasonable, obsessive fears of specific things. Phobias are just anxiety "bound" in specific disguises. You may fear flying, birds, open places, enclosed places, germs, etc. You can just avoid everything that makes you phobic, and watch your life shrink, or you can face what you fear.

My comment about facing what you fear is a little misleading. There is a half-way measure called systematic desensitization. You face the thing you think you fear, gradually building up your exposure to it. This may work-but you will find that something else pops up to take its place if you don't uncover the roots of the phobia.

What I am saying is that you really fear something else than what you think you fear. If you don't find out what fear you have disguised from yourself by concealing it in a phobia, you haven't faced what you really fear. In that case when you face what you think you fear, you are just out of one Chinese finger trap and straight into the next one.

I have to admit that I haven't been entirely honest with you. I've been trying to pretend that the only reason that I believe in facing one's feelings is because it is the only policy that works. It is true that I believe that it is the only policy that works, but I really do admire the courage and integrity that I see at work when people do face their feelings. The truth is, I have an idealistic, or perhaps macho streak. I feel that it is noble to seek out the truth and speak it despite the cost that one pays.

Maybe it comes of having seen one episode too many of "Profiles in Courage". I suppose that it dates me to discuss Profiles in Courage, but actually I was quite young and impressionable when this television series was being shown on the air. John F. Kennedy was its creator, or sponsor, or something like that. Every week we would be told of the story of some starry-eyed idealist who was a "majority of one". The series was about people who supported and propounded unpopular views because they felt that they were morally correct. I was too young for me to remember any real details, but I still remember the thrill that I got from identifying with people who stuck their necks out like that.

I remember watching some movie on television in which John Wayne bit the bullet, literally, while some calvary surgeon dug a bullet out of his leg without anaesthesia. I was impressed. I thought he was great.

I remember being a teenager when in the movie "Butch Cassady and the Sundance Kid", they were being chased by a posse. They stood before a cliff, a very high cliff. Below them, far below them, was a deep river. Butch said to the Kid, "We're going to have to jump." The Kid protested, "Jump! I can't swim!" Butch replied, "Don't worry, the fall will kill ya!" And they rode over the cliff. They survived, that time. I loved it.

I think that life and psychotherapy are like that. If you take your courage in both hands and press on, it is beautiful. I'm not really just proposing a practical solution to life's problems. I am affirming an ethic that I have loved since I was a little boy. In a nutshell, the ethic is be brave and be honest with yourself.

According to my dictionary, the word integrity is based on the Latin word integrare, meaning to make whole. Integrity is defined as having unimpaired moral principles; honesty; soundness; and the quality of being whole or undivided. I believe that making one whole is what psychotherapy is all about. It is common to claim that psychotherapy is amoral, as it is based upon the science of psychology. Science is supposed to be morally neutral. Alternatively, psychotherapy is thought of as being a branch of medicine, and thus the ethic of psychotherapy is to heal.

I don't really believe that the moral dimension of honesty, of integrity, wholeness and integration, can be separated from the scientific or medical dimension of healing. I believe that the moral quality of being whole and undivided is the same as the medical quality of being healed. I believe that the practical fact is that it is necessary to face one's feelings is inseparable from the moral value that it is good to be honest and whole. I believe that the structure or character of human nature makes it so.

Self-deception divides the mind. Dividing the mind makes it sick. Truth unifies the mind. Unifying the mind makes it well. To be well and to be moral, if morality consists of knowing oneself and being true to oneself, are the same thing. Socrates and Hippocrates gave us the same message. To be honest, to be moral, and to escape the Chinese finger trap are the same thing.

Now, this morality, this ethic, is not everyone's morality or ethic. I was sitting in a doctor's waiting room about a year ago, reading Reader's Digest. I read one of the little quotes that they place wherever they find room to put them. It said, more or less, "I feel that it is better to be kind than to be honest, because I am far surer of what is kind than I am of what is true." There, then, is a different ethic. I appreciate this ethic. I understand that kindness is a virtue. But when it is carried to the extreme of self- deception, then I believe that it is harmful.

In fact, sometimes it is people's morality that induces them to lie to themselves. This was Freud's big point. Freud was interested in sex. In the Victorian era, nobody was supposed to be interested in sex. Being interested in sex was considered immoral. In the Victorian era, people lied to themselves about their sexual impulses so often that Freud thought that was the only thing that people lied to themselves about.

Freud seems to have thought that lying to oneself about sex was the only thing that got people in trouble. Today we know better. Lying to yourself about anything that you feel strongly about will get you caught in the Chinese finger trap.

In my experience, people get in trouble most often by lying to themselves about love and anger. Of course, some people still lie to themselves about their sexual impulses. Especially "kinky" ones. Sometimes, self-deception about love, anger, sex and jealousy get knotted together, as in the famous Oedipus complex.

You probably know what the Oedipus complex is, but just in case you don't, I'll give you a brief synopsis. As in the ancient Greek myth, little boys and girls are supposed to want to replace their same-sexed parent and be their opposite-sexed parent's sexual partner. They can't, so they feel possessiveness, jealousy, and inferiority to their same- gendered parent who they feel is the rival for their opposite-gendered parent. These feelings are supposed to be at their height at age four to age six, after which they are repressed. Repressed is a word Freud invented for fooling oneself into forgetting a wish, feeling or perception that hasn't really gone away.

I didn't used to believe in the Oedipus complex. After working with people as a therapist, however, I found the evidence for it was unmistakable. It didn't usually cause much of a problem for most of my clients, unless there were unconnected complications in their relationships with their parents. I find that the Oedipus complex creates real problems for those who experienced trauma, sexual abuse, or inadequately empathic parenting during the "Oedipal period". The Oedipal period occurs between age four and age six, which is when these feelings first crop up.

Anyway, I didn't mean to launch into an exposition about Freud's favorite obsession, the Oedipus complex. Freud thought that the Oedipus complex was the primary human problem, which is not a view that I share. I think that the Oedipus complex is just about the least of people's problems. My point is simply that sometimes people fool themselves about what they really feel because they are trying to be more more moral than they really are. They are trying to be more moral than anyone can be. They are trying to censor feelings. The truth is, you can't banish feelings just because you don't like them.

People just tie themselves into knots when they try to treat feelings and impulses as though they could be subjected to moral guidance. They can't. Behavior can and should be subjected to moral guidance, but it simply doesn't work to try to "legislate" feelings, perceptions and impulses. Trying to make your feelings moral is just another way of putting your fingers into the Chinese finger trap.

Human nature is a given. If you understand human nature, you know what people are likely to feel and want in a given situation. Its' no good trying to change this. Our choice is to fully accept ourselves and to be whole, or to reject parts of ourselves and be divided.

Chapter 2:
The Truth Will Set You Free

Actually, I like the amended version better: The truth will set you free, but first it will make you miserable. My comments on accepting yourself may be misleading. If you are feeling some painful feeling which you have been avoiding, the first thing you must do is accept those feelings. This means that you must get in touch with those feelings. That is a phrase much beloved by psychotherapists. It means that you must actually, fully, consciously experience whatever feelings, perceptions and memories you have been avoiding.

When I say accept yourself, I don't mean that you should just give up feeling bad about yourself. If you feel bad about yourself, the first step in self-acceptance is letting yourself feel bad about yourself.

This probably sounds a little vague. Perhaps it would help to give some concrete examples.

I was once a volunteer phoneworker at a crisis line. This meant that I showed up for one four hour shift a week, and answered the telephone. People would call up and talk to me about whatever concerned them. Sometimes they just wanted to chat or get some information, and sometimes they were in extreme distress. Sometimes the callers were so distressed that they felt intensely suicidal. The suicidal callers would call us up in the hope that we would be able to make them feel better, or at least talk them out of committing suicide. Most callers fell somewhere in between the two extremes.

As I have already written about anxiety and depression, I'll tell you about two calls I answered while I was there. One was from a very depressed man, and the other was from someone who was very anxious.

The first caller proceeded to tell me that he wasn't sure that life was worth living. He said that he had always felt that way, but that sometimes he felt that way more than other times. I asked him why he felt it so intensely just then. He said he didn't know. He didn't think that there was any particular reason. He was more interested in talking about the meaninglessness of life and so forth. He was getting quite philosophical, but not any happier. Listening to him was beginning to be reminiscent of reading an existentialist novel.

Since, in my experience, people do not get intensely emotionally disturbed about abstract issues and events remote from their lives, I was skeptical. My caller wasn't giving me any clues to go on, so I went hunting. I reflected that the thing that people feel most strongly about are their relationships with their families and their relationships with boyfriends and girlfriends. We got lots of calls from people who had just broken up with their spouses or boyfriends or girlfriends.

I asked my caller point blank if he had recently broken up with a girlfriend. As a matter fact, he told me, he had just had his fiancee break off their engagement and end their relationship. He wondered why I had asked. I told him that I had found that people were often depressed when they had just broken up with a girlfriend. He seemed surprised. I then asked him if he was mad at her. Once again, he seemed surprised. Why, he wanted to know, would he be angry?

I responded that it was normal to be angry with one's girlfriend under such circumstances. He was skeptical. He didn't think that there was any reason for one to be angry in such a situation. After all, if two reasonable adults decide that they were not mutually suited, then why should they not part in friendship? I asked him if he was glad that he was no longer engaged to her, then. No, he replied, he wasn't glad. It was her decision, not his.

I was beginning to feel as though I were wrestling with a greased pig. I said that I would be angry if I were in his place. I went on to claim that anyone would be, and that it was quite extraordinary that he wasn't angry. I even implied that there would have to be something nameless wrong with him if being in his situation didn't make him angry. I dwelt at length on how wretched it was of his fiancee to have jilted him without reason, and so on. I made it clear that I thought his girlfriend was out of line, and got quite incensed with her on his behalf.

My caller began to grow more animated as he began to agree with me. Well, he allowed, he did think it was a little shabby of her to do that to him. She really ought not to have done that. He wasn't angry, of course, but, well, annoyed maybe. I suggested that he ought to be madder. He was entitled to it after all, I said. I would be madder, I claimed. My caller began to grow more heated as he agreed with me more and more.

I asked him how he felt now. With some surprise he said that he didn't feel depressed at all any more. I explained the connection between his depression and his not allowing himself to be angry, and suggested that he should allow himself to be angry more often. He rather gratefully agreed with me, and we said good by to one another.

The second caller that I promised to talk about was in a similar situation. He had just suffered a disappointment in a love relationship, and was suffering from a nameless dread. Like the above mentioned caller, he saw no relationship between his anxiety and his recent loss of a relationship.

Like the first caller, he had intermittently suffered from the problematic feelings that he was calling about for years. Just as the first caller had, he initially denied feeling angry with his ex-girlfriend. I proceeded to say more or less the same things to him that I had said to the first caller. Like caller number one, he struggled against acknowledging and accepting his feelings. As I had with the first caller, I persisted. He then began to be aware of and accept his anger with his ex-girlfriend, and to his surprise, his anxiety attack immediately ceased.

These two situations had a number of common points. The person had no awareness of a feeling that he was refusing to acknowledge and accept. When it was suggested that they might feel something which they would have difficulty accepting, they struggled against the suggestion. Finally, when they did acknowledge and accept the unpleasant and unacceptable feeling, they felt better.

Naturally, one might ask, if it was such a relief to acknowledge and accept these feelings, why didn't they do so earlier? In these two cases, the answer was fairly simple. They wished to avoid the feelings of guilt which they would feel as a result of acknowledging and accepting a feeling to which they did not feel morally entitled. When I, an uninvolved stranger, made it clear that I did not find the feeling unacceptable, they were able to feel that perhaps it was not immoral to feel angry with the women who had disappointed them.

In both of these situations, the act of avoiding the pain is connected with a price that the person involved does not connect with the avoidance. In each situation the person involved is simply "pulling against" the pain in the hopes of escaping it. In each such case, the Chinese finger trap metaphor describes the dilemma and the only way to escape it.

In other situations, the pain being avoided is that of shame, humiliation or embarrassment. Sometimes it is fear of social rejection that leads people not to accept their feelings. Sometimes people do not wish to surrender cherished illusions because they feel that reality has nothing good to offer in return. Yet another reason is because the feeling or perception being avoided is itself too painful or frightening.

When I told my wife about these two phone calls, she wondered if perhaps the callers had been unable to accept their own anger with their ex-girlfriends because it was humiliating. Naturally, anyone could find a rejection humiliating, as it may be taken to imply that the rejected person was inadequate or not good enough.

I doubt if that was the case in these two examples, however. I had not enabled these two men to accept their feelings by pointing out that anyone could be rejected or jilted, often for reasons that had nothing to do with judgements about their worth as men or as persons. I had enabled them to accept their feelings by pointing out that it was normal and acceptable to be angry in such a situation. To me, this clearly implies that the question was one of the acceptability of anger itself rather than one of feelings of low self-esteem in the person involved.

As I remarked above, however, I do agree that the avoidance of humiliation is a common cause for the inability to accept a feeling or perception. This is especially common in people who have had early experiences in which they felt humiliated, unimportant, or powerless. To some extent, we have all had such experiences. Some of us have had them at an earlier age than others, or more often, or have had them delivered at the hands of people who extremely important to us.

I once spoke with a man who was going through the throes of a divorce. For him, the hardest part of the experience was that he felt that he had been tried and found wanting. This made him feel, not guilty or inadequate, but rather enraged and humiliated. It became clear that his tendency to explode into rage when he felt humiliated had been a factor contributing to the failure of his marriage. He was not my client, so I did not feel that exploration of this would be appropriate, but I began to see that the avoidance of feelings of humiliation ruled this man's life.

My experience is that if someone devotes their whole life to the avoidance of a specific feeling, and seems very primed to have the perception that others are trying to make them feel this feeling, then it is a very major project to put them in touch with what they are avoiding. This is especially true of people in whom feelings of humiliation predominate.

Instead of exploring the question of why he felt so humiliated, I ventured the rather ordinary remark that perhaps it was simply a question of them not having been right for each other. He seemed to find this remark quite soothing. It is not the sort of thing I normally say, especially when I am acting in a professional capacity. Most people, or at least most people who have positive expectations about psychotherapists, expect them to be soothing. I find that if one always attempts to be soothing, then one risks encouraging people to deceive themselves, to their ultimate cost.

If I were to speculate about why this man felt as he did about his impending divorce, I would guess that he had had intensely humiliating experiences in his family at an early age. It is almost certain that he would deny this, and would only slowly uncover the memories involved if he explored the matter. From the standpoint of turning his life around, it would have been more valuable to him to have pointed out his extreme sensitivity to feelings of humiliation and to have explored how he became so sensitive to these feelings. Unfortunately, he would probably have construed such a remark as an attack meant to humiliate him.

By avoiding these memories and feelings, he is perpetuating them. If he explored these feelings and memories, he would be able to re-evaluate them from his adult perspective. He might then begin to feel more self-esteem, and lose his inordinate sensitivity to humiliation. Naturally, this would be a painful process.

No one likes to dwell on painful memories. Furthermore, he may be pretending that these things never happened. He may be busy telling himself that the people who humiliated him really think quite highly of him. It may be that the only way that he can continue to believe that is by deceiving himself, since he probably has considerable evidence to the contrary. Loosing this belief would no doubt be painful. His first experience in the process would be one of greater feelings of hurt, humiliation and inadequacy, not less. He is, no doubt, another victim of the Chinese finger trap.

This question of hanging onto illusions about one's parents is one which I encounter a great deal. There are few emotional pains so intensely acute as feeling that your parents really don't love you and never will. Most people who have been given cause to believe this will do anything in their power to pretend that they haven't. Even people who think that they have accepted the feeling that their parents don't love them usually haven't really, fully accepted these feelings.

I call situations like the two telephone calls which I mentioned earlier "miracle cures". This is not to imply that there is anything phoney about the results. It is just that I wish to make it clear that most painful situations do not lend themselves to such a quick resolution.

These situations had two central factors underlying them that made for quick resolution. Situations that are grounded in an inability to accept one's anger about a specific, recent occurrence can usually be resolved quickly. Secondly, situations that are grounded in having feelings that one's emotional response to an occurrence are unacceptable socially or morally, as these were, can usually be resolved fairly quickly and easily.

As you might infer from my remarks, situations that have their foundation in childhood experiences, and situations founded on feelings and perceptions that are rejected for reasons other than moral or social acceptability, are much slower and harder to resolve.

In general, it is harder to resolve those situations which involve an unsurrendered wish. I have compared the unsurrendered wish to the fable of the monkey with his hand in the narrow-necked jar, trapped by the size of his closed fist. Within the fist, of course, was the food that the monkey wanted. The monkey wouldn't let go of his hope that he could get the food contained within. Thus, he was trapped, perhaps to die of starvation. If he would stop fooling himself, he would give up on the food in the jar and go seek his dinner elsewhere. The hardest situations of all to resolve involve both the Chinese finger trap and the fist in the jar at the same time. These latter situations can be very complex and difficult to resolve.

I remarked earlier that feeling unloved by one's parents was the one of the worst feelings that people try to avoid, and in my experience, one of the most common. Frequently, these situations are not only very painful, but involve both seeking to avoid pain and attempting to get something impossible. Both the Chinese finger trap and the fist in the jar apply here.

I call it a reality that some people are unloved by their parents, and sometimes it really seems to be a reality. Other times, it is a question of people being less loved than they needed to be, or simply that they feel unloved by their parents. As a psychotherapist, my concern is primarily psychological reality. By this, I mean that the central issue in my work is what people fundamentally believe and feel. There are sometimes layer upon layer, level upon level of perceptions, feelings and wishes. I seek to uncover the hidden levels, so that people can examine and integrate them with the known.

I think of human nature as being like an onion. There are layers and layers in an onion, and one may peel them back one by one. The same thing is true of people. As people progress in their search to know and integrate themselves, as in psychotherapy, they peel back the onion layer by layer. They discover completely unknown parts of themselves.

Some of these parts of themselves have beliefs, needs, perceptions, and wishes either different or completely opposed to those which they are accustomed to knowing. Some people are very surprised by this, and struggle with the very believability of their own emerging experience. Others seem to take it into their stride as thought they had known it all along.

As they delve within and get to know themselves better, people begin to realize the complexity of their own nature. They begin to see that the process of rejecting parts of themselves has created whole layers of themselves that they didn't know existed. They discover that the simple step by step process of attempting to avoid thoughts and feelings which were painful, frightening, or unacceptable has made a whole new territory within themselves. This territory Freud called the unconscious.

The key to discovering this whole new world is to go towards what has been avoided because it is frightening, hurtful, or unacceptable. The motive for this voyage of discovery is to undo the consequences which this policy of self-alienation has spawned.

The deepest, darkest territory of them all is that territory spawned by feeling unloved by your parents. People who have suffered physical, sexual or emotional abuse by parents are perhaps obvious examples. Other examples are people who have been literally abandoned by a parent. People also feel unloved when they are not protected from harm by parents. They feel unloved when their parents do not give them empathic and concerned parenting. They feel unloved when their parents are preoccupied with their own problems and needs.

They feel unloved when their parents are alcoholic or drug addicted. They feel unloved when their parents are withdrawn into a secret world of depression or mental illness. They feel unloved when their parents are selfishly possessive of them. They feel unloved when their parents prefer someone else to them. They feel unloved when their parents relate to them as status symbols rather than as people. They feel unloved when their parents consider them to be a burden. They feel unloved when parents seem to feel that they have no right to their own feelings. They feel unloved when they feel that their parents regard them as something to be exploited, not as a person to be related to.

In general, when it is the caretaker who has given a person reason to feel unloved, the feelings are stronger, deeper, and more horrifying. The earlier and the more traumatic the experiences of feeling unloved are, the more nightmarish, horrifying, and overwhelming the feelings are. It is usually a person's mother who is one's caretaker, and so it is experiences with the mother that are the most powerful. The caretaker is the most important person in one's life for both of the above reasons: They are the primary relationship in importance, and they are the earliest relationship in one's life.

Thus, it follows that feelings about one's mother's attitude towards one, stemming from early experiences, are the most fundamental. These build the foundation of one's life. Positive perceptions of one's mother in infancy and toddlerhood give one a fundamentally positive attitude towards life. Negative ones contain the seeds of tragedy, horror and madness.

These experiences are the very heart of darkness. I believe that all of the worlds great ills and tragedies start with such experiences. I believe that this is where great villains and the deeply tormented start their careers. This class of painful, frightening and unacceptable feelings and perceptions is beyond the scope of this book. To enter this territory and struggle to reclaim it is a heroic struggle requiring more than a determined attitude and a new philosophy. It requires genius, divine intervention, or professional help of greater than average competence.

This book is oriented towards those feelings having to do with experiences later in life. Once one can walk and talk, one has entered the realm where the painful, frightening and unacceptable experiences may be uncovered and reclaimed given only a new attitude which seeks rather than avoids them. This book is for helping you to deal with experiences that occurred after you were two and a half years old, on to the present. This book is for people like you and me.

This book is for people who can't assert themselves, or who feel that they don't deserve very much in life. This book is for people who find themselves in the same kind of doomed relationship over and over again. It is for people who don't want their next marriage to go the way their last one did. It is for people who are stuck in an unsatisfactory relationship plagued with coldness and distance, or hot with anger, or turgid with frustration. It is for people who are depressed and angry, within normal limits, and don't know why. It is for people struggling to throw off addictions and compulsions, phobias and anxieties, a string of failures, and other ordinary life problems. It is for people whose children don't obey, or who have bad relationships with their children. It is for people who wonder where it has all gone wrong, and how to fix it.

These kind of patterns and feelings, when chronic, have their roots in the avoidance of feelings and perceptions which one had in one's childhood. They center on how your mother and father felt about you, and how they treated you. They center on your reactions to those experiences, and how you dealt with those experiences.

This is where the sources of your chronic, life-long difficulties are to be found. I testify to this with absolute commitment and certainty. The seeds of freedom from these chronic, long-term problems lie in what you have not allowed yourself to think and feel about these experiences. That is why I say that the answer to your problems lies in going against human nature and in doing what is painful, frightening and unacceptable.

Chapter 3:
Your Childhood, Your Family

I made some pretty sweeping claims when I wrote that it is in your early experiences with your family that your current life's troubles lie. Most people find this far- fetched. It seems contrary to common sense to most people to claim that something that happened so long ago is ruling your life now. It also strikes people as immoral and unacceptable to make this claim, as they feel that I am blaming their parents for their troubles. This seems disloyal and unfair to most people.

Let me make it clear, I am not blaming your parents for your problems. Whatever failings your parents may have had must inevitably have been the result of their own childhood experiences. On the other hand, if your push towards the finger trap long enough, you will probably go through a period of blaming your family, and then come out the other side. If you suspect me of blaming your family, chances are that you blame you family right now, and don't know it.

This is because one of the ways that we prevent ourselves from having feelings that we don't want, is to pretend to ourselves that it is somebody else that feels the feeling. Freud and his disciples called this projection. The analogy is to a movie or slide projector. The image is actually inside the projection machine, but it is seen on the screen. Thus, if you think that I blame your family, and you react with horror or distaste, you probably feel the blame which you imagine that I feel, are horrified by your feeling, reject it, and project it onto me.

If you delve into that feeling, you will find that on the second level, you are feeling a conflict between feelings of loyalty to your parents, and feelings of blame towards them. This is pretty uncomfortable, and probably makes you feel guilty. It makes you feel guilty because it goes against your conscience. It goes against your conscience because your conscience is really the voice of your family in your mind. Not surprisingly, your family doesn't want for you to blame them. This might make them feel guilty, ashamed, humiliated, or afraid of punishment or rejection.

On the third level, blaming your parents may make you fear that you will be rejected by your parents. It also conflicts with your natural desire to believe that they love you. If you begin to doubt that, then you lose your idealized image of them. If you lose your idealized image of your parents, then you will begin to feel lonely, unloved, and perhaps not worthy of love. You will feel bitter and disillusioned. You will begin to doubt whether love is really possible in the world, and you will have to wrestle with feelings of bitterness and cynicism. The very value of the world, life, and your hopes and current relationships will come into question.

If this seems far-fetched, I can only say that I have watched people progress from the first level, on which they feel confident that their parents love them and that their troubles are unconnected with their parents, to the second level on which they feel loyalty conflicts about their negative feelings about their parents, to the third level in which their personal value and the value of life itself are questioned. I have watched this not just once, but many times. Simultaneously, I have watched the problems that motivated the person to undertake the voyage of discovery disappear as they delved deeper and deeper into this forbidden territory. I have also made this voyage of discovery myself.

I have so far made the claim that the solution to our problems lies in doing what we have always avoided doing. I have given a few simple examples about the connection between one feeling that someone wanted to escape from and another feeling being avoided. Now I will give an example of someone changing his whole life pattern by facing his feelings about his family.

I will call this man John. Now, let me tell you that this man is not really named John. I will change names and various other particulars in my stories, to protect the privacy of my clients and their families.

Anyway, "John" came to me because he was locked into a custody battle with his ex-wife for his only child, a son. He had just lost a round in court, and was feeling very depressed. John also was having a hard time at work. He kept getting into conflicts with employers, and getting fired. John didn't have many friends anymore, either. It seemed that his wife had gained custody of them, too. Also, John's relationships with women had not been going well. Finally, it turned out that John had been drinking heavily for a number of years, and had a history of intermittent severe depression.

As I explored his past, I discovered that John had been raised by a mother who had been distant, cold, and self- absorbed. She had disguised her indifference to other people under a veneer of an idealistic humanistic philosophy which she preached but did not practice. She used John as an audience, with no regard for his feelings. She also had a knack for discerning who or whatever John came to love, and she would invariably deprive him of it. When she did this, she always had some good excuse, but the outcome was always the same.

John's parents were divorced, and his father took little interest in him. When he did visit his father, his father alternated between being cold and being frankly abusive, both physically and emotionally. Thus, there was no one for John to turn to for warmth or support but his baby sitters. John's mother had no interest in caring for John herself, and she had the means to pay for full-time child are. Naturally, his baby sitters came and went, and they had no influence with his parents. This meant that John was unprotected from his mother's coldness and exploitation and that he was constantly subjected to having his attachments disrupted.

We all have a need to be given what I usually describe as empathy. By this I mean that we all need to have someone show an interest in our feelings, an understanding of our feelings, and acceptance for our feelings. Children need this more than adults, but the need for this kind of caring communication never leaves us. John received little of this kind of empathic communication. What little he did receive of it he got from his baby sitters, from whom he was constantly being separated. Instead of empathy from his mother, he received exploitation.

Most people have difficulty understanding this kind of exploitation. It is not overt abuse, but something subtler. His mother pretended to care about John, but actually displayed indifference sometimes mingled with actual malice. She would force him to listen to her as she would lecture him about the philosophy she did not practice, and never allow him to point out the discrepancy between her words and deeds. She failed to acknowledge him as a human being in his own right, with his own thoughts, feelings, and perceptions. Instead, he was reduced to being a pawn in her games, and she used him as a mirror for her own vanity.

Adding insult to injury, she had made John feel guilty for not living up to the standards she preached. Her reproaches were of the "more in sorrow than in anger" variety. She would explain how it hurt and disappointed her that John was so heartless and irresponsible for acting in ways that were actually perfectly normal for boys his age. John was torn between angry feelings that she was a hypocrite and feeling guilty for not being a better person.

The net result of receiving this sort of treatment from his family was that John felt angry, guilty, lonely, anxious, depressed, and filled with an insatiable longing to have his mother miraculously change into someone who would feel loving towards him and concerned about him. In addition, because we form our image of ourselves on the basis of how our family seems to see us, John had feelings that he was flawed, inadequate, and not really a worthwhile human being.

John's eighteenth birthday finally came, and he went forth into the world. He entered college, in due course graduated, and got a job. He married, had a son, and watched his marriage crumble. He divorced, and became so depressed that he was hospitalized. He got better, found another job, and entered a series of unsatisfactory relationships that were a lot like his marriage. As I wrote above, he kept having conflicts with employers, lost several jobs, and entered into a custody battle with his ex-wife for custody of his son.

As I delved deeper into the relationships and employers that John had had, I began to see a typical pattern. In his relationships, John kept finding cold, distant, exploitative women and trying to form warm relationships with them. In both his relationships with women and in his relationships with employers, he kept trying to get recognition for being a separate person with his own feelings, opinions, and perceptions. What kept happening was that, without being aware of it, he kept reproducing his childhood experiences.

I find that there are three ways that people keep re-experiencing their childhood relationships. The first is that they find someone who really is similar to one of their actual parents and establish a relationship with that person. (Freud referred to this as repetition compulsion, and remarked on the "adhesiveness of libido"). The second is that they induce people to act more like their parent than they would otherwise be inclined to act. (The technical term is projective identification). The third is that they see someone that they are in relationship with as being more like their parent than they really are (which Freud called transference). Frequently, I see some mix of these three ways that people keep reproducing their early relationships.

My impression was that John did see people as they really were. He was not using the third method of reproducing his early experiences. It seemed that in John's case, it was more a question of him finding people like his mother, and then trying to get from them what he could not get from his mother. Naturally, this did not work. It did not work because he was seeking out the very type of person who was certain not to give him the empathy and acceptance that he so desperately wanted.

In John's case, he was reproducing his early relationship with his mother by the first two means that I described. It must seem very strange to you that John would seek out people who were sure to make him unhappy. You probably think that John is stupid, or that he is an example of some rare species. Perhaps you even suppose that I am making this up. I assure you, all of these reactions are mistaken. John is a real person, he is very intelligent, and what he kept doing is quite common.

John did what we all do, for the reasons that we all do it. We all seek out someone like one of our parents to have a relationship with. We all keep trying to get what we wanted but could not get from that parent. Some of us are more fortunate than others, in that our parents were more able and willing to be interested, understanding, and accepting of us. The happier our childhoods were, the more likely we are to choose good relationships later, as we find someone like one of our parents. The fewer and lesser the unmet wishes we had in our childhood, the less we will vainly seek in adulthood.

John was somewhat aware of what he had wanted and not gotten in childhood. In this, he was probably in the minority. Most people fool themselves about this, as I wrote earlier. People usually fool themselves about their unmet wishes regarding their parents. They do this for various reasons. The commonest reasons are the need protect their images of their parents out of protectiveness or in order to pretend to themselves that they don't really have nagging unfulfilled wishes.

As I have remarked, John knew of his unfulfilled needs. John was not aware of the depth of his feelings, however, nor did he realize that he hadn't really given his wishes up. In this, he was fooling himself. Consciously, John had accepted the fact of his mother's character. He had realized that his wishes would never be met by his mother. This knowledge was intellectual rather than deeply felt, however. Deep within, John still wanted recognition, acceptance and love from his mother. He had not really given up, though he thought that he had. His fist was still caught in the jar, even though he thought he had taken it out.

The paradox is this: As soon as you really surrender the hope of receiving your mother's or father's love, recognition and acceptance, you will be free to find love, recognition and acceptance. Once you take your opened hand from the jar, you will be free to seek out food-laden jars with bigger mouths. Only then can you feast.

How people fight this! It is no wonder that they do. It is agony to take one's hand out of that jar. It is a grief-stricken, dark night of the soul. People resist this to the bitter end. In fact, resistance is the very word Freud coined to describe the phenomenon.

In his struggle to avoid this pain, John left therapy with me. He sought out another therapist, who met with him. After talking to John, the other therapist urged John to return to therapy with me. The other therapist understood why John had left his work with me, and understood that it was with me that John needed to finish the job. John followed his advice and returned to me. Then, the dam broke.

John admitted to himself that he really had never given up on his mother. He realized that he had turned from her in anger and bitterness, but he had never really given up his hope. Deeply and keenly, he felt the pain. He wept, as anyone would, about the sorrow of his longing. Then he truely surrendered his unrealistic hopes. Finally, he pulled his hand from the jar.

From that moment, the pattern of John's life began to change. He stopped seeking out cold and unsympathetic women. He stopped seeking out demanding and arbitrary employers. He stopped expecting employers to extend themselves to accommodate his opinions. He stopped expecting women to put up with his extreme attempts to exert his independence by having other women friends. His relationships began to be happier, he was able to be happy at work and keep jobs, and he became more cheerful. His self-esteem improved, his anxiety decreased, and he decreased his consumption of alcohol to reasonable levels. Simultaneously, as he became more confident and his morale improved, he became more able to defend his interests in the battle for his son's custody.

At the same time that he worked on letting go of his hopes regarding his mother, he grappled with his feelings about himself. He faced his feelings of being worthless and somehow flawed. He faced the fact that he had turned his anger with his mother against himself, and created his own depression and anxiety.

He began to realize that he had been self-destructive. He came to see that his self-destructive drinking and relationships were the product of his turning his anger against his mother inward, against himself. He also faced the fact that deep inside, he had nourished a fantasy that his self-destructiveness would please his mother and that she would then love him. He surrendered that fantasy, and began to let go of his anger with his mother.

The more he faced these painful feelings, the freer he became. The freer he became, the happier he became, and the more constructive his life became. All of this he accomplished by going against human nature, and pushing into the Chinese finger trap.
