2003 Books



The following is a list of the books I read in 2003. Some I finished, some I didn't. Was a hell of a year.... They are listed chronologically from top to bottom.


Completed

Chapel Noir, by Carole Nelson Douglas. Really wanted to like it, but... devolved into outright annoying, cliff-hanger, serial crap filled with transparent manipulations and characterizations inconsistent with her earlier books in the series. Too bad.

Mauve: How one man invented a color that changed the world, by Simon Garfield. Intersting pop-history, but a little too "once over, lightly."

Funeral in Blue, by Anne Perry. Another William Monk, very satisfying, more so than several rencent ones in this series as one gets to see Monk work more and does not have to see Oliver Rathbone at all.

Bad News, by Donald Westlake. Funny and satisfying as only Dortmunder can be.

Count Zero, by William Gibson. Good, but not as good as Neuromancer. Pretty decent second novel, though left some threads hanging.

Pipsqueak, by Brain Wiprud. Fun and goofy, but uneven, in my opinion. Still, worth a read.

Gun Monkeys, by Victor Gischler. Evil, wicked, funny, grim in the final third and reminds me a lot of a couple of hard-boiled characters developed by a friend of mine, as well as my own Jack Koenig and others, but very good and totally unexpected. Great modern noir from up-and-coming publisher UglyTown. My personal "best book of 2003," though the last book of 2003 (The Eyre Affair) gave it a serious run for the money, as did Kiln People. Best book of the year for me and best Crime novel, though the best "mystery" would be Dennis Lehane's A Drink Before the War.

Pale Horse Coming, by Stephen Hunter. Pretty good gun-thriller, but long and in places the dialectic speech didn't work for me.

Falling Sideways, by Tom Holt. Cute, funny start, but a little thin and labored, bit of a cheap-ass copy of Adams. Went on too long.

Potato: How the Humble Spud Rescued the Western World, by Larry Zuckerman. More somewhat goofy history. Entertaining and reasonable fact, but perhaps over simplified, here and there.

The Demolished Man, by Alfred Bester. Very good, classic Science Fiction thriller. Reviewed.

Unnatural Fire, by Fidelis Morgan. Lots of fun, though I didn't find it "hilarious" as some did. I found it amusing and well-written, fun and a good mystery yarn, though. I'll willingly read more and I loved the characters, once I got into them. Another wonderful rec.arts.mystery author.

How to Build a Time Machine, by Paul Davies (non-fiction for review). Was unable to finish reading because it was so badly written and so very seriously flawed in its facts and logical arguments. Total crap. Bad science.

The Big Thaw, by Donald Harstad. Not his very best, but highly enjoyable and another satisfying read from Don. Yay, Don!

The Food Taster, by Peter Elbling (fiction for Review at BnB). Sophmoric, prurient, foul and overly clever and self-aware. Very so-so. Reviewed.

Kiln People, by David Brin. Really excellent stuff. Nice noir feel, pretty interesting and mostly well-paced, except for a bit of a slow- and fall-down at the end, but I've often had this complaint with Brin, so it's not too surprising. Generally, I'd recommend it if you like tech-y detective noir and don't mind a less-than-stellar ending. Did not manage to knock Gun Monkeys off the "best of the year" position for me. Slid into third place behind The Eyre Affair, but by the narrowest of margins because Eyre was so out-of-the-blue in concept and maintained a more consistent ending. No damning with faint praise, this is a spectacular book! Best pure-SF I read this year.

The Death of the Necromancer, by Martha Wells. A sort of Steampunk/Industrial Fantasy Crime-Suspense novel about a man seeking revenge for the death of his foster father on a trumped-up charge of Necromancy, who gets sucked into investigation of Crimes Worse than Death. Stylish, spooky, suspenseful and well-paced, I was very pleasantly surprised with what looked and sounded like a fantasy-horror novel, but turned out to be a pretty good thriller. Characters a little familiar, but the overall fit and finish of the book, plot and story was very satisfying. I will definately be looking for more from this author. Reviewed.

Home Sweet Homicide, by Craig Rice. Originally published in 1944. Straight out of the Golden Age of American Mysteries, I really enjoyed this fair-play, easy-going amateur sleuth book. It's strictly "Old School" and not particularly twisty, but it also doesn't aspire to be anything but good, clean fun. Slightly loopy "kids solve the crime" story from the American Grand Mistress of goofball semi-boiled detective fiction. Fun stuff.

Death of a Dream Maker, by Gallagher Gray (Katy Munger). Third of the four Hubbert & Lil books, published in 1995. Alas, the series, which has left me a little cold, did not improve, in spite of the introduction of an early version of Katy's later series-detective Casey Jones. Quite a disappointment.

The Demon Plague, by Joreid McFate. Reads like a High School creative writing paper. Too much stuff and not enough craft. Reviewed.

How to Murder the Man of Your Dreams, by Dorothy Cannell. Another entry in the Ellie Haskell series. Very light and silly. A perfect book for a break from a grim summer.

A Drink Before the War, by Dennis Lehane. Wow. Lehane's debut novel and the first in the Kenzie and Gennaro series. My first experience with Mr. Lehane and it absolutely won't be my last. Extremely impressed with this modern noir, set in the grittiest parts of Boston. Gangs, death and poltics mix with the horrors of relationships and pasts which bend good people into violent shapes. If you haven't, go read it! Not quite knocking off my year's favorite--though it's a very close race--but so good I'll have to go back for more. Thanks to Nan, who lent me the book. I am indebted. Best mystery of the year, for me (though best Crime novel and best novel over all goes to Gun Monkeys.)

Who the Hell is Wanda Fuca? by G. M. Ford. The first book in the Leo Waterman series. I read Cast in Stone a couple of years ago and found it annoying, but had never read this one. Now I have, but I might as well have stopped at one because they both seemed to be the same plot in different dresses, to me. I don't find Ford funny, for some reason, in spite of his reptuation for being "light." Well written, but just not my cuppa, I guess.

The Parcel Express Murders, by Bernadette Y. Connor. Incredibly disappointing. Claims to be suspense but is really poorly-veiled romance and the poorly-plotted, inconsistent kind at that. Was inclined to think well of it--hoped for it, actually--but it fell apart badly in the second half, starting with the explicit sex scene, and came to a conclusion which was certainly no surprise. This book suffers most from a lack of ruthless editing.

Dust to Dust, by Tami Hoag. Snagged my attention in the opening paragraphs, but broke down with a lame red-herring ending. Interesting lead characters I would have liked to see better-treated.

Kisscut, by Karin Slaughter. If you like harrowing forensic/procedural thrillers you must read Karin. While this one is not as sharp as the first and the subject matter is extremely disturbing, she still leaves her competition in the dust by a country mile.

Chocolat, by Joanne Harris. Decently written but without the charm or uplift of the film, which bears only a passing resemblance. I hate to say it, but "I liked the film better." Call me a cretin....

Keep the Baby, Faith, by Philip DeGrave (William L. DeAndrea). Soft- to medium-boiled New York mystery similar to some of Lawrence Block's work. Sly and rather clever, it's another little gem from the late Bill DeAndrea, whom I still admire and miss, even ten years later.

Slow River, by Nicola Griffith. Lambda-winning Science Fiction from a local author. Reminded me a bit of Melissa Scott in tone as well as subject, however, Griffith's pacing is slow and deliberately disjointed. Well-written, but highly manipulative, it came to what was, for me, a rather lackluster, "well, duh!" conclusion. Good writing, interesting character, but the clever style and technical excellence of the craft didn't make up for what ended up being a rather ordinary plot.

Darkness, Take My Hand, by Dennis Lehane. Creepy and very discomforting serial-killer thriller in the Kenzie and Gennaro series. Not as happy with it as with his earlier book, but it's still a long distance in front of most of the competition.

Crash Course, by Wilhelmina Baird. Faintly Cyberpunkish SF about a thief, a whore and an artist who become the "actors" in a psychotic version of reality TV. Clever, but has a tendency to lose focus, and feels like two novellas melded together under the hand of a young writer who's overly-sure of her style and less-skilled with her craft than she thinks. Almost better than merely good, but not quite.

The Cheese Monkeys (A novel in two semesters), by Chip Kidd. Amusing, wicked and slightly surreal. An NYT Notable Book from 2001 and actually worthy of the listing. Writer/designer Kidd creates an interesting and amusing, well-executed package of his tale of late-1950s college romance with Graphic Design and the beautiful and whacko Himillsy Dodd. Pretty damned good, though the ending left me a little put out.

The Death of Riley, by Rhys Bowen. Moderately cosy historical mystery featuring Molly Murphy, an Irish immigrant to New York in the early years of the 20th century. Second in a series of which I didn't read the first, but easy to follow, not particularly challenging, charming, brain-candy. Occassionally innaccurate language usage, but very good historical research for the most part. Was very disappointed, however to discover that the author was female (Rhys is traditionally a male name in Wales) as I had hoped to finally have found a series in the first person female by a male writer which was reasonable in its portrayal of the narrator. Oh, well. I'd be willing to nibble on more of these in the future, so long as the romance doesn't get too sticky or the heroine too lucky, insteady of plucky.

The Eyre Affair, by Jasper Fford. Wonderful, funny, well-constructed and extremely literate book mixing fantasy, literature and mystery with a touch of time-travel, high-humor, low puns and terrific characters. Well-paced and tons of fun if you grew up loving Brit Lit. Marvelous stuff! Second-place for best book of my year, but so fun it's hard to say, fairly, that it's not the best book. Fresh, original and really fun.


Started and stopped

The Complete Guide to Writing Fantasy, edited by Darin Park and Tom Dullemond (Advanced Reader's Copy, not yet for sale). Although very informative and better-written than I had expected, I couldn't finish and had to pass this review book on to another reviewer due to time crunches.

The All True Travels and Adventures of Lidie Newton: a Novel, by Jane Smiley. Well-written, technically, and very well-researched period drama, but slow and dissatisfying when I was in the mood for something uplifting and fun.

Wild Seed, by Octavia Butler. Timing issues, but just not very engaging.

The Pleasure of Finding Things Out, by Richard Feynman. Still difficult and requires thought. Nibbling occasionally.

Writing the Breakout Novel, by Donald Maass. Nibbling....
