An Hour In Sight of William Gibson


Impressions of William Gibson. February 3, 2003, Kane Hall room 130, University of Washington, Seattle, Washington, on the date of the one-day laydown of Pattern Recognition. 7-8 p.m. (Theodore Roethke Room--ironically? Apologies begged for any misquotes as my memory may not be as good as I hope and I took my notes on a bus after the fact.)


"I always knew I would end up in this misnomer genre we call Science Fiction for lack of a better term. I don't write about the future. I write about the present with a sort of future-like spin. So it was, I suppose, inevitable, that I would someday write a book set in the present. And that is what this book is. It is set in the present--the summer of 2002--from a single character point of view, linearly, without all the usual jump-cuts and fast-editing sort of things, without the ellipses and multiple viewpoints. It's very different for me. When I started it, I thought 'I want to stretch' and not long into it I thought 'What am I doing?' It took me almost a year to write just that little bit I've read to you, here. And it was like a first novel, with all the fear and confusion. I thank my first readers for keeping me with it, having perspective. It was a very thrilling and frightening process."

William Gibson's head seems extraordinarily large from this angle. But, that might be as much the impression of the energetic thought processes going on in it as the effect of forced perspective. He seems to be entirely a thing of his own imagination whose body is just conveniently there to carry that mind around and input sensation to it. From here in the upper Mezz, I can see the incipient bald-spot and the dramatically receding hairline which makes his head and face seem a massive, black-plumed edifice, carved from some animate putty and stuck on top of a stick-figural armature covered in a silver-gray shirt and dark trousers which, though properly sized, hang as if cinched on with a certain amount of hope that their wearer will not suddenly do something startling and athletic, which would surely cause them to fall gracelessly flapping. His speaking voice is not what one would expect from the father of Cyberpunk. Neither resonant and bright as the cut-glass notes of electric guitars, nor deep, ringing with dark portents. It is slightly bland, a little congested, as if he has recently had a cold, and seems to come out of the front of his skull as much as his mouth, slowed and sweetened a bit, perhaps, with habit of a Southern childhood. He remains comfortably contained behind the podium, does not deign to bend toward the microphones sprouting there like a blackened weed. His voice will come to it. It, perhaps, may come to him.

He is "decompressing," by his opening admission, after writing; coming down, or possibly up, from the exhausting process of creating and shepherding ideas into accessible forms. He reads the first scene of Pattern Recognition, newly-released from its publishing purgatorial trip to market today. He halts and stumbles occasionally over his own words, foreigners, by now. "I always have two experiences of a book," he says. "Writing it and then reading it again, at an event like this, where it is as new to me as to you. Once I am done with a book, I never have anything to do with it again. I never go back and read my own work." He does not read with great drama; the scene doesn't require it and his own speaking style is not oriented to the grandiose. I suspect that Gibson will end up reading the entire book aloud, a bit at at time, during the present tour, experiencing it anew and in a way foreign to his readers, who will most likely never find themselves in the position of experiencing such discovery of the coalescence of ideas into articulated form in front of an audience. It's hardly like reading a book in the quiet of your own living room, or even in the artificial social distance of a bus, silencing the rage of traffic, chatter, weather and various stinks in the isolation of being head-down over a page in public.

Gibson reminds me of a math teacher I had in High School. He is compactly there, in spite of his gangling size; unmissable, but unpretentiously present, like a dynamo humming quietly in its case. He is there for the benefit of this thing which has come out of his head, a thought-child which requires a small amount of fatherly support to start off on its toddling way, patted on the back of its hundreds of present proxy-legs in the form of bright-eyed fans.

Having read, he waits for questions, his face eagerly alight for their curiosity.

"Tell us about your style... How do you write? What inspires you?"

"What inspires me? Everything," he replies, smiling, shrugging a little, holding onto air and letting his metaphor expand like a bubble floating away. He is a wanderer of the world where even the mundane rouses a question.

"Do you know what you will write, from the beginning, or does your writing take off on its own?"

Gibson refers to E.M. Forster, whom he had come across early in his career and with whom he is still most happily struck. "If your characters do only as you would have them do, if you always have control of them, you'll never write anything new." He allows them to build their story as they go along together; creator and creation.

"If you write to present some position, to answer a question," Gibson continues, "you don't write your own stuff, you're writing someone else's. I don't write to find or give answers. I write for the questions. If anyone, some reader, asks me a question about the book, finds a question there, then I'm happy."

"What question engages you right now?"

"What was the question?" calls another voice from the audience below.

Laughter from the speaker as well as the audience. "What question concerns me right now...?" His pause is so long, the audience seems to think he cannot--or worse, won't--answer and twitches nervously in its collective seat, sensing clay feet perhaps, which their idol has never taken any pains to hide. If they see him as something more than human, that is their own fault.

"The question which engages me right now is why do we answer questions? Why we feel compelled to answer questions put to us, to say something, anything, to appear to have an answer at all, as I am doing right now," he replies, smiling, lifting his hands and beginning to chuckle, "rather than to have the option of saying 'I don't know-slash-no comment'."

There is a moment of giggling silence and Gibson, as if goosed by some invisible finger, says "But I suppose I've wasted enough time talking and should get to signing books!"

He scampers to the table and the line which coalesces out of the seated crowd into the aisle is easily 30-50 people long, instantly. An hour has passed in what seemed more like twenty minutes.

My husband wants to leave. He's bored and unimpressed. I'm too broke to buy a book and my tatty paperback copy of Count Zero seems an insult (I wish I could have found my copy of Neuromancer so casually lent and now gone), so we slink out, me somewhat dissatisfied, but charmed with Gibson's presence and the assurance that I'm not a total weirdo for thinking the things I do or writing them. Jim, I suspect, only wants to go home and eat.

Gibson had suggested, as he spoke, that views of his books espoused in public more likely represent the projections of the speaker than any real intent of his own. This could be both right and wrong. From his stated position of writing without a position, he creates a mirror for what we, ourselves, think. His position is, by default, that of Mirror, not inflective, but reflective. A little more important, to my thinking at that moment, than dinner.

On first approaching the room, we had met a young man also coming in, who asked me what this was about.

"It's William Gibson," I replied.

"Yes, but what is the talk about, this cyberpunk thing?"

"Not the genre. Gibson pretty much invented Cyberpunk and he is speaking about and signing his new book, tonight. That's all."

The young man had seemed a bit confused by that, apparently urged to this event on the supposition that it would be significant in some academically-advancing fashion. Which it would be, if one is prepared to start asking questions of oneself, but, perhaps, Gibson's concept is not as popular as his books.
