Recommended Fiction

  • The Lord of the Rings trilogy and The Silmarillion by J.R.R. Tolkien are classics of our time. Tolkien is a master of the epic and tragic sweep of mythic histories.

  • For most of my reading life, I've been in love with Isaac Asimov's forthright prose, imagination, and light humor. I suggest pretty much anything that he wrote, but especially the Foundation series and one of his better collections of short stories, such as Robot Dreams or Nine Tomorrows.

  • Frank Herbert excels at painting intricate portraits of future societies and alternate ecologies (and their interplay), while exploring our own cultural mythos. I highly recommend his classic, Dune and even more so The Jesus Incident and The Lazarus Effect.

  • In elementary school I was enthralled by Madeleine L'Engle's simple but beautifully woven science fiction Time series, with A Wrinkle in Time, A Wind in the Door, A Swiftly Tilting Planet, and Many Waters. Don't pigeonhole them as "children's books;" they're still great reading.

  • Since seventh grade I've had a love affair with dystopian literature, for its ability to combine tragic, personal plots, intriguing socio-political theory, and contemporary satire. Some of my favorites include Margaret Atwoods haunting, poignant, and very relevant The Handmaid's Tale; George Orwell's bitterly satiric Animal Farm and horrifying 1984; Aldous Huxley's superbly ambiguous Brave New World; and the beautiful imagery and tragedy of Ignazio Silone's Bread and Wine.

  • Rosencrantz and Guildenstern are Dead by Tom Stoppard is a disturbing but hilarious postmodern play from the point of view of two minor cogs in the machinery of Shakespeare's Hamlet.

Recommended Nonfiction

  • Thanks largely to my coursework on Eighteenth Century Philosophy, I've really come to love the period as one of the greatest flowerings of original thought on the mind and on the bounds of that whereof we can sensibly speak. The enthusiasm of these thinkers for careful inquiry is infectious, refreshing, and mostly lacking in today's dried-up world of clique-ish, resigned, nitpicking Analytics, and pessimistic, language-game-playing Continentals. I particularly recommend Bishop Berkeley's Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge, David Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature, and Immanuel Kant's Critique of Pure Reason.

  • Ayn Rand: The Russian Radical by Chris Sciabarra and Feminist Interpretations of Ayn Rand, ed. Chris Sciabarra and Mimi Gladstein are two important works in the growing incorporation of Ayn Rand's thought into the channels of academic philosophy, and offer quite valuable interpretations and assessments of her thought and fiction.

  • Utilitarianism by John Stuart Mill and The Nicomachean Ethics by Aristotle are what I consider to be two of the most profound works on ethics published (as I tend to go with some mixture of virtue ethics and classical utilitarianism in my ethical thought).

  • Beyond Good and Evil by Friedrich Nietzsche, on the other hand, is one of the most profound and important critiques of ethics ever published, and contains innumerable insights on social theory, psychology, literary theory, ethics, metaphysics, and epistemology, among others - despite frequently being wrong (such as in his attacks on Kantian epistemology) and having some utterly revolting passages (such as the remarks on women). Definitely a must-read, especially for those who have a decent familiarity with 19th century Continental thought.

  • Writing Down the Bones by Natalie Goldberg is a wonderful, wonderful book about creative writing - but also about creativity and art in general. It's beautifully written, and filled to the brim with practical suggestions for better writing - especially for improving the way that you actually go about writing (rather than the usual bullet list of commandments about what you write).

  • Guns, Germs, and Steel: The Fates of Human Societies by Jared Diamond is a great anti-racist analysis of the broadest patterns of world history, from the perspective of an evolutionary biologist. His case is powerful and provocative, if sometimes over-broad, and is tied together through persuasive, skillful prose.

  • About Face: The Essentials of User Interface Design by Alan Cooper will change the way you think about interface design. Some of Cooper's concrete suggestions are weak, but his radical questioning of basic interface idioms and practices are more than worth the price of the book.

  • The History of Hell by Alice K. Turner and A History of God by Karen Armstrong

  • Pooh and the Philosophers by John Tyerman Williams

  • The Complete T. Rex by Dr. John R. Horner and Don Lessem and Hunting Dinosaurs by Louie Psihoyos with John Knoebber

Books to Avoid

A Separate Peace by John Knowles

Very possibly the worst book I've ever read. The writing is horrid--if I cut out all of the pointless crap in the book, it would be a novella. Out of all the characters, Phineas is the only one for whom I care whether he lives or drowns in the river. And even him I don't like much.

Great Expectations by Charles Dickens

I actually hesitate to tell people to avoid this book. I thought that the beginning and the end of the work actually formed the core of a good story. Most of the characters are fairly interesting, and I can even sympathize with most of them. But the book loses its appeal when it becomes bogged down by Dickens' overblown prose, and in the middle bloats into a long and boring account of daily life with Herbert instead of an interesting narrative.

The Tragedy of Romeo and Juliet by William Shakespeare

It's probably blasphemy to hate this, but I simply cannot stand the play at all. For such a highly praised figure in English literature, Shakespeare can't put together a logical plot to save his life in this piece of refuse; the entire play is motivated by gross stupidity and random accidents. The view of love presented (as an inexplicable, fated occurrence between strangers) is irresponsible hogwash and the only redeeming event in the entire play is when the leads end the stupid play with their own stupid deaths.

The Fountainhead by Ayn Rand

Thought you hated the characters in A Separate Peace? Prepare to be assaulted by a set of characters so totally unsympathetic, so unlikable, and so flat that they could only appear in an Ayn Rand ideological allegory. While the forces of darkness are obviously unlikable and Bad, the supposed hero of the work is an anti-social egotheistic asshole whose love life is closer to repeated rape than a relationship. Ayn Rand should have just followed John Bunyan's example (from The Pilgrim's Progress and The Life and Death of Mr. Badman) and named Howard Roark "Objectivist Superhero" and Ellsworth Toohey (who is probably the most interesting character in the book) "Wicked Meanguy." If I'm missing any important perspectives on the plot or characters, please forgive me; I only waded through a few chapters before I felt compelled to throw the book across the room and not pick it up again.
