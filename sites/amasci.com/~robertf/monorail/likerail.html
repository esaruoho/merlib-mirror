 		My conception of a monorail system is that it serves the purpose of subways and light rail systems in other cities. Those systems are generally designed to carry a large number of people, with trains of fairly high capacity running at frequent intervals.
 
FREQUENTLY RUNNING TRAINS: I think that the monorail trains should also run at frequent intervals, and indeed the Seattle Monorail Project plans to run trains at intervals (headways) of about four minutes during rush hours and about eight minutes at other times. I feel this is an excellent plan.
 
ROOMY, HIGH CAPACITY TRAINS: I think that the monorail trains should be of similar size to light rail trains, with similar width and headroom. The trains in use for the existing Seattle Center Monorail are spacious and comfortable. I think the trains for the new monorail should be similar.
 
SUFFICIENT HEADROOM: I think that the trains should have a high headroom, that is to say the height on the inside of the cars, so that there is enough height for the majority of people to walk around without bumping their heads on the ceiling. Trains designed for Japan or amusement parks may have insufficient headroom.
 
WALK-THROUGH-TRAINS: It should be possible for passengers to walk from one car into the next. This permits passengers entering one car to move into less crowded cars. Walk-through-trains also mean that trains can be longer than station loading areas, and passengers can move into cars that are beyond the loading area. In case of an emergency, people can move into cars further from the threatening situation.
 
ADEQUATE SEATING: The monorail is intended to be a comfortable and pleasant way to ride. Standing up during the ride is neither comfortable nor pleasant, at least for most people. I feel strongly that there should be sufficient seating that everybody can sit down except during rush hours, special events, or unusual conditions. I would personally prefer a seating arrangement similar to that on most buses, with most seats lateral to a central aisle and facing forward. However, monorail trains will run in both directions, reversing direction at each end of the line. Therefore any seat facing forward in one direction will face backward on the return trip. So in the case of the train it would make more sense to have pairs of seats, back to back.
 
Of course there should be some accomodations for wheelchairs.
 
WE SHOULD PLAN NOW FOR THE FUTURE: I view the Green Line not only as the first line of five planned for Seattle, but also the beginning of a network of monorails extending out from Seattle to nearby cities. These lines should all use the same standards so that trains can be used on any line. With this in mind, the Green Line will be establishing a standard for the region, so the specifications should be chosen very carefully to allow for future expansion. The following points need to be considered:
  1. Seating and comfort will become more important on longer trips.
  2. Higher speed may be more important on longer routes, so trains purchased now should allow for future retrofitting of higher-speed motors and drive systems.
  3. Routes built now within the City should be designed for high-speed service, because if a route is later extended far beyond the city limits, an overly circuitous route within the city would be a hindrance to high-speed service from the suburbs. (Note my objections to the Fifth Avenue routing!)
  4. Trains purchased now should be roomy because ridership in the future will probably be much higher, and as routes are extended beyond the city limits, ridership will increase even more.
  5. Trains should allow for modification to add cars in case future demands warrant it.
  6. If a train is extended in length by adding cars, it becomes very important that passengers can walk from car to car to reach cars that are beyond the station loading platform. With this in mind, I think it is imperative that we buy trains that permit movement between cars.
  7. As routes are added, some of the routes may converge onto one guideway. This is almost certain in Downtown Seattle. There are limits of minimum headway (time between trains). In order to avoid congestion of trains, the trains need to have a high capacity so they don't have to run as often to accommodate high passenger loads.
I AM AFRAID THE NEW TRAINS WILL BE TOO SMALL: I now have concerns that the new trains will be smaller and have insufficient capacity. The two companies currently competing for the new project are Hitachi and Bombardier. Both companies are experienced in monorail manufacture, but the largest Hitachi trains are of a somewhat smaller size than the Seattle Center trains, and the Bombardier trains are even smaller.
 
WILL THE NEW TRAINS BE WALK-THROUGH? I also have concerns about the Bombardier trains — they are not of the walk-through design. Instead of the drive mechanism being mounted below the floor, Bombardier’s design has the drive mechanism above the floor-line at the ends of the cars. This design lowers the floor in the passenger area and makes the entire car lower and sleeker, but blocks off the passenger area at each end. I consider a walk-through feature to be much more important than the exterior height of the car, so I personally find the Bombardier design unacceptable.
 
HOW ABOUT HEADROOM? Hitachi is a Japanese company and most of their monorail systems are in Japan and other Asian countries, where the average height of the riders is less than in Seattle. I think we need to be sure that cars have sufficient headroom for Seattle riders.
 
Most Bombardier monorails are in amusement parks, such as Disneyland, and in short systems such as at airports. I think we need to check out their headroom also.
 
SO WHAT DO I THINK WE SHOULD DO? I think that the SPMA (Seattle Popular Monorail Authority) should consider the specifications I have explained here, and that there should be public hearings to get public opinion on these specifications, and then to prepare a set of specifications that the manufacturers must meet. I think that between Hitachi and Bombardier, Hitachi comes closest to my concept of what the new trains should be like. I think Hitachi should be able to modify their design sufficiently to provide larger and roomier trains.
 
The existing Seattle Center Monorail, built by Alweg of Germany, from my point of view, has the best design. I think that the Alweg design should be used with some changes to incorporate new technology. It is my understanding (uncomfirmed!) that the Alweg patents have expired, which would mean that anybody can use them. I would like to see Hitachi or another company design the new system to the same basic specification as the existing Seattle Center Monorail, with the same width of guideway and similar train size.
 
Newer monorail designs use narrower beams in the guideways. This makes them more attractive and they block less light, but the narrower beams also mean that the stabilizing wheels running along the side of the guideway are closer together. Wheels closer together (laterally) provide less stability, so there is more of a tendency for the train to rock from side to side. The Alweg train is quite steady and comfortable because the side wheels are spaced further apart. A rider on the Las Vegas monorail (built by Bombardier) reported that it rocked more and was less comfortable than the Seattle train.
 
MY SPECIFICATIONS: If I were in charge of designing the new monorail (of course I am very definitely NOT in charge of anything!), these are the specification I would present to potential builders:
 
  1. GUIDEWAY: Same width, and same critical dimensions, as Alweg Seattle Center Monorail, so that the two are interchangeable. Non-critical dimensions can vary to provide a more pleasing appearance.
  2. TRAIN DRIVE SYSTEM: Wheel locations and spacing must be compatible with Seattle Center Monorail train so they can operate on the same guideway.
  3. TRAIN LENGTH: Similar to Seattle Center Monorail trains.
  4. PROVISION FOR ADDING CARS: Allow for possible addition of cars to trains if future conditions warrant it. Addition of cars should be possible without extensive modification, although I don't expect it to be as simple as the coupling system of railway cars.
  5. INTERIOR HEADROOM OF TRAIN CARS: Sufficient for American population.
  6. TRAIN WIDTH: About ten feet.
  7. TRAIN SPEED: Should be able to reach a maximum speed of about 50 miles per hour at moderate acceleration. A top speed of 60mph would be better, especially when the line is extended.
  8. PROVISION FOR HIGHER SPEEDS: The drive systems should be so designed, that if future conditions demand higher-speed trains, the drive motors and systems can be retrofitted for higher speeds without major modifications.
  9. WALK-THROUGH-TRAINS: The trains should be designed so that passengers can readily walk from one car to another for the length of the train.
  10. SEATING: Seating should be comfortable and there should be sufficient seating to accomodate all passengers except during rush hours or unusual conditions. Most of the seats should be arranged like on a bus, laterally installed with one end against the sidewall and with a central aisle. However, unlike a bus, the trains are bidirectional (they reverse direction when they get to the end of the line), so seats can be arranged in pairs, back to back. This also gives a group of friends or a family to sit together facing each other. Bench seats with their backs against the sidewall and facing the central aisle may be necessary in some loctions, but they deny passengers a good view out the window.
  11. AVAILABILITY OF REPLACEMENT PARTS: The Alweg train was built using parts in use in Subway trains and other easily obtained parts. The new trains should also use parts easily obtained from multiple sources, rather than special parts only obtainable from the train manufacturer.
