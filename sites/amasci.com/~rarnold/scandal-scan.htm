SCANDAL-SCAN: PROBLEM SECTORS

Curing corporate problems before they become corporate disasters
A pro-free enterprise watchdog trying to keep the system honest rather than trying to destroy it

The following sectors of the United States economy have been examined by Scandal-Watch and judged to have potential problems that, if unchecked, could develop into corporate scandals.

Greenmail

  • The question: Do firms give coerced payoffs to non-profit attack groups that accuse the firm of "social irresponsibility" or "wrecking the Third World" or "environmental damage" and threaten shareholder devaluation through bad publicity campaigns? Are non-profit groups subject to Federal Trade Commission rules on anti-competitive acts and collusion with for-profit firms to unfairly manipulate markets and damage competing firms?

The Nature Conservancy

  • The question: Do executives of for-profit firms take positions on the board of directors of the non-profit Nature Conservancy to enrich themselves with access to land deals and other exclusive perquisites? Is The Nature Conservancy subject to oversight by Congress?

Waste Management

  • The question: Are waste management firms entering into "sweetheart deals" with the Environmental Protection Agency on human sludge applications to farmlands, and are environmental groups receiving payoffs or favors to look the other way?

Hostile Takeovers

  • The question: Is the highly unusual hostile takeover bid of Oracle Corporation to acquire PeopleSoft just a ploy to batter a competitor, or is it for real, and an overt attempt to monopolize the software industry in the large enterprise and government sector?

Newspapers

  • The question: Are newspaper companies that participate in Joint Operating Agreements, as authorized by Congress in the Newspaper Preservation Act of 1970 to legally skirt antitrust rules, violating the intent of the law by trying to shut down some of their newspapers not because they are "failing," but because they are not making enough money to suit the parent company's profit desires?

  • Another question: Are "dominant" newspapers in Joint Operating Agreements (JOAs) using the Newspaper Preservation Act as a cover to build empires by buying out others newspapers, then when large enough, trying to kill the competition by pulling out of JOAs?

Insurance

  • The question: Are insurance/reinsurance brokerage combines coercing their clients (insurance companies) to kick back future business, subjecting clients to higher premium rates (and/or inadequate coverage) than could be obtained by competitive bidding among brokers?

Gambling

  • The question: Although casinos have avoided scandals by tough regulations that prevent "cooking the books" and executive sellouts of shareholders, is Internet gambling a virtual invitation to money laundering because operators can be based offshore beyond U.S. regulation and will massive consumer debt problems result from credit card use to pay Internet gambling debts?

Bank Credit Cards

  • The question: Are banks "slamming" customer accounts shut when they receive large lump payments the bank believes to result from transferring balances to a competing bank, even when the large payment came from customer windfalls, and did not benefit any competing bank? Is this anti-competitive restraint of trade?
