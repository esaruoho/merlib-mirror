Supreme Court to Decide Nike Free Speech Case

January 10, 2003

The U.S. Supreme Court said it will hear the case of sneaker company Nike Inc. to decide whether it can be sued for false advertising for defending itself against allegations of "sweatshop" conditions at overseas factories.

The justices today agreed to hear Nike's appeal of a California Supreme Court ruling that Nike's statements about its labor practices were "commercial speech" designed to sell products and thus did not deserve full First Amendment protection under the U.S. Constitution.

The crucial free-speech case stemmed from a 1998 civil lawsuit filed in California by Marc Kasky, former executive director and current trustee of the San Francisco-based Fort Mason Foundation, claiming that Nike willfully misled the public about working conditions for Vietnamese, Chinese and Indonesian employees.

Kasky, an anti-capitalist activist who lived in a posh home in Golden Gate National Park when he filed the suit, claimed Nike knew workers were physically punished and sexually abused, had to work in dangerous conditions and did not earn a "living wage," even with 14 hour workdays.

Kasky's supposed "concern for the downtrodden" took a beating when The Bay Guardian reported that he wouldn't support the use of 466 existing housing units in the National Park where he lives for homeless people. It was okay for him and other foundation bigwigs to live there, but not the homeless.

The hypocritical Kasky claimed the Beaverton, Oregon-based company mounted a false advertising and public relations campaign to boost sales of its products.

Nike denied Kasky's claims, and has argued the case should be dismissed because all the statements cited in the lawsuit were protected as free speech. It said its statements were part of an international media debate on issues of public interest.

Nike appealed to the Supreme Court. It said the state court ruling applied to statements that appeared in editorials and on the editorial pages, and in comments made to reporters, not just to paid commercial ads.

Harvard University law professor Laurence Tribe, one of the lawyers representing Nike in the appeal, said he was pleased the high court agreed to review the case.

Walter Dellinger, also representing Nike and a former acting solicitor general, said, "No company should feel impeded from engaging in the marketplace of ideas just because they operate in the marketplace of goods."

Alan Caplan, a lawyer for Kasky, expressed confidence the Supreme Court will affirm the California decision and allow the case to go to trial.

"The lawsuit specifically describes numerous factual misrepresentations Nike made to the public about the labor practices in the factories that manufacture its shoes," Caplan said.

Caplan has probably not thought through the consequences should his client win: non-profit groups would fall under the same standards of truthfulness any time they sent out fund-raising appeals, because that too is "commercial speech" designed to sell products (memberships and services).

The Chamber of Commerce supported Nike's appeal, saying the ruling suppressed speech by corporations.

A number of companies expressed concern that "a corporation's every press release, letter to an editor, customer mailing, op-ed article and Web site posting" may be the basis for a lawsuit under California law.

News media organizations were alarmed. They warned the decision posed a serious threat to their ability to report on important issues regarding corporate America.

Kasky's lawyers said the appeal should be rejected. "There is no constitutional right to dupe consumers; this has been the law for decades," Patrick Coughlin, one of the lawyers, said. Again, Kasky and his lawyers have probably not thought about the consequences of advocacy groups duping consumers.

The Supreme Court will hear arguments in the case in the spring, with a decision due by the end of June.
