This rebuttal was provided to the Center for the Defense of Free Enterprise by Mr. Scott C. Wallace, CEO of Gardenburger, Inc after Mr. Scott initiated contact with the Center by telephone on Wednesday, September 11, at 1:45 p.m. We post Mr. Scott's rebuttal in full, unedited and without comment.

 

Gardenburger rebuttal to: The Other Fake Meat by Steven Milloy.

 

Mr. Milloys conclusion that  Quorn is deceptively labeled makes a mountain out of a molehill and his comparison Quorn is derived from a fungus and mushrooms are fungi oversimplify the issue.  Based on this logic, virtually any fungus or mold such as that growing on a piece of cheese or in any damp location could be processed into mycoprotein and sold as mushroom protein.

 

Gardenburgers only objective relative to Quorn and its claim that its ingredient mycoprotein is mushroom based or mushroom in origin, is to correct what we see as a deceptive and inaccurate labeling claim.  Our motivation for asking FDA to intercede is due to our concern that Quorns deceptive labeling claim could damage Gardenburgers business.  Mushroom purchases represent a significant portion of Gardenburgers annual ingredient cost.  We believe that if the facts regarding Quorn's mycoprotein were to come out at some future date, consumers could develop a distrust of all products that contain real mushrooms.  The American Mushroom Institute, a national non-profit trade association representing the domestic US cultivated mushroom industry, and its counterpart in the United Kingdom, the British Mushroom Bureau have also made complaints about Quorns label.  The Trading Standards Office and Advertising Standards Agency in the United Kingdom have taken action to prevent Quorn from using its mushroom description and we believe Quorn is working with the FDA on this issue as well.

 

Mr. Milloys assertion that there is any relationship between Gardenburger and CSPI is untrue and groundless.  He cites as proof a four-year-old CSPI newsletter that highlighted a Gardenburger product and a 2002 CSPI meatless products review that included favorable comments about Gardenburger products.  He neglected to point out in his article that Gardenburgers competitors Morningstar Farms, Boca, Worthington and even Quorn also received favorable reviews from CSPI in the 2002 review.  By pasting this address into a browser (http://www.cspinet.org/nah/03_02/), the 2002 meatless products review Mr. Milloy referenced can be seen first-hand.  Also, Mr. Milloy makes no mention of the US and UK mushroom trade associations, The Fusarium Research Center of Pennsylvania State University and Giorgio Foods, a major US mushroom grower, which also filed complaints about Quorns mushroom references.  In regards to pestering the FDA, Gardenburgers only communication with FDA was two letters on this subject.

 

Mr. Milloy characterizes Quorn as smaller than CSPIs usual targets. Quorn is sold by Marlow Foods, a subsidiary of $17 billion UK drug giant Astra Zeneca.  Further, there was nothing sinister about the e-mail Mr. Milloy described other than how he obtained a copy of a confidential internal Gardenburger e-mail.

 

Mr. Milloy chose to cite Gardenburgers Fiscal 2001 results in an effort to further disparage our company.  Gardenburgers results through the 3rd quarter of the Fiscal 2002 year are available to the public but werent used because they dont support the story he wanted to write.  In fact, Gardenburger sales are up 4% versus last year and operating profits are up by $4 million.

           

Quorn has stated that it uses the mushroom reference to make it easier for consumers to understand its ingredient mycoprotein.   We have a different theory as to Quorns motivation for using its mushroom description.  Over the last 17 years, Gardenburger has invested over $100 million to develop the domestic U.S. market for veggie burgers containing mushrooms.  In our opinion, Quorn is simply trying to trade on the premium image of veggie burgers containing mushrooms developed by Gardenburger to make more profits.  When introducing yogurt to the U.S. market, manufacturers faced a similar challenge to Quorn in that yogurt contains bacteria.  However, rather than attempt to trade off the image of an existing food, yogurt manufacturers invested the money necessary to educate consumers about the benefits of eating yogurt.  Quorn could take a similar approach and invest the dollars needed to educate consumers about mycoprotein.  Of course, educating consumers about mycoprotein would mean making less profit as compared to the approach of trading off existing products. 

 

At Gardenburger we understand that it is sometimes difficult to take a stand on controversial issues.  Nonetheless, we stand by our decision to challenge Quorn on its labeling claims and believe that the attacks made on our business are a small price to pay for providing consumers with accurate and clear information on which to base their meatless products purchase decisions.
