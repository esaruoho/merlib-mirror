International Petroleum Exchange's uninvited guests escorted to the pavement

London, February 16 In an effort to tell the world that Kyoto is not enough, 35 Greenpeace protesters unlawfully entered the International Petroleum Exchange (IPE) to raise a ruckus and stop trading for several hours. Instead, they got a vigorous explanation of why disrupting a room full of floor traders is not nice and a personal escort to the pavement outside.

The brawl that landed them back in the street wasn't exactly as Greenpeace planned. That night Greenpeace said two of its intruders were in hospital, one with a suspected broken jaw, the other with concussion.

The Greenpeace plan looked great on paper: Shortly before 2 pm, a trader left the IPE building by using a security swipe card to open the door. Greenpeace was waiting for such an exit. Activist Mark Strutt said security guards were distracted by two protestors dressed in suits to look like traders, who dropped some coins on the floor.  As one of them bent down to pick them up, he put his boot in the door to keep it open. He used his cell phone to alert fellow trespassers.

Two minutes later, three Greenpeace vans pulled up and another 30 gatecrashers leapt out and were let in by the shills.

The invaders made their way to the trading floor, blowing whistles and sounding fog horns, encountering little resistance from security guards. Rape alarms were tied to helium balloons to float to the ceiling and create noise out of reach. The IPE conducts open outcry trading where deals are shouted across the pit. By making so much noise, the intruders hoped to paralyze trading. Mr Strutt added that the campaigners had brought earplugs to give to traders who were upset by the noise.

Two climbers scaled the front of the building and unfurled a banner saying Climate change kills, stop pushing oil.

Greenpeacies conceded that a disruptive operation where such huge amounts of money trade hands every minute may not have been the best plan. The violence was instant, Jon Beresford, 39, an electrical engineer from Nottingham, said.

They were set upon by traders, most of whom were under the age of 25. They grabbed us and started kicking and punching. Then when we were on the floor they tried to push huge filing cabinets on top of us to crush us.

Greenpeaces executive director Steven Tindale said: They kind of pinned us into the corner, there were a couple of dozen around us.

We were non-violent and peaceful and we made it clear thats what we were there for but there were quite a few blows raining down on our heads.

They pulled a metal bookcase down on our heads. They were trying to use that to push us back out so that was the moment we decided to retreat for everyones safety. The Greenpeacies were on the trading floor for only about 10 minutes, but some those not escorted beyond the egress remained outside the floor in the IPE building for another 3 hours.

Tindale said he was shocked by the reaction of the traders: They werent interested in our message, they just laid into us.

They were just swearing at us, it wasnt very subtle. Everyone who goes on a Greenpeace action is trained in non-violent direct action, so we know not to respond, although a few swear words may have come from ourselves.

Another intruder described the traders as incredibly violent and added: The Greenpeace people here have rarely seen people react like this. They have been very physical.

Mr. Beresford said: They followed the guys into the lobby and kept kicking and punching them there. They literally kicked them on to the pavement.

We bit off more than we could chew. They were just Cockney barrow boy spivs. Total thugs, one protester said, rubbing his bruised skull. Ive never seen anyone less amenable to listening to our point of view.

Hmm... "Amenable to listening to our point of view. An interesting concept from an intruder. Let's see if Mr. Beresford or Mr. Tindale is amenable to listening to our point of view. We'll try a more gentle way than intruding on Greenpeace workspace, just writing, okay?

Mr. Beresford, do you understand the floor traders' point of view? They weren't in your workspace, you were in theirs without authorization. And on the trading floor, that's an offense you can go to jail for.  Do you grasp that?

Mr. Tindale, you were the thugs trespassers, interlopers, intruders, unlawful entrymen, whatever a lawyer might call it if you're taken to court not the traders who had a lawful right to be there. And they listened very carefully to the noise you brought into their workspace. Then they offered their point of view about your disruption.

Mr. Beresford, do you understand any of this? Do you get it at all? The traders make a living there, just as you do as an electrical engineer in Nottingham. Get it? You battered their ears with violent noise and stopped them from earning a living. Get it? That's violent. You stole their time, their most precious commodity, by hitting them with a violent noise. Get it? You took bread out of their mouths with violent noise. Get it? That's violence, no matter what your self-righteous, logic-twisting cause calls it. Get it?

Mr. Beresford? Mr. Tindale? You're not listening. You can't hurt peoples' lives and livelihoods and not call it violence. That's the worst kind of moral dishonesty. The traders explained to you that it was not a nice thing to do. Get it? They didn't come to hurt you. You came to hurt them. Get it? Probably not.

Another Greenpeace intruder said: I took on a Texan Swat team at Esso last year and they were angels compared with this lot. Behind him, on the balcony of the pub opposite the IPE, an angry-eyed trader, pint in hand, yelled: Sod off, Swampy.

By making so much noise, the protesters hoped to sabotage all trading between 2pm and 7.30pm, but a spokeswoman for IPE said all trading had resumed by 3.15pm. A spokeswoman from IPE said, The floor was invaded by a small group of protesters. Open outcry trading was suspended but electronic trading carried on.

Eighteen police vans and six police cars surrounded the exchange and at least 27 protesters were arrested. Ten were arrested for various public order offences and taken to local police stations, a Scotland Yard spokesman said. A small band blocked the entrance to the building for the rest of the evening.

Richard Ward, IPEs chief executive, would not discuss whether he would press charges against the intruders, and said he would not know until the next morning if there had been any financial loss.

Greenpeace later started a second protest at the annual dinner of the Institute of Petroleum at the Grosvenor House Hotel on Park Lane, in Central London. Greenpeace claimed that five campaigners had got into the Great Hall. About 30 protesters were outside the hotel, some blocking the front entrance by sitting down and locking themselves together, while others sounded klaxons and alarms. Climbers scaled scaffolding to unfurl a banner reading, Climate change kills, oil industry parties.

Lee Raymond, head of US oil giant Exxon-Mobil, bete-noire of environmentalists and donor to the Center, has warned European governments they may need to take a 'reality check' in their efforts to prevent global warming.

Raymond said that attaining targets laid down by the Kyoto protocol was "going to be very challenging given the energy supply and demand realities".

The American, guest of honor at the Institute of Petroleum's annual dinner, added that oil would play a pivotal role in meeting the world's energy needs for years to come.

By 2030, he said, a population boom and increasing economic prosperity meant global energy needs were likely to be 100m barrels a day higher than today - ten times the current output of Saudi Arabia.

He said: "Even with an expected rapid growth rate for wind and solar energy - driven in large measure by public subsidies, I might add - their contribution to global energy will still be in the 1% range in 2030."

The Center adds that when you think of energy, think of flying a jetliner with it. Think of wind and solar-powered jetliners, for example. Do you get it?

Greenpeace climate campaigner Emily Armistead said it was disgraceful that on the day the Kyoto agreement had been struck, the oil industry was toasting "the number one climate criminal".

Two protesters managed to interrupt Raymond's speech before being forced out by security guards.

Sod off, Swampy echoes in the distance.
