Ian McQueen <  > wrote:

I'm getting caught up on my SMALLnet postings and just found this question from Andy Woitowicz: "Has anyone had experience with tuned pipe diesels?"

I haven't tried one and doubt that I will. A tuned pipe works by using the resonant frequency of the pipe to draw the air-fuel mixture into the engine and then cram it back in as the ports close. Since the ignition timing of a diesel depends on the compression ratio and filling of the cylinder, using a tuned pipe would result in an engine that would run well only at one speed. Below that speed, the filling of the cylinder would be diminished, the timing would be retarded, and power would fall off. It might not be possible to get the speed back up to resonance.

Or so I have read.

A "magic" muffler, which is intended to provide a resonant boost over a range of speeds, might work better. The MVVS 2.5 (.15) diesel is available with a "magic" muffler. Ian

...Allan, I have a copy of the original Yogi plan, and well remember that offbeat design. (A free flight "pusher" with the propeller just behind the wing; a tall, narrow "cabin"; and a twin-finned tail. Original power was a Bantam .19 sparker, but I remember seeing one Yogi flying with an Ohlsson .23)

The plan shows a recommended balance point 4 1/8" behind the leading edge of the wing. (The wing's nominal chord is 8", but at the center it's considerably narrower to make room for the engine and for prop clearance.)

The reason that this design -- and many other free flights -- can fly with their balance points well aft of "the usual location" is that they employ "lifting tails". The Yogi's stabilizer has a thin cambered airfoil, and is rather large in area -- 114 square inches the way I figure it. That's for something like 275 squin of working wing area. Pretty big! [JW]

Don Butman <  > received plenty of suggestions from helpful SMALLsters for methods of transferring model part patterns to sheet balsa. Don's grateful to all:

A BIG THANKS to all that took the time to answer my request for printing on balsa wood!!! A couple of them I've tried but not too successfully.

The felt-tip makers have a "blender" pen that also works, but not for me!

My final solution was to securely glue a copy of the formers onto a sheet of 1/32" balsa and then just cut 'em out with a sharp X-Acto knife. This kept the 1/32 sq. cut-outs in place, and didn't add too much weight -- as I did not try to remove the paper.

AL A. A. Lidberg model plan service ff/rc kits and plans < http:www.aalmps.com >

...Al, you sure made a lot more work for yourself than was necessary! K&S's tinplate stock solders READILY with electronic solder (60-40) and resin flux. In fact, if you keep the sheet metal clean (wiping off the joint surfaces with acetone just before soldering) you need no added flux. Just use resin- core electronic solder. (There's no need for, or benefit from, using lead-free solder in modeling applications.)

Sta-Brite flux is meant for high heat applications; too hot for resin flux. It contains an acidic salt of hydrochloric acid. I dislike even having this kind of flux in my shop, because the fumes it releases can catalyze rusting of steel tools. (Voice of experience here!)

Resin flux has one evil effect in model fuel tanks. Alcohol (as in glow fuel) dissolves it -- and you DON'T want flux in your combustion chamber! Therefore whenever I make a metal tank, I rinse its interior thoroughly, with 2 or 3 half-tankfuls of isopropyl alcohol or lacquer thinner -- shaken up well, then expelled. [JW]

Dave Segal <  > wrote:

I have managed to find some real excitement in my life by building a hot delta model, the Reflex, from John Hoover at Aspect Aviation. It is powered by a .25FX engine, goes like a rocket, and has looks that make it seem even faster!

I have used this engine on other planes, but did not get such high speeds. The frontal area of the delta vs. the conventional planes does not seem so different, and I wonder what the cause might be?

I had a hard time with takeoffs from grass using my usual 9x5 prop. A retired aerospace engineer in my club said that long takeoffs are characteristic of full-size deltas and canards. I went up a little in wheel size, and went to a 9x6 prop. That seemed to help takeoff a bit, and produced great speed in the air.

As I don't need such high level-flight speeds, would a 10x5 be good? I also prefer vertical climb ability to level speed.

Regards, Dave Segal

...Dave, I've covered this before, but not for a while. Guess it's time for a reprise. Propeller THRUST is proportional to the square of the rpm, but to the FOURTH POWER (square of the square) of the diameter. (This assumes identical prop "geometry".) Therefore, assuming that your .25 will spin the 9-6 and 10-5 at about the same rpm -- you can expect about 50% more static thrust from the ten-incher.

BUT: an even more important factor is what brand of prop you're using. There's a HUGE difference. For the kind of flying you're talking about -- comparable to control-line stunt, I'd guess -- the Australian Bolly props are considered the ultimate. But they're hard to find in the USA, and exceedingly high-priced.

The Graupner "Super Nylon" props are also pricey: $4.70 for a 10-5 in the latest Hobby Lobby catalog. But Graupners are only slightly less efficient than Bollys. H. A. Thomas introduced me to Graupner props about 15 years ago, and for rpm under about 16,000 I know of nothing better for my own model flying.

APC props work great at high rpm -- they come into their own at speeds of say 18,000+. I haven't yet tried their newer, wider- blade styles. Those may do well at lower engine speed. [JW]
