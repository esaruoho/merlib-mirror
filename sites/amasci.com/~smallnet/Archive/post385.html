David Larkin < dslarkin@blvl.igs.net > contributed:

Regarding Dan McLeod's Super Tigre, he might want to touch base with Brian Wattie (also in Ottawa) who used them. As I recall, this engine was from around the time when mufflers were being introduced, and it was one of the engines which did not take too kindly to mufflers.

The reason was sub-piston induction, the sleeve of the piston being relieved so that there was a gap on either side of the exhaust port at TDC. (Now I'm speaking from memory and may be subject to correction).

As I recall, Super Tigre solved the problem eventually by no longer machining out a rectangular exhaust port, but cut out one in which the lower edge was curved.

Another engine of that timeframe was the Webra .21 Glo-Star. I had one and liked it a lot, but when I added a muffler it would hardly fly my Dave Boddington 'Red Arrow'. So I bought an O.S. .25 and let the Webra go, rather sadly.

I must look into your modification mount for the K&S wire bender. This is a device which has served me well. The company stands behind its product. I did manage to break mine when I used it on some incredibly hard 1/8 wire. I enquired about buying a replacement part, but the company sent it to me free.

While I'm feeling garrulous I should also suggest that anyone using vintage glow heads (and certainly also Cox engines) should not use a power panel for ignition, but stick to a ni-cad device like those provided by McDaniel or Head Lock. For Cox engines I use the Head Lock 1/2A adaptor.

If anyone in SMALL is also into SAM flying, they may be interested to hear Roy Bourke's proposals concerning 1/2A Texaco competition. Roy is our Canadian SAM committee chairman and has proposed opening up the engine choice in Canada, given the problems with availability and performance of Cox reedvalve engines.

He himself plans to use either Mills .75 or Irvine Mills .75 engines. These will make for more enjoyable and consistent flying. I'm also going to try the PAW's -- the old Classic 80 and either regular 80s or 049s, depending on whether I want to go for a long engine run or a fast climb.

Unfortunately the question of engine choice for 1/2A has become very much a 'sacred cow' in the US SAM community: usually with the justification for the limitation to Cox 049 reedvalve engines is based on the 'level playing field' argument, and keeping costs down.

Trouble is that some modellers have been known to buy more than a dozen Texaco 049s to find one that is 'level' enough!

Roy has done a number of engine tests. These show, as did a similar series I did about 8 years ago, that there is a considerable difference in performance between different designs of diesels, and that the fact that an engine is a diesel, does not necessarily make it a competitive choice.

But what virtually all the diesels have in common is that they perform with much greater consistency than the Cox Texaco engines. I was hoping that the old McCoy 049D (with the poppet valve in the venturi) would be suitable, but it turns out to be rather thirsty.

Of course you have to make your own 5 cc tank. But many of us used to do that for control line, and if I could make one as a teenager, I should be able to do it again now.

Best regards, Dave

Tommy Wilson < jawa@ies.net > reports on his formerly nose- heavy R/C model:

Thanks for the words of wisdom & advice. One thing I forgot to mention earlier was that I had added downthrust when I installed the O.S. .20, as I thought that it might be required. That's why I was so surprised to see the model climb so steeply even though it was nose-heavy.

I did a lot of thinking on what had been advised, so decided to give it a try. With the CG back where it should be, the model should be able to fly OK even if is breezy, which it has been here for several days...

Friday by lunch time I was really curious as to what was going to happen, so decided to fly the model even with the strong wind blowing harder than ever.

I assembled the model & started the engine behind the car as a wind break. Stepping out into the wind, I hand-launched the model into the turbulence spilling from a building a quarter- mile up-wind. My plane flew out and away from me, climbing steadily as it should. However, it was bouncing around & rocking from the rough air. I throttled back & it cruised at a level attitude, hardly making any forward progress. So I increased the throttle enough to make headway. It was fun!

Then I realized that although the model flew OK, getting it down again through the turbulence would be a problem! I did not relish the task of re-building it after it cart-wheeled after arrival! I gained height by opening up; then took a few quick glances around & saw an extent of coarse grass a short distance away.

I managed to keep the model more-or-less on an even keel & a constant heading towards the grass as I walked towards it. On the second attempt I flew it down gently into the grass. No damage at all, and a huge boost to the ego!

The wind was 25 mph gusting to 30 mph -- when I went back to work I checked with the gate house where they have a wind speed read-out.

The elevator had no down trim & did not seem to need any even with the strong wind. Hopefully the next flight will be under more favorable conditions with less wind, so that I can check out the aerobatics that the model was capable of performing & see what effect the corrective weight in the tail has.

Altogether I enjoyed a good flying session & lots of fun. The model survived & so did the pilot! Thanks again, Joe.

Regards, Tommy Wilson

Charlie Fries wrote, "Surprisingly, a total ON-OFF switch is not included in many speed controls !"

The absolute shutoff an electric power system is not the function of the ESC. You must incorporate an on-off switch to safely disarm the system. A very small amp capacity switch can be used, as it will never (normally) be actually doing any switching with current going through it.

Bernard Cawley < ABCawley@peoplepc.com > contributed:

It occurred to me that other SMALLsters might have an interest in this. Here's a note I sent to Charlie Fries in response to his comment about "total on/off switches" on electronic speed controls (ESC's) in Posting 384.

There are NO speed controls which include what I would call a "total" on/off switch. On all those that do have switches, the switch controls power to the BEC and the control electronics.

As long as the drive battery is connected, there is always a (small) chance that a static spark or some other failure of one of the output MOSFETs could cause the motor to turn, uncommanded, even with these switches "off".

The only safe way is to put a (big, heavy) switch in one of the motor leads (BEC controller) or drive battery leads (when using a separate receiver battery) if you can't easily plug and unplug the drive battery. This is what is properly called an arming switch. Some folks use fuseholders for this purpose if the fuse projects through the fuselage side.

The best solution (at least for me) is to have some quick, easy access to the drive battery, and just treat the prop as potentially "live" anytime the drive battery is plugged in.

Steve Morris < mlbco@sirius.com > reports:

I purchase quite a bit of RC equipment for my work, and lately I've come across some poor-performing products.

Several of my Hitec HS-81 and HS-85 servos have failed upon first use. The failure mode is they will only move properly in one direction and not in the other. It appears that some of the transistors on the servo circuit board go bad the first time they are loaded up, and the servo dies in one direction.

I've had this occur on 3 different brand-new servos when tested under light loading. I've also had this happen on a brand-new FMA servo. I'm guessing that a bad bunch of transistors made it into the production line of servo manufacturers, and could show up in several brands.

My local hobby shop sells Ni-MH battery packs for slow and park flyers. I purchased two pre-assembled 1600 x 7-cell packs, and both had improperly-welded connector tabs. This resulted in intermittent battery power and loss of control.

These packs are sealed in heat-shrink and look professionally made, but aren't. I've seen this problem in NiCad packs years ago, and thought manufacturers had become more careful since then. This sort of failure could easily crash a model, and possibly lead to injury.

It's been great that the past few years have brought us cheaper and smaller equipment, but the push for mass production and profit has let quality slide in some cases.

Be careful! Steve Morris

...I've also run into unreliable battery problems this year, Steve. Bought a new Hitec FM transmitter (to be able to use GWS Pico receivers), and when I first turned it on, the batteries were as dead as Joe Stalin. A 30-hour charge provided only 5 minutes of operating power; and fast- charging did no better.

I sent the Tx back to Hitec for warranty service -- BTW, they were unhappy about my doing that, but I insisted that they make good on their warranty.

It took about a month before I got the Tx back. The "service slip" enclosed said that a new nicad pack had been installed. Sure didn't seem that way to me! The "new" batteries were only a tad less dead than Mussolini.

I decided to replace the Hitec nicad pack -- a no-name brand -- with a set of Sanyos that I assembled myself. In doing that, I think I found out why the inferior nicads were used.

The Hitec Tx was designed to use alkaline AA's. The inside of its "battery box" has molded-in polarity markings, plus bosses for the battery contact springs. The Sanyo 650 mAh pack I made up wouldn't fit! In comparing the Sanyos with the dud nicads, I found the Sanyos were about 1/8" longer overall...

Anyway, if YOU are the owner of a recently-made Hitec R/C transmitter, I advise that you check out the capacity of its battery pack -- and to make SURE, I'd replace it with a set of Sanyo cells, even if they are hard to shoehorn into the available space. [JW]
