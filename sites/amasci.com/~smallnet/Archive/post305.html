Roy L. Clough, Jr. <  > contributed:

On .10 engines: In the mid-40's I bought an Atom, with subpiston, and the radial finned head, which I think was the last of that line. The instruction sheet said the subpiston would occasionally foul up, but I had very little trouble with it. The engine ran beautifully on cigarette lighter fluid and SAE 70 oil. The only problem I had with it was ignition capacitors -- two in a row were bad.

This engine was used in a stressed-skin paper plane control-liner built for Popular Science. "Pappy" Herbert test flew it for them -- and had another ignition failure. I used the engine in a C/L delta wing ducted fan of exceptionally limp performance -- you could whip it into the air, but it would only fly level at top output.

Also used the engine in a cabin plane I foolishly turned loose in Elizabeth Park in Hartford, Connecticut. (No problem, it circled up and circled down and landed nicely; but everybody commented on the noise, and I didn't repeat the performance.)

Also had the Atom in an early pylon free flight airplane, inspired by Carl Goldberg's Zipper. Finally I converted it to compression ignition with nothing but an aluminum disk and a cardboard gasket, held in place with a 3-48 screw through the sparkplug hole.

I screwed the cylinder up and down in the crankcase to adjust compression, and it ran beautifully on 70% anesthetic ether and 30% drug store castor oil. Only problem was the castor dissolved the nitrate dope on the fuselage of the Nieuport 17 to which the engine was mounted. (Sans cowl.)

I wondered if the cylinder or case threads would suffer from taking the thrust of the combustion, but had no problems. Finally I filed off the aspirator carburetor and reworked it to spraybar needle valve. It still ran, but started harder.

I've used a number of Cox .09 engines and they all ran well with a variety of sleeve throttles, no throttles, and conventional barrel- types. I also have a Cox .09 Medallion with a very peculiar piston type exhaust choke and a rotating needle valve. That works OK too.

My favorite sport .10 seems to be no longer available. The Magnum .10 I own has flown a six-foot tailless, operates in any position, starts with a flip, doesn't overheat as a pusher and is an all-around good guy. I like it, but can't say how it would stack up alongside later engines.

Charlie Fries <  > reports:

Re the comments about Norvel .061 idling. I had troubles from the start. Suffice it to say that they all disappeared when I replaced the plug with an old garden-variety COX plug. Starts easily. Idles well. In my Aqua-Star.

Re: projects for small electric fans. You can't go wrong with Dan Belfort's "Electro-Screamer" as published in Model Builder, January 1995. I built one about March of '95, with HiLine's RED FLAME BLASTER fan unit.

Have flown it frequently every year since. Not pristine right now, but a good flyer that is not squirrely. Suggest that you use the newer small 500 mAH batteries---7 of 'em. I flew it with the typical AA and it was reliable, but the reduced weight of the smaller ones is truly noticeable in flight performance.

Charlie Fries

Scott Winans <  > amplifies the ducted fan topic:

I would like to add mention of several other small ducted fan activities that are out there. The small-size fans recently avail- able have created a surge in interest, with quite a few designs now being kitted, or talked about on the 'homemade' front.

There are 3 fans that I am familiar with available in the 2" diameter size;

  • The old standby Union fan that has been around for many years, 2.35", found in the Great Planes Sonic Blast FF.

  • The Cox unit, 2", found in the 'Airlifter' and upcoming 'Boomerang'. These are both foam RTF R/C ships. The Airlifter is a twin-fan airliner-style ship, controlled with differential thrust steering. The Boomerang is a flying wing, single-channel, rudder- only. Both ships are fine for novice flyers, and quite a bit of fun to fly! This fan unit is available as a replacement item for the Airlifter now, in a neat foam nacelle, but is also on its way to hobby shop shelves soon for probably about $20 street price. Thrust is about 2.25 ounces for good lifetime of the motor.

  • The Knight and Pridham unit, 2", called the EDF-200, available in several places (Todds Models, Electric Jet Factory, others?). This is an earlier generation design of the Cox unit, comes with a higher revving motor that operates at much higher current levels. Thrust is about 2.5 ounces for reasonable motor lifetime.
All of these units are excellent places to start for designing your own 'JET'! All allow for motor replacement, so testing various motor/battery combinations is pretty simple. I have become so excited about using them it's been hard to contain all the ideas that pop up ! and it is exciting to see the momentum gathering and new designs being talked about/shared for these little fans.

I am a designer for Estes/Cox. The Airlifter and Boomerang are my babies. This is not just a shameless plug for Estes, but rather I want to share the excitement that I have for these fun and rather inexpensive little fans and all the neat opportunities they open for model design.

I hope this helps to stir some interest for a few of you.

Sincerely, Scott Winans

Sam Brauer <  > contributed:

In response to your recent request for material, let me fill people in on a combination that DOESN'T work. I've been working on the Dare Sopwith Triplane, and I wanted to go to a lighter motor than a Speed 400. It turns out the battery pack is on the center of gravity, so

I thought I could go to a lighter motor, move the battery pack forward and lighten the airplane.

The airframe is quite light and not very rugged, therefore reducing the mass of the airplane seems like a good idea. Yes, I know to build them to fly, not to crash, but this is REALLY light.

Furthermore, anyone considering building one of these airplanes should be forewarned: the instructions are essentially nonexistent, and the construction is not typical. So far though, it seems to be well thought out.

Anyhow, I wanted to try something smaller than a Speed 400. I would have used Dick Miller's motor (MG1) -- the same combination that's used in the Yard Bee -- but I was concerned that the prop would be too small. Therefore I tried a Speed 300 with a Horst 4:1 gearbox, an APC 8 x 3.8 inch electric prop, and a Pixie Speed controller; along with 7 (600AE) cell pack.

The problem lies in the gearbox. The gearbox is designed for Speed 280 motors, and is just a plastic sleeve that fits over the motor. The Speed 300 is a smidge larger than a Speed 280; so after pulling off the decals and revving up the trusty Dremel, I had the gearbox snugly mounted on the Speed 300. Playing around with the gearbox in the shop I seemed to have good pull from the motor. (Very scientific.) Using the very accurate test of: If you can hold onto the motor, it's not too hot to fly; that seemed to work as well.

The problem came in after a few minutes of running wide open. This motor does get warm, and unfortunately so does the shaft that the prop is mounted on. This shaft is a piece of wire around 3/32" or so, and plenty strong enough for the task when cool, although it's just a press fit.

Unfortunately the gearbox is thermoplastic and when the motor warms up and the shaft does as well from the friction generated, the shaft comes out of the gearbox with the propellor attached. No adhesive is going to work given these materials, plus I think the material of the gearbox itself is melting.

This shouldn't be seen as an indictment of the Horst gearbox. It was designed to sit on front of a Speed 280 and be light and cheap. The Speed 300 generates about double the RPM of a Speed 280 and thus is much more power (and heat) than the gearbox was designed for.

The one thing I'd watch is, if I was really putting a lot of watts into a Speed 280 and the motor was running very hot for a long period of time the same thing might happen.

I've since ordered an S-300 motor/gearbox from Mike's Tiny Models in Germany. When I get the thing up and running, I'll let you all know what I think.

Sam Brauer
