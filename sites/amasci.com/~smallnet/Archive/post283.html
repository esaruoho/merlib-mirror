Ken Park <  > reports:

Well, it's minus 10 degrees Celsius in Ottawa, but the flying is just wonderful! Clear skies and no wind at all. Perfect weather to test fly a new airplane. I built "Chips": a Randy Randolph design, constructed from his plans. The only change made from the plans was a foam wing.

The plane uses 5 mini servos. I opted for a Y-harness and a servo in each wing. The roll rate is amazingly responsive this way.

I bought a brand new Blue L.A..15 by O.S. Well, it ran like it was already broken in even at -10 degrees. High praise from me!

The plane tracked down the runway rock steady and climbed steadily. Only a few clicks of elevator were all it needed to fly hands off. The plane has no bad habits. Stalls are straightforward and gentle. Loops and rolls are just plain fun to do. I've flown Chips a total of 4 flights, and it cruised nicely at 40% throttle.

I got a 4-ounce tank in with a shoe horn and flights are always in the 30-minute range! When you cut power, Chips goes into a gentle rock steady glide and with a little flare at the end touches down feather light on her mains. If you flare a couple of times you can bleed off enough airspeed to stop with a 3-foot rollout.

No skis were needed, as Ottawa is almost snow-free this winter. There wasn't a flake on the runway! My friend Mark Winstanley flew his Chips today too, and his first flight was exactly the same as mine. Ken Park

Dave Larkin <  > contributed:

I went over this business of spraybar orifice orientation with Gig Eifflander at the factory recently, and it should be exactly as you say - symmetrical with both holes just visible from the bottom at full throttle position.

The PAW 049 and 060 should have a pretty good idle at around 3,500 rpm, which is a hell of a lot better than any glow engine that size. Now the idle may 'roll' a little bit and seem unstable, but it should be fine.

I haven't seen any correlation between idle and ether content, but think that if someone has a poor idle the two most likely causes are:
* spraybar position (easy to disturb if you adjust the throttle arm),
or
* incorrect adjustment of compression and mixture.

With regard to the latter, a diesel will run smoothly at a huge range of adjustment positions, from too rich with too low a compression to too lean and too high a compression. Some people have trouble finding the optimum position, which is that setting which gives maximum rpm.

Also sometimes a diesel will not idle too well until it is broken in and you can run it at a relatively lean setting.

By the way, there is an Elf biplane kit available from Ben Buckle, though I think it is slightly enlarged from the original. Dave

I built a Pou du Ciel about 15 years ago, from RCM plans, I believe. Powered it with a Saito 45, although it was designed for a Saito 30, the open rocker version, which I could not obtain at that time.

JW wrote: "...many's the time I've considered building myself a Pou. But those elliptical-dihedral, reflexed-airfoil wings always stopped me. I did figure out a practical way of constructing the wings -- but I never solved the problem of attaching the covering. I surely don't want to do that the way Mignet did: stitch the covering to every rib. But how else can you adhere the material to the full contour (top and bottom) of those wiggly-eel-shaped ribs?"

Right now, there's a new breed of Flea clattering around England and France, called the HM14/93. It's a "modernised" HM14 that looks pretty much like Henri's original. Differences are a wing section based, I believe, on a NACA 23012 with some reflex on the last 30% - amount unknown. The wings are now flat across the top, and have acquired a tip made from one bow, as opposed to that nightmarish arrangement on the original whereby the LE and TE were hauled around and up to meet up with that curved spar. If you made them like Lazy Bee tips - the laminated ones, not the monstrosities off the Speedy - you could be very close from the photos I've seen of the 14/93.

Would imagine the wings, which have a significant amount of dihedral due to the uplift on the lower surfaces towards the tips, would work well for rudder / elevator control. The 14/93 might have a longer span front wing, and the aft wing was moved back to get it out of the slot effect of its LE being under the TE of the fore wing. Not sure about this, but I think there's a cutout for the rudder aft of the rear spar, and the wing is further back on a fuselage much as the HM14 had.

The only way I ever figured to make the wing is basically to make it like Henri did, but smaller! I had a swing at an HM14 once - developed the mainspars by hand on a drawing board, built them from solid balsa with ply skinning. Henri did it by 'stringing' the spars like a recurve bow, messing with stand-offs to get the shape from the "bowstring" and then nailing ply over the spars. His book showed the method in sketch and dimension form.

You'd need to really want to build one to build one!

Other "modern" developments have included full ailerons on the front wing -- kind of defeats the purpose - and many have various trimming flaps to help out in pitch.

....Gang, to provide a little more "dimension" to the "Flying Flea" picture: I've owned a first "English Edition" copy of Henri Mignet's Pou book for close to 50 years, and have re-read it many times. (This book was recently re-published, and Bill Hannan { Hannan's Hangar } sells copies.)

This book reveals some little-known facts about the Pou and its designer. Mignet started his "home-built aircraft" career with a much more conventional design than the Pou. But flying that turned out to be beyond Mignet's abilities. (However, Otto Kuhni has a "pistachio-sized" FF CO2 version of this pre-Pou Mignet design, and it performs marvelously.)

Mignet then took a new approach (the Pou), which he vigorously insisted was NOT an "airplane"! He claimed that airplanes were too difficult for the average person to learn to fly, largely because their control systems were un-natural. That was the reason for the Pou's unique "3-control" setup, wherein the rudder acted exactly opposite that of a conventional aircraft. (The Pou steered like a "Flexible Flyer" sled: push forward with your right foot and the vehicle turns LEFT.)

Because the aerodynamic arrangement and control setup of the Pou were so different from that of a "real airplane", it killed a number of experienced pilots.

The deadliest characteristic of the Pou came from its variable- incidence front wing. The Pou's airfoil had a rather sharp leading edge radius. That characteristic induces abrupt stalling. Now, nobody in his right mind would attempt aerobatics in a Pou. But because of the craft's small size and light weight, sharp turns in it seemed safe...

As the Pou's bank angle increased (from the dihedral effect), its nose would begin dropping. The pilot might then pull back on the stick to level the turn. Far too often this induced a stall.

And here's where the Pou's tandem wing became an instrument of destruction. The top, front wing when stalled lost much of its lifting ability. But the REAR wing, entirely behind the pilot, continued lifting just as well as ever. The result: a steep dive.

Natural instinct made too many pilots continue pulling back on a stalled Pou's stick to the limit of their strength. That only made the situation worse, and many Pous met the earth in semi- inverted death dives.

When the US CAA became aware of this ghastly flaw in the Pou's flight characteristics, they revoked the aircraft's Certificate of Airworthiness. Further: at the same time, the CAA banned ALL use of "lifting tails" in man-carrying airplanes; a ban that is still in effect today.
