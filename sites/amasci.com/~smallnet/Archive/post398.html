Dr. Michael Hawkins < mikeh@samart.co.th > submitted:

The fun of small models is in trying something different. Some- times it works, sometimes not -- but we learn a bit each time.

A year or more ago I was trying to get an Eric Clutton-designed Sharkface with a Norvel, to fly off water. It made a great motor boat but I never did get it to lift off. Recently I had a Clancy Yard Bee kit and built a new fuselage with the old Sharkface floats fitted. With an O.S. 10 FSR, it did not want to lift off, so I put an SC 12 in it. This ran at about 12,200 rpm on a Master 7 x 5 Scimitar, but kept dying after a few minutes.

I did get the airplane to lift off, but it sagged into the rushes on the bank. I then put a Magnum 15 in it (same holes on the engine mount). I used the same silencer (muffler to you colonists) and thought I would check if there was a hole through the nipple. There was not -- and what was more, the drilled hole, blocked with carbon, was so small that I could not get a large pin through it.

I drilled it out to 1/16th. This was an early SC silencer, and other examples had the large hole in their nipple -- but it might be worth a check if you have a similar problem.

This setup now gives about 15,000 rpm on an APC 7 x 6 with straight fuel. I use a folded metal tank soldered up from a tin can.

First attempt at takeoff I dug a float in when all that power came on. Tried again and got it off for the most exciting 20 seconds flying that I or the spectators have seen for a while. The model disappeared over the trees behind my back in a series of 90- degree rolls, heading for the restaurant and the car park -- only to reappear in an inverted dive.

By this time I had realised that the airplane had FAR too much control movement, and managed to complete a reasonable flight by not moving the stick more than 1/16th inch each way.

It may be that some of the onlookers were muttering things about "incompetent pilot". It may be that they were right, but on the fourth visit to the field I got it to fly and will be back to do it again next week. I am happy.

That is enough for now. I will tell you my recent diesel experiences next time.

All the best, Mike

Mike Gillihan < GillihanMike@JohnDeere.com > wrote:

This is a simple topic when compared to the sometimes brutally complex material on engine porting, etc., but here goes.

Why is it that so many people insist on shoving the biggest engine possible on every airframe they have? To each his own I suppose, but in most cases I feel a point of diminishing returns is reached at a certain point. Fully forty percent of my customers have elected to power the aircraft that I have designed with an engine gobs bigger (for lack of a better term) than the aircraft was designed for. While the airframe is certainly strong enough to handle the power, the flight characteristics of the airplane change dramatically.

Designers generally put a lot of thought and research into their aircraft. I designed my biplane to be flown with an .061 engine. In doing so I considered airfoil, design weight, wing area/ incidence/loading, moments, control surface area and frontal area. All were optimized to the best of my ability, to extract the best performance possible using a 1 cc glow powerplant.

I have surprised even myself, as the performance with a Norvel .061 up front is excellent. I have received many letters from customers, and have yet to receive a complaint on performance with the designed power source.

However, those that have chosen to use larger engines always report flight characteristics that are not within the intended parameters. Quote: "My God, that's a fast sucker!" "What engine do you have on it?" "Oh, I used an O.S. .10." Well, DUHHH!

The biplane has 420 square inches of wing, but sports a very thin airfoil. At full throttle, the aircraft is MUCH faster than one would expect with the .061, but retains a wing loading light enough to hang around the sky at ridiculously low speeds when throttled back. That was the design parameter for the aircraft's speed envelope. With double the horsepower and a healthy increase in weight, performance is still good -- but the aircraft has become something I did not intend it to be. Stall speeds are higher. "P" factor is greater. Controls set at the recommend throws become very sensitive. Yes, that looks like a lot of wing for an .061, but trust me.

In talking to other designers and manufacturers, I find I am not alone. I do feel that some companies market their wares with as large a power specification range as possible, presumably to try to gain sales by hitting on an engine size that a potential customer has sitting around waiting on an airplane. "The New Whoop-De-Do 2000, for .020 to 1.20 engines!" Fine. But which engine do I need on there to be happiest?

In my last ad I am guilty of mentioning that engines up to .10 have been used (I'll not do that again), but I did list the .061 as "the designer's preferred engine". A reasonable range for the biplane would be .061-.074, glow or diesel. But hey, it's a free country... Now if I could just get some help mounting this 427 Chevy on my skateboard!

Mike Gillihan

Stephen L. Cheney < slcheney@mindspring.com > observes:

I remember the old model kits very well -- especially a line of prefabricated die-cut kits offered by Top Flite. They used sheet sided fuselages and sheet wings & empennages, with tab-in-slot construction. The sheets were printed with simulated riveted panels and other details to give the model a touch of realism without painting or decals.

As I remember, they were available in Piper Cub, Aeronca, and my favorite, the "Rascal". It's making a comeback under the Sig banner (ARF, Ugh!). The Top Flite Rascal didn't have wing struts, which were always getting busted on the other designs.

These kits would go together in an evening or two -- even for a ham-fisted ten-year-old. They encouraged me (through the success- ful building and flying of these models) to expand my efforts. My first powered kit was a Jasco Jet 100 -- which was basically just a plan and some sheet balsa with the precursor of Lite-Ply for the engine pod. It would give several minutes duration with a Jetex 100.

We are blessed with a pretty well-stocked local hobby shop, to whom I funnel my business if they can even get in the ball park on the price. I thought I would try to snare my 9-year-old grandson with a stick-and-tissue kit like I had built with my Dad in the late 40's. I picked out a Peck Polymers "One Night 16", thinking the title was indicative of the building time (as we are dealing with a finite attention span).

I thought the kit was taped shut to keep the parts from being lost by inquisitive fingers. Boy, did I get a wrong number! For my twelve bucks I got the old ten cent "kit". An instruction sheet (you couldn't call them plans), a bundle of 1/32" sticks, a plastic prop and rubber band, a sheet of Jap tissue (festive red!), and a small sheet of 1/16" balsa with parts printed on the wood -- not stamped, not die-cut, not laser-cut -- just printed on the wood!

The wing ribs were so small that I had to use magnifying glasses to see the camber. I have yet to meet the ten-year-old who would be able to assemble this kit without assistance.

The question was, "Where should this sixty-year-old look for assistance?"

I quickly determined that if I wanted to insure that my grandson would never try to get into my model stuff, then I should max out his frustration by trying to force him into attempting to convert this scant $1 of materials into something enjoyable to build that might fly. There was no way that the effort that would be required to assemble the kit would be justified by the resulting model.

I went back to my dealer (the rip-off wasn't his fault!) and spent an almost identical amount on one of Hobbico's fine little electric FF models -- and we were having flying fun in just a few minutes after returning home.

With the best of intentions to create another model builder, I contributed to the creation of another ARF "modeler" and all that goes along with instant gratification. I haven't given up yet, but I'll clearly have to try another tack. SC

...SC, aside from the all-pervasive effects of inflation (back in the heyday of those Top Flite "Jigtime" kits you remember so fondly, I bought a MG TC sports car for $1600) a major reason for the high price of today's model plane kits is that they're no longer mass-produced.

The Top Flite "Jigtime" models were turned out in batches of ten thousand. Now kits like the Peck Polymers' small rubber models are packed a dozen or so at a time. That's expensive. [JW]

Andy Woitowicz < ajw@ntl.sympatico.ca > contributed:

My reply to the discussion on poor throttling, be it diesel or glow, became way too lengthy for a submission to SMALLnet. For those interested, I'd be happy to send a copy directly. The bottom line is that no excuse can be made for poor throttling. I've done the work, made the changes and have been happy, along with a number of others with the results. If you're happy with the way your engine throttles, then that's what's important. Maybe my standards are too high, but so be it.

On the subject of building, I was surprised to read of a complaint about laser-cut kits. When I do build a kit, I sure wouldn't want to go back to the days of die crushed, inaccurate rubber powered models that didn't fly because the wood was rock hard and the hardware hopelessly too heavy. Consider the many thousands of youngsters who gave up on modeling because of this.

I gave my 13-year-old nephew a Herr Cub to build, and with absolutely no experience, he did just that: build it accurately. It turned out light and was a very good flyer. That was the only plane he has built, but today, at the age of 19, he has expressed an interest again, and is building a Goldberg Eaglet.

He has also started on his own design, and is at the same time working on a corrugated plastic thingy that he's downloaded off the Internet. The thing is, the seed was planted and is finally blooming into a potential lifelong modeller.

But, I do see the point about building from plans. Selecting your wood, cutting accurate parts, and being able to input your own ideas and techniques, is certainly very rewarding for those so inclined. I guess maybe that's why I didn't build the Cub but instead gave it to someone who could benefit from its qualities as a kit.

Back to engines: I seem to have stumbled on a method of restoring the fit to worn-out engines. This includes ABC, AAN, AAC glow engines, and diesels with cast iron pistons. It's occurred to me that this might become a worthwhile commercial venture. I'd like to test the process before proceeding. If you have a diesel or glow, any size up to .25, that is baffed, worn out, fried, totaled etc., I will take the first half dozen of each engine type that are willing to participate in the experiment.

You pay the freight here, I'll pay the freight back. (I live in Canada). For diesels, I only need the piston. For glows, I need both the piston and cylinder. All I ask is that you run the engine and give me feedback, positive or negative.

Eternally grateful to SMALLnet, Andy Woitowicz

Dave Larkin < dslarkin@blvl.igs.net > contributed:

I was very interested, and not a little envious, to read Mike Rolls' story about his PAAgeboy. I started one for a 1cc (0.06 cubic inches) contest that was proposed by our SAM group in 1988, but made some mistakes in the construction and did not finish it. I thought it ought to be an excellent flier, and am glad to have this confirmed.

I used to know Vic Smeed when I was a teenager in the Eastbourne MFC, and have always enjoyed his designs, having become a compul- sive builder of Tomboys. Lately I've been very tempted to build his small R/C model the Chatterbox (so called because the original had some form of pulse proportional single channel control. It would be, in my opinion, a great model for modern micro gear or even ACE single channel pulse.

Has anyone built one lately? Like the PAAgeboy, it came out as a free full-size plan in Aeromodeller. A similar interesting model is Chris Foss' Smoke Trail, or perhaps Vic's Cherub.

But the PAAgeboy, with its greater wing area, and lighter construction, should make a better 'Park Flyer'.

I'm still a keen proponent of 'radio assisted free flight' By this I mean a model with the minimum of radio, just rudder control, and perhaps throttle or shut-off. You let it climb to altitude with a limited engine run, then enjoy the glide, hunting for a little lift, and bringing the silent model back to land at your feet. It's a relaxing form of flying. I think it is a great pity that SAM ever allowed elevator control in their competition models, thus allowing people to control the pitch effects of overpowered and inappropriate engines.

Apart from my experiences with several Tomboys, I had enormous fun with a Mills 1.3 powered Paul Plecan Hepcat. I used the Ben Buckle kit wood, resulting in a fuselage that would have made a fine defensive weapon, but it still flew well until it got thoroughly oil soaked. There's a new fuselage being built. I can't comment about the wood quality in current Ben Buckle kits, but the kits I've seen since I built the Hepcat, had much better wood.

The way has been opened up in Canada for more enjoyable SAM 1/2A Texaco flying. Roy Bourke introduced rules that allow us to use any 1/2A engine. As expected, the Mills 0.75 has emerged as a contender, particularly the Irvine Mills, but certainly hasn't had things all its own way. No need to use fussy reedvalve engines, but they are still in contention. Dave
