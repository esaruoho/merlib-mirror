Roy Clough <  > sticks his oar in:

Got to agree with JW's notes on castor oil, wouldn't fly without adding a bit to any commercial fuel. My first experience with it was tons of years ago when I converted a sub-piston Atom .097 to fixed-head diesel. It ran great by screwing the cylinder back and forth into the case to discover just where the compression was right. Then I held it in place somehow (maybe a drop of Loctite) and never changed the setting again.

Ran, as well as any of the other diesels I've owned, on two parts drugstore ether to one part laxative castor. As for the castor lube, I discovered that it was apparently a solvent for the old acetate dope I had on the model. Made a mess of it.

Let's see if anybody else remembers this: One of the mechanical/ science magazines ran a piece on Ray Arden, I think in the late 1930's. One illustration of his tiny engines showed one with a bore of about 1/8" running full blast, but the head was devoid of anything resembling a spark plug, and I saw no wiring in the picture.

As for Mark Meunier's comments on the throttle arm position of his ASP .12; my .46 has the arm straight down at half throttle and advances and shuts down at about 45 degrees on each side.

I hesitate to mention it, but if the servo has buzzing extremes at both ends of the arm travel, then drill another shorter-throw hole for the control rod!

Chuck Swiger's comments on Little Sticks and models with minimal control using a single S-133 servo: I still get mail about my Multiwiz (MAN, August 1992). I concocted a simple mechanism worked by an S-133 in which movement of a single stick either side of neutral gave proportional R-L rudder -- and further movement to the right gave "up" elevator -- and further movement to the left gave "down" elevator. It proved very easy to use.

The original model still survives, along with video tape showing its very lively performance with a Cox reed .049.

Roy C.

Otto Loorents <  > contributed:

Curt Huletts' comment on the wear-out of MDS engines is quite interesting. Joe's response to it about lubrication is also timely. Back in the April or May, 1969 issue of RCM, Clarence Lee wrote a comprehensive article on the subject of synthetic vs castor oil lubricants.

That was during a time before the marketing gurus latched on to synthetic oils, and cautionary articles could still be published without hurting the feelings of the advertisers.

Also, this pre-dated the stampede to ABC engine construction, and their clones. With the interference fit of the piston and cylinder, cold, at top dead center -- along with the use of synthetic oils, you are looking for trouble.

Frankly, from what I see at the field, most engines are used at part throttle while flying and the piston/cylinder probably never reach the proper operating temperature to take advantage of this type of engine construction. This interference fit at TDC leads to high stresses in the wrist pin/con rod.

Also, synthetics cook off at lower temperatures than castor. So it does not surprise me that the MDS engines are having rapid wear problems. Using synthetics I have ruined a K&B .20, K&B 40 and an OS .61 FS. AND these did not use ABC or similar construction.

If nothing else, in my 50 plus years in this hobby, there has been a ton of talking heads spouting various wonderful things about this and that, and it is difficult at times to separate facts from BS!

Otto Loorents

Frank Weaver <  > asserts:

I have never been without a Littlest Stick!!! When I toast the one I have, I build another right away. After all, it only takes one night. Presently I have one powered by a TD .010, no wing incidence, and fly it rudder/elevator. It does the greatest inverted sort of flat spins: just start a spin, then give it instant full down elevator, awesome!

Lot of fun with an old Cox Failsafe radio as well, flying single channel. The newbies can't believe what you can do with rudder- only. The guys with the big models all want a turn too; they can't get enough, but it seems to be below them to build one.

I guess I'll have to cut them off and make them build their own. The last time I took one to the field it was in the air all day long and I only got in one flight with it.

The "big model flyers" are slowly seeing the light, though. More and more small models are showing up at the field: many electrics thanks to GWS, and glow thanks to Norvel.

In answer to Mark's query about the throttle arm setting on his ASP, I have a carb from an ASP 15. I think it is similar to the .12's carb. If it has a low-speed needle on the right side of the carb, you can loosen the retaining nut and re-adjust the arm and centre it where you want. I had to drill another hole closer in, as my servo did not have enough throw.

To adjust the idle needle, put a length of fuel line on the fuel nipple and close the low speed needle all the way. Now slowly open the needle while blowing through the line to a point where the air just starts to go through the needle. Use a fuel bulb or syringe and you will notice the pressure difference easily.

I know of a few local modelers who have the ASP .12, and it seems to be a very strong small engine, probably since they have a ball- bearing supported crankshaft. I wore out my .15 after many, many flights. It was a great engine! I read a report in a British model magazine where they tested all the Asian engines, and claimed the ASP was the best O.S. clone made.

Fly SMALL, have a BALL, Frank

Michael Blott <  > submitted:

GWS has come out with a dual version of their IPS geardrive. Homebrewing a dual is easy with 15 minutes labor. A piece of 1/16" styrene plastic glued to the contralateral side of the motor provides the proper spacing for correct gear lash. Tie the second motor to it and lash with dental floss.

Flying the motor on 8 cells using a 9 x 7 prop barely got the motors warm. Besides the increased power, longer life should also be expected.
