Patrick LeRay <  > contributed:

I found a handy-dandy gadget at my local hobby shop, made by a company that caters to the model railroad guys. It's a hot-wire foam cutter, only 4" or so across the span. Heats instantly, and cools just as fast. Cutting through blue foam with this leaves a perfectly smooth, sealed surface, and is so much fun I sometimes find myself just making foam "things" of no particular use.

Pin a straight-edge of scrap balsa to one edge, line up another on the other side, and draw the wire across. Or pin 2 airfoil shapes to each side of a block, pull along the edges, and you get: a very short wing, but then slice that into sections for a buncha light- weight ribs. Two different size airfoils give you the ribs for a tapered wing!! Or formers for a fuselage. I glued (styro-glue) foam slabs to the 4 sides of a flat-sided fuselage, drew the wire along the corners at 45 degrees, then with a sanding block, in about 20 minutes I had a round section fuselage! At a 60 degree angle, you'd get an oval section fuselage. Quick and easy.

Source: Foam Cutter
The name is Subterrain Foam Cutter, made by Woodland Scenics, Box 98, Linn Creek, Missouri 65052. They also have a website. I think it's < http://woodlandscenics.com >. The tool is a little pricey at about $30, and $4-5 for 4 replacement wires (I haven't needed to replace mine), but there are a whole lot of potential uses for this tool in our kind of modeling.

Patrick LeRay in Louisiana

Don Garry <  > adds to the Thunder Tiger saga:

I have finally got my Thunder Tiger .07 running and handling to my liking. I tried everything possible with the stock setup, and could never get the engine to richen up while running open exhaust.

I even pulled the little aluminum cap and the inner jet (where the fuel takes a U-turn on the opposite side of carb from needle), checking for debris blockage. I never found any foreign matter anywhere in the (complicated) fuel system.

What I did find was very fine (small diameter) fuel jets throughout the entire system.

I drilled out all the jets as much as I dared, and completely overhauled the main needle assembly with a Cox "product engine" NVA. The main jet diameter on the Cox NVA is very much bigger than the stock TT main needle jet. It took a little "Dremel" work and a little silver soldering, but now I have a very nice fine thread needle assembly on the engine, with overall larger jets throughout.

The engine now has a very broad needle range and richens easily with the needle nowhere nearly backed all the way out (open exhaust).

As I mentioned above, about where the fuel takes a U-turn, in my opinion this is an area just waiting to gather any debris (crap trap) and cause problems -- as it will be hard to clean the "crap trap" area without taking it apart, which is a pain! And with the tiny stock fuel jets, it will not take much foreign matter to clog up.

I am getting 18,400 RPM open exhaust now on the same Sig 15% fuel and Cox 6 x 3 gray prop, but the engine still needs much more break- in. I expect it will probably come up to around 19,000 RPM by the time it gets fully broken in. I did not run it with its muffler any more yet, but the initial runs seemed to zap the RPM's with muffler attached. That's the opposite to what Gary found...

I expect more power from the Norvel .074, mainly because it is running the superior "one piece" glow plug. But I don't think the Norvel will ever idle and transition as well as the TT.07.

One other problem I encountered was that when I turned the fuel nipple around, it then interfered with the throttle lever arm. I installed three #3 washers between the throttle arm and throttle barrel as a fix. Everything is working nicely now.

Don Garry

Dave Campbell <  > relies to Mike Maupin's questions on the VA .049:

The VA .049 MKI was the lightest 1/2A made that I know of. This comes by way of nearly all-aluminum construction and a linerless case. The weight is 35 grams for a complete motor, and another 5 for the muffler they offered at the time.

As for the break-in, our instructions show that those props have to be cut down considerably to turn the RPM that the instructions mention. After adding 2 piston timing shims to mine, the best I got from a Cox 5-3 prop was 24,500 on Red Max 25%. The way the motor is shipped to you, it will not turn above 25K or so with any props on the market except the new APC's.

These motors are not really intended for sport flying, so you are really asking a lot of it by putting a 6-3 on it. They are just too wild for that, even when you fiddle with the timing. A 5-3 Cox is about as big as you want; but the new APC 4.7-4 and 4.2-4 will work great because of the smaller blade profile.

Since you mention adding a carb, my G-Mark throttled and muffled VA is the sweetest of all my 60-some odd 1/2A's. It's easy to start, idles and throttles well; you can talk over it at full power, and it maxes out at 20k on a 5-3. I guess that would make it the Ultimate T.D. 049?

My first VA .049 (set to run hot) was enough to lift a 14-ounce plane vertically from my hand, so your motor would be ideally suited for something really large (48"+) that doesn't need a lot of weight up front to balance. To put it on a 36" sport plane is really pushing it for small-field operations. This motor is a big enough step up from a T.D. that you'll have to start building a little stronger if you like to fly hotrods.

The G-Mark carb required very little machining to make it fit the VA. Use a rubber O-ring between the carb body and the case, and make a retainer nut/screw to fit in the old needle valve hole. Then you're in business. You could also JB-Weld the carb body on.

Dave

...Dave, I dislike raining on your high-rpm parade, but the thrust output of any propeller-driven airplane depends FAR more on the prop disk area than on the rpm. And in general, the optimum prop disk area is proportional to the wing area of the airplane. I have seen a Gentle Lady (wing area around 630 square inches -- I'm too tired to go out to the shop and check that number for accuracy) with a TD .049 on its nose spinning a 5 1/2" prop at something close to 28,000.

All that shrieking powerplant managed to do with the G.L. was pro- long its glide somewhat. On my advice the flyer removed the nose- mounted TD and replaced it with a pylon-over-the-wing Black Widow installation, swinging a Graupner 7-3 prop at half the rpm of the TD.

THEN the airplane flew great! [JW]

Carl Risteen <  > wrote:

The question of optimum incidence/decalage is, like most things in the real world, impossible to answer in solid black and white.

Although intuition would tend to suggest that ideally, a biplane should have its thrust line centered between the upper and lower wings, my experience has indicated otherwise. The first (full scale) Extra aerobatic competition monoplanes had the wing roughly on the thrust line. When the wing was later lowered for better pilot vision and cockpit comfort, performance did not suffer.

Most biplanes have fixed landing gear, the drag of which is significant; probably roughly equal to the profile drag of one wing, and the landing gear is well below the thrust line.

Reducing the incidence of the upper wing has various effects. First, reducing the wing incidence, upper, lower, or both, is the same as adding upthrust, since wings must fly at whatever angle of attack they need to generate the lift required for flight.

Reducing the wing incidence will force the fuselage to fly at a slightly increased angle, which is, in effect, adding upthrust. Adding upthrust is not the usual thing to do when you have a center of drag that is well above the thrust line.

Reducing the incidence of the upper wing (reducing decalage) has a de-stabilizing effect, particularly if the wings have positive stagger. That gives the wing combination more diving moment, and tends to require a larger horizontal tail for good handling. It also gives a slightly sharper stall break; good for clean entry to spins and snaps, but bad for a putt-around-the-field Sunday flyer.

In addition, it forces the lower wing to make up for the lift lost by decreasing the angle of attack of the upper wing. The lower wing must develop a higher lift coefficient -- and this increases its induced drag quite a bit...

Patrick Leray's quest for better stability by adjusting biplane wing incidences may be achieved more readily in other areas - moving the CG forward would be one. There isn't just 'one best way' and that has been borne out by the other submissions on this topic.

Horses for courses and planes for purposes. Just look at some of the immaculate de Havilland designs for example - the DH60 Moth and the DH82 Tiger Moth. The first with straight wings and little stagger flies fine. The Tiger has swept wings and much more stagger, and flies fine too. The difference was brought about by operational requirements and not some arcane aerodynamic formulae.

The RAF and Air Ministry wanted the Tiger to have improved cockpit egress, and DH's solution was to move the wing centre section forward ahead of the cockpit and moving the tips back 19 inches in order to avoid large CG movements. Voila ! The Tiger Moth, one of the best biplane trainers of all time, gained its swept wings !

...Malcolm, according to "The Tiger Moth Story" (Bramson & Birch, Cassell, London, 1964; pp 30 - 39) the Tiger Moth was never designed, but resulted from cut-and-try modifications to a DH 60 "Moth Trainer".

The complete story is given in the book, but that's too long to include here. However, there's room for a few excerpts:

"...it is almost unbelievable but nevertheless true to say that the Tiger Moth, perhaps the most successful biplane trainer of all times, was evolved without drawings."

"...they wished to move the centre section [of the top wing] forward of the front cockpit entrance to permit unimpeded exit from it. The amount of forward movement was not known, and the easiest way to determine this was to rig up the centre section with temporary struts until it was sufficiently clear of the cockpit."

"...initially the fuel tank which acted as a centre section...was moved forward 18 inches, but after Hubert Broad [the DH test pilot] and de Havilland himself had examined the new arrangements it was decided to move it forward another 4 inches. Since the upper wing was attached to the centre section, this too moved forward, placing the lift from the wings too far ahead of the aircraft's centre of gravity. To bring the aeroplane into balance again it was necessary to sweep back the wings."

"To achieve sweepback, it was necessary to shorten the rear spars on each of the four wings... Three sets of rear spars were tried before the engineers were able to re-establish the aircraft's centre of gravity in relation to lift. Here again trial and error were used to find the correct amount of sweepback..."

(Quotation from one of the engineers involved in this project): "After some discussion...it was decided to try moving the wings back 9 inches at the interplane struts from the original position. When the rear spars...had been shortened, the aircraft was temporarily rigged...and it was found that 9 inches was not enough... Rather than remove and refit new rear spars to all four wings it was decided to swing back the upper wings an additional 2 inches... This was sufficient to re-establish the centre of gravity range -- hence the difference in sweepback between the upper and lower wings."

"...when sweepback was added the tips of the lower wings were perilously close to the ground with the aircraft in the tail-down position...it was all too easy to strike the ground with one of the lower wing tips. To overcome this fault...inter-plane struts were shortened so lifting each lower wing at the tips."

"The Tiger Moth was born. There had been no complex calculations and no drawings; just a group of men in a little shed and a dis- mantled Moth Trainer..." [JW]
