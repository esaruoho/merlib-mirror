Michael Sommer <  > observes:

I have just received part of my order for the Cox fighters, having to wait an extra week for customs since I'm in Canada.

I bought them for the engine parts, but couldn't resist flying one so I have a few suggestions for those that had concerns about the fuel tank. I believe the reason for the plastic plugs in the fill and vent holes is to restrict the flow sufficiently to prevent fuel from squirting out and into the air when the tank is full. Since these things are designed for kids, this would be a safety feature.

I would also guess that the fill and vent holes are sized to fit the outside of the tubing supplied by Cox in their starting kits, as opposed to the protruding tubes we are more familiar with.

Attaching the fuel line to the engine is rather difficult due to the narrow slot provided. When I finally got it on, there was a kink from the near 180-degree bend made in such a short space.

My solution was to drill a 1/4" hole about 1" back from the landing gear, using the injection molding dimple to help start the bit. The fuel tube was then re-routed through this new hole, along the bottom, then back up through the original slot to the engine. You may have to rotate the tank (it is just pressed on) to position the outlet above the hole. Mine had already been apart to remove one of the plugs and epoxy a short length of brass tubing for a fill line and I can't recall what position the outlet originally was in.

I hope this might be of some use to those who intend to fly these planes, Mike

Alan Hahn <  > adds:

After reading Mike Cook's comments about the Droid Fighter fuel problem, I began to see my future retirement dreams (from reselling the $6 Tower Hobby Specials on E-Bay) go up in smoke!

So I broke down and opened one up. I think Mike should simply try to follow the directions. What he thinks are plugs, are actually fittings with holes in them. (The holes are certainly hard to see, since the "plugs" are black and inside a fueling hole in the plastic body.)

If you insert a (small!) fuel tubing into the fueling hole on the body (as the instructions advise) and squeeze the fuel can, fuel enters just fine into the tank. The outside of the fuel line seals with the fueling hole and not any fuel nipple (like on a normal Babe Bee tank).

Now I certainly can't rule out that Mike's particular model was flawed, but in this case it probably would pay to have no previous glow engine experience.

So now I can again plan on a luxurious future!. Maybe I'll try to fly (if that is an accurate description) mine tomorrow. By the way. I "only" bought 4, so my future isn't completely secure.

Don Garry <  > contributed a copy of an e-mail he sent to someone who contacted him directly about the same "flaw" that Mike Cook notified us about:

In regards to the "apparent" plugs in the fuel tank holes, please do not remove them! They are there for a reason: to keep your fuel in the tank and to help prevent siphoning and spillage. The main reason for this is to help prevent fire from excess fuel accumulating on the airplane. Cox has been sued for the "fire" thing, that is why they do everything possible to eliminate fire hazard.

The plugs in the fuel inlet holes act like an anti-splash device to keep the fuel "in" the tank. The fuel will go in the tank with no problem; just pretend the "plugs" are not there when fueling your Droids or Naboos.

I really can't believe so many people would assume the fuel holes are "blocked" without first at least trying to follow directions and fueling the planes as directed. Hope this helps you and others about the "phantom plugged fuel holes".

As to whether the planes will fly any better than the old Cox plastic ready-to-flys ..... I doubt it!

Hope you and all the others who bought out the Cox planes enjoy them very much; they should make great stocking stuffers for Christmas.

P.S. I see where someone just paid $14.95 for a Naboo on eBay .... someone tripled his money!! Don Garry

I bought 6 of the Cox Naboo planes. The fill and vent for the Naboo are two holes on top of the body. The holes also serve to attach the tank to the body a la conventional plastic rivet wizardry.

<  > questions:

There has not been much chat about PAW's recently and I do not recall seeing these particular topics covered. I bought one from Eric Clutton recently and got it started easily enough (see endless postings about pesky diesels).

I had to relocate the throttle arm, so took the chance to relocate the famous angled spraybar holes both pointing downstream at full throttle (see Posting 283). (As delivered one was somewhat visible from upstream at full throttle and the other was downstream).

Running on Red Max plain-bearing fuel and a balanced 7x3 Zinger. Question 1: The given needle valve settings are way rich. Opening it up to 2 turns for the first start, it pumped fuel all over the place. Even 1.5 turns is a mess. Ideal setting (backed off from misfiring due to over-lean is around 3/4 turn).

The fuel tank is a little clunk tank at the same level as the engine, pressurized mildly by a bit of fuel line connected to the vent pointing into the prop-wash. When I start the engine I point the plane slightly uphill to avoid flooding. The engine pulls fuel up happily at 3/4 turn when the plane is vertical.

I say all this to point out that I am not forcing fuel into the engine by a funny tank setup.

Question 2: If I can persuade the contra-piston to stick when running so I can back off the screw without it following, the vibration causes the cooling fins to start to unscrew quite briskly (the aluminum cooling fins are threaded onto the steel cylinder barrel). This makes it chaotic to re-establish compression settings as the fins are rather hot and greasy to screw down again while the engine is going. What kind of goo or technique would fix the fins firmly enough to keep them in place but not immovably?

I understand that the fins are normally just screwed on finger- tight. Any advice appreciated.

...William, there's no hard-and-fast rule about how many turns to open the needle in ANY model engine. Tanks vary; so do fuels and atmospheric conditions. And there are manufacturing variations that can affect the needle valve components too.

Whenever anyone buys a PAW from Eric Clutton, he ALWAYS hand-starts it before shipping it out -- and he records the needle setting that he used. He does that because PAW's are shipped with their needles removed from their spraybars, to eliminate damage from impacts "in transit".

Changing the fuel orifice positions as you did increases the suction effect. And using a slipstream-pressurized fuel tank also reduces the work the engine has to do to draw its fuel. Most of us who use the small PAW's in R/C and free flight models use straight suction fuel feed. As for me, I have no idea how many turns ANY of my engine needle valves are set at for flying. I adjust them as necessary and don't bother to count.

The way you state your second problem puzzles me! That implies that you WANT to back off the compression screw and leave the contra- piston stuck in the cylinder bore. That sort of thing is considered to be a malfunction -- and in fact I have a small diesel in my shop now with that problem, and haven't yet been able to devise an effective cure.

As long as the compression screw presses on the contrapiston, its "end thrust" loads the cylinder fin threads enough to prevent unscrewing. That is, unless the threads have become worn from several vibration loosenings... However, if you want to tighten down the fins really well, but without overstressing, clean off the oily external surfaces; heat up the fins well with a Monokote heat gun; then with leather gloves on, hand-tighten the fins onto the sleeve firmly while the parts are still hot. [JW]

Sam Brauer <  > wrote:

I've just finished a 1/2-size Request. The Request was an old- timer from the 1930s that has been kitted in 1/2 size by R/N models. It's available at Penn Valley Hobby Center for around $20.

I've used my now trusty combination of a Speed 280, Kontronik 10 controller, 5 x 2 Graupner prop (direct drive) and a rechargeable 3-cell lithium pack. Guidance is a pair of FMA S60 servos with a Sky Hooks and Rigging receiver. Finish is courtesy of the local Stop and Shop - Avery purple glue stick and Reynolds Crystal Clear colored wrap (rose and yellow). Flying weight is about 7 ounces. If I can con my buddy into taking some shots of it, I may try and post pictures on the E-zone or something.

The airplane flies superbly. It's very easy to fly, climbs out strongly (you shouldn't run down lithium batteries -- I fly for 15 minutes or so and call it quits) and has very little trim change when the power is cut. You can set the throttle at about 1/3rd and the airplane will just do lazy left-hand circles without any input.

At full bore, it's even mildly aerobatic -- loops are kind of tricky because I only put the elevator on one side, but it will sort of barrel roll. I think it's even easier to fly than my Comet Clipper in this size. Furthermore, the airplane is more rugged than the Clipper -- the wing has much more beef. I've given the Clipper away to a buddy, we'll have to put them up side by side to see which one has better hang time etc.

Either way, I still haven't seen an ARF which comes close in terms of flying ability. Balsa still flies better!

Sam Brauer Norwalk, Connecticut
