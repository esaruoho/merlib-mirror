Sam Brauer <  > reports:

I thought I'd update the gang on the results I've gotten from playing around with the Vasa 55 mm fan in a Flying Styro F-16. In a word: Success! This is the best-flying ducted fan jet I've seen without going to more expensive brushless motors.

As a memory jog, here are some statistics: two-channel taileron control -- no pushrods, mount the stabilizers directly on the servo arms. You must have end-point adjustments on your transmitter for this trick.

All-up weight is about 12.5-13.0 ounces. I'm using a Speed 300 (out of the box, no retiming), a 7-cell 600 AE nicad pack (the motor pulls too many amps for NiMH) and a Pixie 14 speed controller.

Much of the earlier problems with the jet were my own doing. I applied too much force trying to glue the wings on, and snapped the right wing. Gluing it back together, I induced a warp which caused a crash and so on.... This airplane does not look pretty at this point (not that it really ever did -- I don't enjoy working with foam) but it does fly. It also needed some fairly hefty cheater holes cut in to let the fan breathe.

Flying performance: the airplane takes a bit to get "on step", but then you can pull the nose back and let it climb. Like a jet, best rate of climb is well above the stall. Once you have some altitude, you can drop the nose and do a nice fast low pass, or even loop. (That's something most low-powered ducted fan jets have trouble with. I've done several. For instance, the Kyosho F-16 had difficulties with this maneuver.)

The flight speed of the Flying Styro F-16 is probably scale-like for a 50's jet -- still a little slow for an F-16 to my eyes, but it looks better than the Kyosho stuff or the previous Flying Styro airplanes. (A cautionary note: there is an older F-S F-16 that is often sold very cheaply- however, it doesn't fly unless you put a prop on it.)

Flight times are probably in the 3-4 minute range -- an improvement over the Blaster series of fans. Landing is pretty easy: just keep pulling the nose up when you get near the ground.

Problems: the airplane is not sturdy. On my last flying session, I caught a clump of grass with one of the tailerons, which took a big piece out. That was on a nice slow landing. too. I doubt that these airplanes will have a long life. My airplane's nose is getting pretty bashed as well.

Overall: for a quick and dirty (but not exactly cheap) entry into a ducted fan, this combination of a Vasa fan and this Flying Styro F-16 is not a bad way to go. It's probably the least expensive setup with reasonable performance.

For folks playing around with fans: there's a lot of design that's critical. Using an electric motor, you don't have power to burn; thus getting the most efficient fan will really help. I wouldn't bother with the Blaster stuff at this point; the Vasa is a far superior unit.

Also, the cheap motor and battery combination of a Speed 300 and a 7-cell 600 AE nicad pack is lightweight and powerful, and may well be the best combination for this fan -- price no object. Most of the other motors intended for this fan will require more cells, thus increasing weight. Since the fan is going into small airplanes, I wouldn't be surprised if this puts you behind the power curve.

Good luck with your fans -- they can be addictive. Now if somebody would just come out with a nice balsa kit.....

Sam Brauer, Stamford, Connecticut

...Frank, you're quite right about intake screens. I remove them from ALL my model engines, because they interfere FAR more with the inlet flow than most modelers realize. Early experimenters with ducted fan models discovered that covering over a "cheater hole" with screen (for better scale appearance) was equivalent in effect to covering the holes with sheet balsa. Screens restrict airflow almost that badly in model engine intakes. [JW]

Eric Clutton <  > contributed:

It seems to me that some of you ex-glow guys have not completely got over the habit and you are using too small a prop. This probably explains some of the cases of the comp 'backing off' because it's dat old debbil HIGH FREQUENCY vibration.

A compression lock will work of course, but I don't like them; and another method that often works is to replace the comp screw with an allen screw. The regular comp screws will act like a tuning fork at certain (usually high) rpm and the allen screw will cure this completely. If the contra-piston is loose you are out of luck, but the cast-iron and steel ones can often be tightened by placing them on a hard surface, holding a short steel bar in contact with it, then belting it with a 2-lb hammer! Crude, but effective if done right. ERIC CLUTTON
