Jim Scott <  > inquired:

I picked up a Sterling "Hawk" profile high-wing free flight, about 27-inch span, now powered by a .074 Cub. It has a formed sheet wing (like AJ's interceptor) and a two-piece stamped aluminum motor mount. It appears to have been designed for 020 or 049.

There are two hollow rivets in the fuselage just behind trailing edge of wing, and another at the right wing tip. What could have been the purpose of these? And, can anyone help with "vintage data" and with detail on what the front is supposed to look like -- a catalog picture, perhaps. I'd like to restore it and get it flying. Thanks.

...Jim, the rivets were to allow "tethered" flying of this designed-for-free-flight model. A length of fishing line from 10 to 25 feet long connected the airplane to a central weighted "pylon", usually 5 or 6 feet tall.

Tethered flight began with a ground takeoff from a smooth surface (e.g. a gym floor or paved parking lot). The centripetal effect of the line tension, plus running the line through the wingtip eyelet, prevented the model's flight altitude under power from exceeding ten feet or so.

After the engine quit, the model would glide to a landing like any free flight plane -- but covering much less territory. [JW]

Dereck Woodward <  > returns at last, after too long an absence:

Dave Segal reported on his .25-powered "Reflex" delta R/C model. I had what sounds like a similar craft years ago in England -- a .25-powered delta I did for a magazine kit review. It really had two major flight tricks -- going very fast (and it only had an OS 25FP), and going very slow. It could fly slower than any of the "old timer" R/C conversions in my club. You could have added 'rolls very fast' too, but slow and fast were really about it.

For takeoffs, it had no gear and was no sweat to handtoss; while bellyflop landings were more like 'stop, then land' than the usual way, even in calm.

The best way to slow down was to pull "up" too hard, when it would bleed speed like crazy, and not get going again until the nose went down -- descending nose-high on full power is an uncanny feeling!

Look at the front view of a delta head on, then compare it to the same held 20 or so degrees nose high. The latter case ain't going anywhere fast.

The only deltas with wheels I've seen much of were Peter Russell's designs, usually in the UK based RCM&E magazine. These had very long, stalky UC legs, to accommodate the high angles of attack met at takeoff and landing, without banging the back end on the ground -- rather like the dear old Avro Vulcan in a more manageable size!

If you want to see how fast it will shift, take the gear off and hand-launch the brute. Losing those big old do-nuts on wire stalks should speed things up. Make sure it isn't balanced nose- heavy -- that needs more up reflex on the elevons, resulting in more drag and less speed. For verticals, make sure she's really moving level, then pull smoothly to vertical in a big quarter loop, to avoid getting into the drag-ridden corner of the envelope.

Different tack -- If anyone has been thinking I'd dropped out (remote chance, I know) -- no such luck. We've had an overly interesting year of 'other stuff' and modelling has been restrained somewhat. However, I can report that my flirtation with big electrics was (A) fun and (B) is getting scaled back to where the majority of my models are now pretty much the same size, weight and performance as my beloved old .25 glow-powered ships.

Okay, I have a 48" span electric that is causing embarrassment in a somewhat diverse club by passing most 46 glow models; but they are smaller than I got to!

With the 'discovery' of the $50.00 Kyosho Endoplasma, Great Planes GD-600 'box and the CP2400 cell at under $4.00 a cell, we've now got a quiet and easy to own/operate "sub" for the .25- powered upper end small model; one that will cut your cleaning materials bill and ensure you never have to bow to club noise level checks!

Yours in modelling, Dereck Woodward

David Bonin <  > contributed:

Regarding thermal lift in quarries...

You may get very good lift in some areas of a quarry, but remember that the air going up has to be replaced by air coming in and down. Somewhere, probably on the shady or windward side, there is a large column of air with very negative lift.

This situation is very similar to that of full-scale aircraft flying through mountain valleys. The shadier side of the valley often has large downdrafts, while the sunnier side often has large updrafts. And in the vicinity of the peaks, there can be severe turbulence where these drafts mix with the prevailing winds.

If you fly in or around a quarry, please be very careful for loose or fallen rock, lest your hobby turn into more adventure than you expected. Dave Bonin

...Mike, most model diesels are amply flexible in their operation; and for sport flying it's not necessary to be absolutely precise in adjustments or fuel mixture. Eric Clutton once told me that if you can smell ether in your fuel, you have enough...

Diesel fuel doesn't deteriorate with age. It DOES lose ether content whenever you open the can, though. Solution: never open the can! I've been using a diesel fueling setup for years that keeps the can constantly closed.

What my setup amounts to is converting a one-quart paint thinner can top by adding a pair of soldered-on brass tubes. When not in use, these tubes are connected with a short length of heavy-wall Tygon tubing. For fueling, I pull the Tygon off one brass tube, and connect the loose end to my tank filler tube.

Gravity does the transfer. I used to employ a rubber bulb as a pressurizer, but that turned out to be more trouble than it was worth.

As for refilling the quart can: I have a similar converted can top on my gallon containers. Transfer from the gallon can to the quart is again by gravity. And when I use up the whole gallon can, I merely transfer my converted cap from the empty can to the new one -- using a few wraps of Teflon plumber's tape to provide an airtight seal.

Eric Clutton sells an alternative "additive" for diesel fuel called hexyl nitrate. But, as mentioned in my Engine Shop column in the current Model Aviation, exceeding a small amount of this does no good: 5/8 of a fluid ounce per quart of fuel is MAXIMUM. [JW]

Johnson 4046 motor (cost a buck at Electronic Goldmine); MP Jet 5:1 BB gearbox for Speed 280s (nice snug fit, although this gearbox is a bit noisier than the one on my Speed 300); 7-cell 720 mAh nickel metal hydride pack, Pixie 7 speed control, and an FMA Extreme 5 receiver.

I think I'm using a pair of the Maxx MX-30 servos -- I like them. A few bucks cheaper than HS-50s, but apparently more accurate than the GWS stuff -- and I think lower ampere draw.

On the front of the Super Sniffer is a Sonic Tronics 8 x 4 folder. It's kind of ugly, but it seems to work. All-up weight is about 12.5-13 ounces or so. The S-S does fly in some wind. I've had it out when it's blowing in the 8-10 mph range, but penetration is pretty marginal. It's certainly easy enough to fly to make a nice trainer -- heck, it's really guided a free flight! One of these days I'll have to see how I do with it at night.

Adam Bryant <  > adds:

Something that I use when making balsa parts is a product called "stencil adhesive", which you can find in your local craft store. You spray it on the back of your paper plan for the formers, ribs, whatever; then stick the plan onto your balsa and cut with an X-Acto knife. The great thing about this adhesive is that you can remove the paper plan and reuse it.

This stuff has about the same stick-um as a good Post-It note. You can re-use your plan/stencil several times before you need to reapply more adhesive. This product also works great for making stencils for painting aircraft. Now if I can just remember what I did with my can of this stuff. :) Adam

Red Scholefield <  > amplifies that:

Ni-Cd vs NiMH:

The NiMH cells that are available to us today from Sanyo/Panasonic may be treated essentially the same as Ni-Cds -- EXCEPT FOR PEAK CHARGING. You have to be careful here. Some chargers will accommodate them; others won't -- and end up severely overcharging them to the point of destruction.

In a given cell size NiMH has on the order of 20 to 30% more capacity than Ni-Cds. They have slightly higher internal resistance, but not enough to concern us in most radio use applications.

This higher internal resistance can be of concern in electric flight operations, where it is common practice to add an extra NiMH cell to compensate. They have a shorter cycle life in general, but again not enough to be of real concern. Most people run out of calendar life before cycle life in flying R/C -- meaning that the separator system in the battery (same for both types) fails (shorts) before the cells fade away in capacity delivery ability.

NiMH batteries are less tolerant of abuse (excessive high-rate discharge, excessive overcharge, vibration, cell reversal) than Ni-Cd. But again, it seldom becomes an issue if reasonable care is given in the installation and maintenance of the packs.

NiMH capacity falls off with cycling, whereas Ni-Cd stays pretty constant, seldom going below 80% before shorts develop. NiMH has a higher self-discharge rate (3-4% per day) than Ni-Cd (1%/day). The rate of self-discharge is very temperature-dependent.

NiMH batteries cost a bit more per Ah of capacity.
