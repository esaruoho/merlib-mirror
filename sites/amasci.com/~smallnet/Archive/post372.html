Carl Risteen < Risteen@nbnet.nb.ca > contributed:

I agree most emphatically with JW's quote from great test pilot Frank Courtney to the effect that "no airplane was ever designed with enough vertical tail area." I have cured evil handling in many R/C models by simply adding area to the vertical tail.

If you have a model that is a real dog, try simply taping a temporary cardboard extension to the rudder. The difference in controllability is frequently quite dramatic. (Reduce the rudder travel proportionally if you already had all the rudder authority you need.) Later, you can permanently enlarge the vertical tail.

On scale models, I would simply blow up the vertical tail, keeping the same shape, by as much as you think you can get away with before people notice. WW1 designs are probably the worst offenders -- which probably accounted for their terrible stall/spin accident rate.

One caveat -- This does not apply to free-flight models. Too much vertical tail will cause spiral instability. Non-scale free-flights should have smallish vertical tails -- the scale size is frequently large enough. I would increase the vertical tail size on a free- flight only if it tends to dutch roll. Joe Wagner's Dakota biplane is an example of a free-flight model with wonderful spiral stability. Built just a little tail-heavy, it dutch-rolled to incredible angles, but never crashed.

For Peter Havriluk: Comparing the G-Mark, Norvel, and Thunder Tiger .061's: the TT will likely swing the larger prop best. By larger, I mean a 7x3, such as the APC. The G-Mark is an older design (no longer made) based kind-of on the Cox engines.

Its weak point, in my opinion, is the ball-and-socket joint between the piston and con rod (like Cox). With Cox fuel and a Cox 6x3 prop I got 16,300 rpm. I did not try a 7x3 prop, but the instructions specified 6x3 and gave a speed range of 5,000 to 17,500 rpm. They also recommended 10-15% nitro. I doubt that this engine would handle a 7x3 prop very well -- but, then, it does have a very small venturi, which suggests that it is not a highly tuned design. As I remember, the G-Mark started easily and throttled well as long as one did not expect a low idle.

The Norvel .061 definitely likes high nitro, small props, and high speed. It has fairly aggressive port timing. Many have decided that it runs best with a smaller prop than a 6x3. Not really easy starting and friendly, particularly if you hand flip.

The TT .061 has milder port timing and handles a 7x3 prop pretty well. I got a speed range of 3,000-14,600 rpm with an APC 7x3 and Omega 10% fuel with 4 ounces of castor added per quart. Not particularly easy-starting and friendly when new, because they are set up with a really tight piston fit. This engine is easier to start as it wears in but I don't know how friendly it gets in the long run.

I think that a better choice than all of the above is the Norvel .074. It is larger and heavier, but has the mildest port timing of the bunch and a fairly small venturi for its size. Mine turns a 7x3 APC at 3,000-16,200 rpm, using Norvel 15% fuel. Starts easily and throttles well. Negatives, for me, are the very modest carb, which leaks air and does not allow steady engine speeds and the power falls off as the engine comes up to full operating temperature, apparently owing to increasing piston-cylinder gap.

I suggest that the best choice of all for swinging a larger prop and ease of operation, once you get used to it, is a small diesel. For example, the PAW 06 R/C. Regards, Gary Edson

Bob Stought < RStought@aol.com > contributed:

Q & A on Electric-Powered Models (SMALLnet #371)

Question: When should old Nicad batteries be replaced? (Bud Orren)

Answer: There is no simple answer; but in general, it is heat that degrades Nicad cell performance -- both the heat of charge and of discharge. Much depends on the cell design and size, as well as how hard a battery has been used. Nicad cells of the "R" type are the most rugged, and can (with normal use) last many years.

A big factor (for cells of the same type) is the increased cell capacity of newer cells. These allow longer motor run times at the same current (or power) levels. That alone can be reason to upgrade packs.

Question: Should I replace Nicad batteries with NiMH batteries?

Answer: The NiMH cells have much higher capacity than Nicad cells do, but a given size NiMH battery usually cannot deliver as much power as did the Nicad pack it replaces. So if the plane seems under-powered with a NiMH battery, you might consider adding 1 or 2 more cells in order to get about the same power as the smaller-capacity Nicad battery had.

Question: How do the power of the AstroFlight 020 and 035 ferrite motors compare to modern motors? (Gary Edson)

Answer: It was 30 years ago (January 1971) that the first practical electric-powered R/C model airplane was flown. That was in Pasadena, California, by Bob Boucher (of AstroFlight) and his twin brother Roland. Right off people wanted to know how to compare the power of electric motors to the gas engines of the time.

Thus Astro began naming his motors 020, 035, 05, 075, 10, etc. -- and has stuck with that system even though, due to the relatively higher flying weight and lower performance batteries & motors, the system was lacking in accuracy from the start.

In time another system developed which expressed motor sizes according to the designations used by Mabuchi, which is the largest model motor manufacturer in the world. Mabuchi's system is based upon motor design type and length.

Thus the Mabuchi RS-280 has a round can about 28 mm long; and an RS-380 is also round and about 38 mm long (24.5 mm to an inch).

The Germans, who used the same parts to construct motors of similar design and dimensions, replaced the RS, RE, SA, etc. of Mabuchi with terms such as "Speed", "Race", etc., and rounded off the dimensions. Thus the Mabuchi RS-280 and Graupner Speed 300 are of the same design and size -- and so are the Mabuchi RS-380 and the Graupner Speed 400.

As nearly as I can see, there is not a very much difference between the small ferrite magnet motors of today and those of years ago. More important is the better-quality small batteries we now have; and using the right Prop to get more power and duration than was possible until about 5 years ago.

Bob Stought

Bernard Cawley < abc_quiet_flight@operamail.com > wrote:

Gary Edson inquired about old Astro 020 and 035 ferrite motors (and the 020/035 belt reducer). He wondered if they are worth fooling with in comparison with today's motors.

The Astro ferrite 020, power wise, is similar to a weak Speed 400 of today. The 035 is like the 6V Speed 400 that is most commonly used. However, as you noted, they are intended to run on fewer cells. These motors were sold before the advent of any sort of practical BEC speed controls. In models powered by these you used a servo-operated on/off switch for motor control (if any) and carried a small receiver battery.

So, the low cell count winds were chosen to keep overall system weight down.

The 020 would draw about 10A on 4 cells, turning the Top Flite nylon 5 1/4 X 3 prop. The 035 on 5 cells drew 12 or more on that same prop. The motors were from Igarashi rather than Mabuchi (FWIW).

HiLine's Imp-30 and Elf-50 motors are intended to be direct replacements for the old Astro ferrites, but they have even hotter winds and want even fewer cells: the Imp-30 (and Troll-35) 3 cells, the Elf-50 4 cells. The Elf-50, in fact, is essentially a 4.8V Speed 400.

The belt drive works well, and in fact combining that belt drive with the 035 would give you something that on 6 or 7 cells would swing an 8-inch prop with good authority. You would need to experiment with props to choose one that drew no more than 10 or 11 A. You could also use the belt drive with a Speed 400 if you could find a source for an .080 inch pitch toothed belt pulley, and bushed the motor shaft out to 1/8 inch with some brass tubing. Such pulleys are available from Stock Drive Products, but I don't know if they sell small quantities any more. The pulleys and belt in the Astro reducer were from SDP.

I flew a Top Flite Schoolboy with a ferrite 020 for a number of years. It had three channels including the servo operated switch, using the smallest servos I could afford at the time -- Dunham micros from Ace (0.75 ounce each). The receiver was a one-ounce Royal Vanguard, and the radio battery was made by disassembling a "9V" GE NiCd and using four of the six 65 mAh cells you find inside. For a motor battery I used 4 800ARs or 900 Max cells from SR. All-up weight was 16 ounces!

The Schoolboy was capable of some three-channel aerobatics, but power off the rudder was almost useless. Made landings interesting.

I used to fly it over a vacant parking lot at lunch while at work and wore out two motors before the airframe was in such bad shape that it wasn't worth rebuilding. I still have the wing. I'm not at that building any more, and the Northwest Region of the FAA's building is sitting on that parking lot.

Dr. Michael Hawkins < mikeh@samart.co.th > advises:

Recent correspondence concerned suitable tubing for 1-ounce clunk tanks. I would ask, "Why bother ?" A 1-ounce clunk tank takes up a lot of room for its capacity, due to the thickness and curvature of the wall -- and the clunk is inefficient at that small size.

For small models I recommend a square or rectangular metal tank which can be soldered up from two pieces of tinplate; one for bottom and sides, and the other for top and ends. Drill for brass tubes before soldering together. If you can get at the carb, only two tubes are needed: one to the carb from the back bottom corner of the tank, and the other as a vent, to the muffler pressure fitting if present.

A recent question regarded the Norvel .061, which I regard as very "user-friendly". I have one in my Aquastar, flown most weekends and great fun. I recommend an APC 5.7 x 3 prop and 25% nitro fuel. As you only use 1.5 ounces in the metal tank, per flight this is not extravagant. I also recommend extending the spray strips on the model's hull chine up to the nose block.

At first I could not get the Norvel to idle below 12,000 rpm, but over the months this has come down to about 8,500 and I can get landings and a taxi back almost every flight (unless I cock it up!).

I fill the tank, blow through the vent tube to ensure fuel has reached the carburettor and away she goes every time.

Yours sincerely, Mike Hawkins (Dr.)

1/2A ball-and-socket connections that rattle are evidence of not enough castor oil in the fuel. I cannot emphasize this too much! The model fuel business is the most highly competitive area in the hobby industry, and the makers cut every corner possible to reduce costs. The latest ploy is to figure oil percentages by WEIGHT instead of volume. Oil is expensive -- cutting the actual amount used in fuel mixtures cuts manufacturing cost accordingly.

SOME glow fuel on the market today has as little as 12% oil by volume -- which is the way the percentage always used to be calculated. "Cleaner Burning!" say the ads. Translation: insufficient oil! Remember: castor oil does a LOT more in model engines than just keeping things slippery.

...As for extracting ether from cans of "Starter Fluid", there are at least two ways of minimizing loss. One involves freezing the can, then holding it upside down and bleeding off the pressurizing gas. Then you can punch two holes in the can and pour off the contents.

However, you cannot judge ether loss by the aroma! When I obtained my first model diesel -- a Drone .29 in 1947 -- I was able to buy the fuel ingredients at my local drug store. That was a long, narrow place with the cashier's counter up front and the pharmacy stores way in the back, at least forty feet from the counter. And whenever I bought ether, I could smell it in the front of the store within seconds of the druggist opening the ether can at the back.

Those fumes PERMEATE air instantly...! [JW]
