Larry Marshall <  > contributed:

Bud Orren asked: "When should old Nicad batteries be replaced?"

Bob Stought answered: "There is no simple answer; but in general, it is heat that degrades Nicad cell performance..."

I don't disagree with Bob on this, but I do think there is a simple answer to the question asked. The thing to do is to monitor the peak voltage and discharge time using a standard load, and record that in a logbook. You'll find that as the cells age the peak voltage will increase and the discharge time will decrease. The peak voltage rises because the internal resistance of the cell is going up (it's wearing out) and capacity goes down -- which is the important thing for us.

I don't know if Bud's question was relative to receiver batteries or power system batteries but I think the answers about when to get rid of them may be different depending on your view. I think it best to be conservative with receiver packs, and discard them with the first sight of decreased capacity. With power system batteries, however, it becomes a more personal decision and depends on whether you're still getting satisfaction from flying with the pack. A flight that's a bit shorter doesn't necessarily mean you're not having fun.

Bob also said, "... NiMH cells have much higher capacity than Nicad cells do, but a given size NiMH battery usually cannot deliver as much power as did the Nicad pack it replaces."

Here I do have to disagree with Bob a bit, though only in semantics. NiMH cells will deliver the same "power", they'll just do it over a longer time and at a lower voltage. With our model airplanes, volts and propeller rpm are directly related. I'm sure that's what he means as his solution of adding an extra cell (to increase the voltage) is consistent with this.

"As nearly as I can see, there is not a very much difference between the small ferrite magnet motors of today and those of years ago."

Except that the question was "old Astro ferrite 020 and 035 motors" vs "modern motors." The Astro 020 of today is a rare-earth magnet, brushless motor and will produce far more torque and rpm (determines size of prop and rpm it'll spin) than its predecessors. Again, I'm sure Bob knows this but it's unclear what "modern motors" Bud was asking about.

Cheers --- Larry

Gary Seckel <  > sent:

To respond to Glenn's concern for getting ether out of Starting Fluid spray cans. I only just spray the ether into the inside edge of a mason jar type container. The ether wasted amounts to very little. If you measure the ether sprayed into a container, you will find more fluid than advertised as the net weight.

This is due, in my opinion, to overfilling by the factory to insure full net weight. The appearance of large ether loss in spraying out the contents seems like an illusion. I never bleed off the propellant anymore. I usually mix fuel right before I fly. It is quick to do so, and I am already outside.

Roy Clough <  > wrote:

Discussion of small fuel tanks noted. Several years back, Larry Renger reminded me of the use of small balloons for lightweight fuel tanks that would feed in any position. I've been using them for practically all my small tankless engines. I made up a nylon plug, 1/2" diameter x 1/2" long, with a single hole in the middle.

A fuel line, cut with a sharp taper to be poked though an undersize hole, can be pulled through, and seals perfectly. No clunk needed, just cut a vee in the end of the fuel tubing. I double back the neck of the balloon to keep the over-all length as short as possible and bind it to the nylon plug with a small rubber band.

I fuel-proof the tank compartment with a coat of Elmer's Glue All, and make the tank accessible with a panel or trapdoor, to be opened for checking its filling through the single fuel line by removing it from the engine. Easy, because at the end of the flight the balloon will be sucked flat.

Never had a feed failure, and alcohol model fuel is kind to the balloon--I have one tank in use for a year now (but cannot speak for diesel fuel).

Now if Larry sees this, maybe he'll share with us that double- walled pressurized balloon tank arrangement he told me about. I never tried it, because the single balloon satisfied all my needs -- but pressure might be a good idea for larger-capacity balloons.

Roy C.

Dave Robelen <  > might know the answer to that; but here addresses another topic:

I've noticed the recent comments about the throttle characteristics of the BigMig .061. I too was disappointed with the early results, where the minimum idle was much too high, and the settings were tricky.

The following are things I changed which improved the situation.

1- Swap to a Cox plug (screws right in); either the high-compression Tee Dee head or one from the hot reed valve engines. Adjust gaskets for best running. (I use 15% Norvel fuel). This cleared up the misfiring and touchy adjustments.

2- I made a separate needle valve that I could fit in the incoming feed line. This became the control for full throttle, and the original valve controls the low and mid range.

3- I also used some "Bondo" body putty to fill the little notch in the carburetor throat just above the barrel.

With these modifications my engines will idle at 4500-5000 on a Master Airscrew 6-4 and have great midrange response. With regard to ease of handling, the first cold start is kind of annoying with the ceramic version, but from then on they are sweet and powerful little engines.

Regards, Dave Robelen

Bob Stought <  > advises:

Being new to electric power can be bewildering, and it took me a long time to get a handle on it. My advice is to proceed as follows;

1. Determine the shortest acceptable flying time for your plane. If it is an R/C type, that probably ought not be less than 3 minutes flying time at full power.

2. Determine the maximum current based on the minimum flight time for the battery you intend to use. Here's how to do that.

(a) Convert the battery mAh (milliAmpere-hours) to Ah (Amp-hrs). To do this, divide the mAh by 1000.

(b) Convert battery Ah to Ampere-Minutes (Am). To do this, multiply Ah times 60.

(c) Divide Ampere-Minutes (Am) by minimum flying time in minutes. The result is the maximum current in Amps that you can use.

3. Install a fuse rated at about this maximum current level (or slightly above it) in one of the motor leads.

4. Run the motor with a trial propellor with speed control set at full power.

(a) If the fuse burns out, replace it and switch to a smaller- diameter propeller.

(b) If after about two minutes of motor run time the motor is not warm to the touch, try again using a larger diameter propeller.

Example: You have a 7-cell 600 mAh battery and a Speed-400 motor. The minimum motor run at maximum current is to be 3 minutes. What is the maximum current and fuse size to use?

Solution: if 600 mAh is divided by 1000 the result is 0.6 Ah. The Am rating of the battery is 0.6 times 60, or 36 Am. The maximum current is 36 Am divided by 3 minutes, which is about 12 Amps. Therefore install a 15 Amp fuse in the motor lead before doing motor test runs with different size Props.

If the motor test results in a large, slow turning prop that produces low thrust (i.e. plane not being pulled very hard), the voltage may be too low for that motor. Try replacing the battery with one that has more cells. Then replace the large Prop with a smaller one and start the test procedures all over again.

Hope this is of use. Bob Stought

Gene Nightengale <  > submitted:

I have been using 35mm film canisters for 1/2A fuel tanks. I drill holes smaller than my fuel line. Cut a point on the end of the line to get it started in the hole then pull it through. The lid fits very tight. The tank is leak proof and quick and easy to make.

I direct the vent line forward and plug the filler line for tank pressure and more consistent engine runs. I am using this tank on ultra-light foam gliders that I converted to power and R/C.

I have one with a Cox .049 and another with a Norvel .061 with throttle. They both fly good, plenty of power and they loop and spin really nice. I also have a foam F-16 glider that I converted, and I use surgical tubing to make a pressure tank that works better than a clunk tank for aerobatics. The F-16 flies good with a Cox Tee Dee .049. It is very maneuverable, fast rolls, glides like a brick, lots of fun. Fun -- that's what it's all about.

I would rather fly than build, so I like the foamies and ARF's. I like the small models because they are easy to transport, and they are assembled and ready to fly when I get to the field. When I auger one in, most of the time there is no damage. I just dust it off and fly it again. If it is damaged, it is minor and I always have a backup with me anyway.

Gene Nightengale
