Patrick LeRay < PLeray@aol.com > asserts with verve:

Small-istas, Mike Gillihan made several good points in his last message, regarding power-to-size in models. Mike and I met at the last Maumelle meet, and to our pleasant surprise, found that we'd both designed and built modest little biplanes of about the same size and with the same powerplant. We had the best time flying them around more or less in formation (loosely interpreted).

Kindred spirits, birds-of-a, etc. The underlying idea of both designs being the mathematical term "elegance", i.e.: Use just enough whatever to solve the problem: no more, no less.

An over-powered, overweight airplane is, to my mind, a creation of the devil, fraught with anxiety and complication. I have enough of that in my life already.

Mike's described some points of his design, so here are few of mine, to show how two approaches can yield similar results.

Mine's a biplane of 36" span with 3 sqft of wing -- as opposed to his 2.9 sqft. His design has a thin, generic flat bottom airfoil, whilst mine has a thick, very slightly undercambered one. As a result, the unthrottled Norvel .061 in mine, at optimum needle, spins a 7x3 wood prop with exactly enough grunt to fly the critter in a perfect rendition of "scale", while still putting out enough "umph" to not bog down in loops, hammerheads, 90-degree banked turns, etc.

Mike gets to play with a throttle, while I don't have to deal with one. My landings are deadstick, but with the chosen airfoil, it can float from waaaay out in the field back to my feet.

Mike's point was that often modellers overpower because they've been told it's "better", or for one reason or another. I also have an old-timer look-alike with 54" span at 3.3 sqft pulled by a TT .07, and another biplane of 3.95 sqft with an Enya .09. On each, their flight is calm, predictable, and dare I say it, FUN; without anxiety.

Granted, it does take a little bit of mental exercise to gauge your requirements and options, and learning to build with "just enough" material, but therein lies the potential for Joy, (or further chin-scratching, and yes, I've had some horrid flops.). Nothing ventured, nothing gained, etc.

One of the things I really enjoy about wearing the cloak of "Smallness", is having the others in the local club walk over as I'm unpacking, and cluck their tongues over the "little-bitty" engines, and how I fuel them with a syringe! I love to hold up a half-empty pint of fuel and say loudly, "Damn, I'm gonna have to buy another quart of fuel this year!", while they're pumping a pint per flight into their .60 size behemoths. Giggle, snicker.

I realize I've been preaching to the converted here, so ...carry on, Troopers. --- Patrick LeRay

Glenn Flynn < g-flynn@charter.net > submitted:

To continue the recent discussions regarding throttling small engines, and especially to address the purpose of sub-piston induction:

According to my understanding of the dynamics of 2-stroke cycle engines, the only purpose of sub-piston induction (i.e. crankcase opened to atmosphere below the skirt of the piston at TDC) is to increase power and RPM. This is achieved by the induction of a cooler positive atmospheric pressure differential into the crankcase, which is at that point negative, thereby packing a more dense fuel/air mixture to be delivered by the bypass ports into the cylinder. (Sort of a cheap Rootes supercharger effect.) This was first utilized in the Atom engine designed by Ray Arden.

With the addition of a muffler and the attendant back pressure created, part of the exhaust is recycled back through the crankcase, via the sub-piston opening, thereby diluting the incoming mixture density and reducing both RPM and power in most cases.

The byproduct of a muffler can be better idling and transition to high speed.

Regards, Glenn

...Glenn, I was too "otherwise occupied" to respond to this particular topic when it came up earlier. I'll try to cover it now, because misunderstandings seem common in this area.

The amount of power that any internal combustion engine can develop is limited by how much air it can breathe. All that sub-piston induction does is allow more air to enter the crankcase of a 2-stroke engine than the main intake passage permits.

In model engines, the first people to explore the advantages of sub-piston air induction (we called it "free-porting" in the Good Old Days) were the race car crowd. They showed how extra power (and speed) could be achieved by one or two small holes drilled through the cylinder sleeve.

The first "factory standard" engine with "free ports" was the Bunch "Speedway" .45 of 1940. This car engine (and its marine counterpart, the Mighty Marine) first came out in 1938, without free porting. The change was made to both in 1940.

Around the same time car racers also customized early Super Cyclone .647's by adding drilled-hole "free ports". The results were good enough so that Mel Anderson made "free-porting" a standard feature on all the Super Cykes from 1940 on.

The 1940 Atom did NOT use free porting. It DID feature what Ray Arden called "valve-in-piston" induction. In that ingenious little (.097) engine, the fresh fuel-air charge passed THROUGH the 2-piece piston rather than around it via a bypass.

(The Atom also used no needle valve; merely a variable air throttle. It had its quirks -- but many of us got to love that unique little "gas engine". It's being made again today!)

The reason that most Cox engines featured sub-piston air induction is that their reed valves don't open very far. The inlet fuel-air mixture has to flow in a tortuous, restrictive path to get from the fuel orifice to the case interior. That limits air flow -- so sub-piston induction was added to admit a little more combustion air to the case. (Remember that prior to the introduction of the Tee Dees, all Cox model engines -- except the RR-1 -- were reed-valvers.)

Anyway, the sole purpose of sub-piston induction is to let more air enter the crankcase of a 2-stroker.

Now, intake throttling restricts airflow into the case. As the throttle is closed, less air can enter on each piston upstroke. That's why the power (and rpm) decrease.

But sub-piston air induction will INCREASE on a throttled engine, because there's a higher "crankcase vacuum" at part- throttle settings. And that's what can interfere with the throttle response. The mixture becomes leaner as the amount of sub- piston air flow increases.

Adding a muffler compensates for this, because the muffler acts as a sort of reservoir for the exhaust gases. Those get sucked back into the engine case when the piston approaches TDC, instead of fresh oxygen-containing air. Yes, the fuel-air mixture IS still affected -- but not so much as it is without a muffler. [JW]

Paul Lund < luftbuggy@yahoo.com > inquires:

A recent posting mentioned using tubing to steer exhaust from a diesel to where it's less likely to mess up a model. I have several PAWs, and it seems like that's a great idea for scale models, since scale exhaust pipes could be used.

My question is, what kind of tubing should be used to be unaffected by heat, castor oil, etc.? I'm not too concerned about a bit of power loss -- not really into high performance anyway.

Thanks. Paul Lund

...Paul, I use thin-wall K&S aluminum tubing for extending the exhausts of some of my PAW's. The OD is the same as that of the muffler outlet, and I make the junction with a short piece of heavy-gage vinyl plastic tubing, of the sort used in home aquariums. (Wal Mart carries this.) Yes, after a while the vinyl loses elasticity; when it does, I cut it off and replace it with a new piece.

I've found that aluminum tubing is much lighter than plastic, and can be bent as required to fit various model installations if the work is done slowly and gently -- preferably with a "stuffer" inside to prevent buckling. (I've used insulated heavy-gage electrical wire for this purpose.) [JW]

...Ed, Superior Balsa is owned and operated by Sal Taibi and his son Mike, both of them lifetime modelers. (Sal is over 80 now.) They do excellent work. So does Lone Star's Riley Wooten -- another guy who's been active in modeling all his life.

But balsa is a "natural product", and varies. The best way to buy the kind of wood you want by mail is to tell the vendor (Superior or Lone Star or AeroDyne) EXACTLY what you want. Describe the wood you need as "light", "medium", "stiff", or whatever qualities you'd search for when hunting wood for your project at a "brick & mortar" hobby shop. [JW]
