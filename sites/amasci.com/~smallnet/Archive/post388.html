...Mike, it makes sharp-edged propellers a little safer if you radius the leading edges of their blades. I do that by scraping; just enough to remove all of the "seam line".

The sharp leading edge is a by-product of the plastic molding process, and not an intentional feature.

However, a sharp TRAILING edge definitely adds efficiency. Leave that unmodified, but round off the leading edges. That improves thrust, besides making propeller cuts SLIGHTLY less of a danger.

By the way, with plastic props larger than 6" diameter, I've formed the habit of ALWAYS using a leather "work glove" on my prop-flipping hand. Those don't cost much -- and I think I've saved their price already in decreased Band-Aid use. [JW]

Larry Renger < lrenger@NAMKUNGPROMO.com > returns with:

Here's a new idea for a pressurized balloon-type fuel tank. Make a wood, rubber, or plastic plug with two holes to pass brass tubing. The plug should be about 1/2" diameter and around 1/4" thick.

One tube stops flush with the inner surface. The other goes through, and has fuel hose attached on the inside. (If using a wood plug, it should be epoxy-coated to keep it from getting oil-soaked, and also to prevent pressure leakage.)

Tie a "helium-quality" balloon on the inner hose to be your fuel tank; then slip all that into another balloon and tie that to the plug. The outer balloon will take and apply pressure to the inner balloon. My guess is that the whole thing should fit into an epoxy-coated chamber in your model, so that the whole assembly is constrained; but for muffler pressure that probably isn't necessary.

Every once in a while, it WILL be necessary to suck the excess oil out from between the balloons.

Jim Porter < airporter@worldnet.att.net > advises:

In response to Milton Dickey's dilemma on transporting models. Rather than going with the PVC pipe racks, which can be limited in their utility, try making a removable shelf for the back of your van. With small planes, perhaps even two shelves.

Several years ago, when flying buddy Ed Harris and I started going to Lake Wilson for slope soaring, we discovered that we wanted to take a lot of planes and were having just the problems Milton mentioned. I realize this will relate more to sailplanes than power planes, but I think the approach is still relevant.

Our first step was to make a number of boxes out of corrugated or liteply for individual, or groups of similar, models.

Second, the shelf that covers about 2/3 of the back of my Suburban splits the available height in half, allowing us access to boxes without having to move two or three layers.

Third, a pair of crossbars suspended from the coat hooks, or off risers on the ledges around the back space, allow you to store wings above the rest of the space.

There is still space in front of the rack but behind the front seat, for flight boxes, luggage, coolers, and awkward-shaped planes.

Last trip to Lake Wilson, Ed and I managed 35 planes.

Jim Porter Bettendorf, Iowa USA

Dr. Mike Blott < DRBLOTT@aol.com > amplifies:

Mabuchi N-20 motors are the same size as the B2 bomber motors. (5 grams) They generally come in two flavors: "PA" = higher voltage, and "PN" = lower voltage.

The "PN" are what we generally use, and over-voltage them. The PN motors give the same thrust as the B2 motors, but the PN get hotter. It appears that the B2 motors may be a special order of WATTAGE. I have inquired to them for the specifications, but have not received them yet.

Mabuchi places a little italic M on the end bell. The B2 motors I have seen do not have the little M.

...Thanks, Mike! Now I will answer the question about "severely restricted Cox .010's".

When the Cox .010 first came out, a lot of us were extremely disappointed with its tiny propeller and incredible (for that time) rpm output. We wanted to use the little engine for flying small free flight models, using larger props than that microscopic plastic thing that came with the engine. (One of those almost cost me an eye.)

However, a stock Cox .010 won't run reliably on a propeller much larger than 3 inches. But some ingenious southern California modelers (Bill Hannan for one) discovered that wrapping the .010's lower cylinder tightly with brass shim stock, then drilling a single 1/16" diameter hole through that for the exhaust opening, made the engine nicely docile.

Another flyer I knew found that a coil spring (in a size that he'd discovered by blind luck) could be worked around an .010 cylinder to cover the exhaust ports. Enough of a "leakage path" existed between the tightly-fitting coils to permit the engine to run -- and spin a 6-inch prop well enough to fly a small semi-scale free flight model.

It's surprising how small an exhaust opening is required for model engines at non-screeching rpm.

For example: I recently ran-in a special modified-for-control-line -stunt engine of Larry Renger's creation. After a 5-minute run I wanted to stop it, so it could cool off for another period of "dimensional stabilization".

I shut off the muffler outlet (with a leather-gloved finger) -- but the engine kept right on running. Slower -- but still smooth and steady. The outlet provided by the "pressure tap fitting" (not connected to the tank -- I seldom use muffler pressure) was all that engine needed.

An even more surprising instance happened some years ago while I was breaking in a new PAW .19 diesel. Again I wanted to stop the motor, and sealed off the muffler exit with a finger.

(Diesels run much cooler than glow -- at least my PAW's do.)

Rpm dropped noticeably -- but the engine kept running. And KEPT running; I wanted to see how long that motor could keep it up with NO EXIT for its exhaust.

My youngest daughter was standing by while I did this; and she said that the engine sound just about vanished when I sealed off the muffler outlet.

Now THAT's what I call "severely restricted" ! [JW]

...Dal, I can't tell you where to find servo gears, but I do have a couple of possibly helpful tips. Years ago when Bill Cannon first introduced his Ultra Micro servos, I visited him to get data for a report I wrote for Model Airplane News.

Bill's new servos were just about the same size as the "new" Pico-size servos. Bill told me that they required special care and treatment. First, he said to NEVER rotate the output wheel manually. NEVER !!!

Bill said that was a sure way to overstress the tiny gear teeth.

Bill Cannon also "ran in" every Ultra-Micro servo he sold. He had a "bank" of servo output cables on a device that enabled him to "cycle" several servos at a time. He put a few hundred full-motion cycles on each servo before he would ship it out.

There were two reasons for this "break-in" process. One was to make sure that everything worked really RIGHT; the other was to mate the feedback pot brushes to the resistance element. Bad contact there can drive the servo hard against its stop in one direction... [JW]

...Here's an idea that might spark some "creative interest". This isn't new -- but may be unfamiliar to some of us: designing scale models of "real airplanes" that never existed.

The earliest I encountered this approach to model airplanes was in the 1930's, when the publication that later became "Air Trails" was a pulp fiction aviation magazine. The "Bill Barnes" tales in each issue were always lavishly illustrated (by Frank Tinsley) with some of the sleekest airplane designs anyone ever created.

They were all imaginary, of course -- but they looked more real than some of the craft that were then traversing America's skies.

I particularly liked the biplanes that the "bad guys" flew, and made solid models of those, working "by eye". (Dagnabbit, I'll bet I could design a NEAT R/C model of one of those!)

During the War Years a few flying model designs appeared in model magazines that looked like "real airplanes" but weren't. One that I still remember was a sexy biplane called "Mayfly", which included retractable landing gear operated with the mechanisms taken from a pair of mechanical pencils...

Two CO2-powered model projects I'm now working on are "pseudo-scale". I think of them as being scale models of "real airplanes" that I WOULD have designed, had I been an aeronautical engineer in the 1930's... [JW]
