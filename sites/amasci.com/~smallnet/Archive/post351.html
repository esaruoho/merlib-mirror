Greg Stewart <  > contributed:

In response to Sandy Ross who'd like to electrify an Ace Whizard kit and make it rudder-only...

Source: Electric, motors
Electrify - Good Idea! Use a Kyosho AP-29, Graupner Speed 480, Multiplex Permax 480 or 450, all better if geared about 2:1. 7-8 cells, 600AE cells to 800AR cells. Check Hobby Lobby, Dymond RC, Multiplex USA, Maxx Products, and New Creations RC as sources.

Consider a Castle Creations or Kontroniks speed controller (see below). A lot of people have good luck with Jeti controllers, but I'm not a fan of them.

Build it light... replace fuselage sides and tail wood as needed if supplied wood is over 8-pound stock... with a geared setup, a reasonably light 3-channel radio system, and a 7-cell pack your Whizard should weigh about 28-30 ounces. You'd have a good flier with the above recommendations.

In fact, you've now got me thinking about the Ace Pacer kit in my basement...

Rudder-Only - Bad Idea! You will appreciate throttle and elevator for your first RC plane... being able to throttle back or shut off the motor will make the difference between a single flight with a crash and a destroyed plane and power unit, and a single flight with a bad landing that may damage the plane, but be quickly repairable to let you get back up in the air again soon.

If radio gear is an issue, a 3-channel HiTec radio can be had for under $100 and is a very good performer. Greg

Fred Reese <  > has a new trick to teach:

Regarding the Thunder Tiger .07. The engine needs to be wet to start. I prime the engine by placing a finger over the exhaust on the muffler and turning the engine. If using an electric starter, just cover the exhaust for a second while turning and it will fire instantly.

I had a terrible time starting the motor when new until I tried this. Actually, I could not get it started until I primed it this way. The pressure in the muffler pushes fuel into the engine. Mine will hand start easily now, it just needs to be WET.

Bill Lee <  > wrote on this topic:

One reason that a drilled and tapped C*x 1/2A head usually is inferior to the original is the fact that there are threads in there! Even if the glow plug fills those flush with the interior of the head, there is significant disruption to the combustion by the boundary between the head and the plug where the threads exist.

There is a significant amount of combustion actually taking place IN THE THREADS since the washer that seals the combustion chamber is "up top". Have you ever taken a plug out of an engine after extended running and found the threads caked with varnish and carbon? Where do you think it's coming from?

I have run tests in years past regarding just this phenomenon, and it is definitely there. When Henry Nelson first came out with his glow plugs, he sent me three heads to test on a Nelson 15.

One was his conventional head for a regular 1/4-32 plug. The second was the same head but with the threads cut so tight at the bottom that the last couple of turns when inserting the plug HAD to be with the plug wrench. The third was the same head, with his plug that seals at the interface with the combustion chamber.

There was 1,000 rpm increase between the first two heads, and an ADDITIONAL 1,000 from the second to the third!

The C*x engines (and almost EVERY competitive FAI speed engine) uses the integral plug design exactly for that reason. And it is also why the Nelson plug, which seals at the face of the plug instead of "up-top" is so vastly superior in every application.

Several conversion heads for the C*x engines are becoming available which use the Nelson plug. I recommend them. At $9 a pop for C*x heads, a suitable alternative at a cheaper price is not only needed but welcome. (Is C*x TRYING to discourage folks from using their engines? $9 per plug is a GOOD way to do it! )

Regards, Bill Lee

Sam Brauer <  > submitted:

In the last posting there was a request for information on the Lanier 1/2A Shrike. My information is second-hand -- I've seen one fly at a fly-in. The airplane is very fast, very maneuverable, and is not for the faint of heart.

It is reasonably rugged, but you had better be on your toes to fly the thing -- it has a roll rate that's just unbelievable. I say this after having flown the House of Balsa 1/2A ships with a Kustom Kraftsmanship Tee Dee 051 -- the Shrike is faster and twitchier. I watched Mike Ramsey (of Flying Models) wring it out -- he can fly....

The plane is also rugged. The first flight attempt went in due to the CG being too far back. No damage -- Mike moved the batteries and tried again. I think there was a Norvel 061 motor in the airplane -- it's not exactly quiet.

I would highly recommend a throttle -- the ability to slow it down and take a breather would help.

For anybody contemplating electrifying an airplane in the 15-20 ounce category, I'll recommend the S-300 by Mike's Tiny Models. The more I fly this motor in my Megowcoupe, the happier I am with it. I'm flying the 5:1 ratio gearbox with an 8 x 3.8 slow flyer propeller on an 8-cell, 350 mAh nickel metal hydride pack.

All-up weight of the motor system and batteries is less than 6 ounces. I'll bet it'd do fine in a Guillow Corsair, although you'll need a higher-pitched prop. In the Megowcoupe the motor is loafing, and I'm getting flights over 10 minutes with throttle management.

Surprisingly, the battery pack seems to have significantly more capacity than rated. After my last flight, the pack took 400 mAH and had not discharged below 10.5 volts -- meaning I still had plenty of power left. I'm charging the pack at 0.8 AH using an Astro 110D charger. I suspect that many people have been slow- charging nickel metal hydride cells, which is why they don't perform well.

Sam Brauer, Norwalk, Connecticut

Mike Goliber <  > also answers Craig Beare's question about the Lanier 1/2A Shrike:

I've built 5 modified versions of the Shrike 400 from Flying Models plans, for 2 and 3 channels with BigMig .061's. I don't know how the plans differ from the kit, but they all flew extremely well -- but get small fast, and are quick on control input.

Large "invasion stripes" on the wing bottom help greatly to keep proper orientation, and throttle helps to control the adrenaline; but the glide on the two-channel version is fine. The biggest change I did was to replace the main balsa spar with an aluminum arrow shaft or carbon fiber rod. Rough landings and high-G maneuvers are tough on the wing/fuselage joint. Just be sure that your flying skills aren't too rusty before you fly the Shrike :)

Mike
