Roy Clough <  > concurs:

Clank tanks, well and kindly remembered. I built one into a 35mm translucent film can for my Aileron Trainer, (Model Aviation, December 1996) still flying nicely. To get the thru-pass I used a drilled-out brass 8-32 screw with thin washers on both sides to compress the plastic can to a leakproof fit.

The fuel tube was soldered in, otherwise the same as recounted by Byron Lechler. The vent was silicone tubing through a topside hole running to the bottom wall as per control-line practice. Quite elaborate, but has never leaked or caused any other problems with the Cox .09 Medallion attached to it. Flown again as recently as a month ago.

My discovery for the year is that Goop cement -- which I have been using to hold servos for several years past -- can be diluted to any desired consistency with hardware store lacquer thinner. This stuff sticks everything I've tried with it -- just consider that the lacquer diluent may not be kind to everything it touches.

Now I wish I had back all those old tubes of Goop I discarded after they hardened solid when only half-empty. This stuff is probably the single most useful adhesive-for-everything on my bench. Just be patient with diluting the stuff -- it took about a week for my first batch to unjell, and should be used with good ventilation -- just like lacquer. Roy C.

Ron Ferrer <  > asserts:

In response to Gary Edson's review of the new Norvel .074; I applaud the fact that he got hard anodizing's description right; I used to do it for a living for the Navy before I got into the hobby shop business....

Anyway, we have been running Norvel .074's in our club for a few months now; in fact you can see one in action low and inverted at 1/4 throttle on my website at < http://www.creative-net.net/~armadilo/ > and it throttled up just great and pulled right out of the maneuver, as always. The engine was a little burbly for the first 1/2 hour of run time, though.

It has about 3 or 4 hours of run time now, does great on 7-3 prop, 35% nitro. The muffler has never come loose. I personally run one of the new 061 Revlites in my 1/2A Fokker D-VIII, inverted; has run great. I don't know about the timing and such, but it seems to have a lot more power than my old AME!

I guess it is more thrust, as the AME liked a 5.5/4.5 trimmed to 4.5 inch diameter. It went fast on my pylon stuff, but not much vertical! The Fokker takes off regular grass (you can see the grass on my page; I R.O.G.'ed it that day several times) but it noses over on landings almost every time!

The engine has only bogged once in the air, and that is when I set it too lean. Of course, that was at the Mint Julep, and it cost me half a flight! Oh well, that's competition!

The engine is shoehorned against the firewall, with a hole through the firewall for the rear of the muffler, and an exhaust extension out through the bottom of the fuselage behind the firewall. By the way, the muffler has a seal now, and an improved spring with cast-in bosses to retain the spring on the crankcase. With the extension, it actually is clean! Only gets oily on bottom of the plane. What an improvement! I have oil soaked two fuselages on my combat planes with the old ones!

The new muffler can't be mounted backward, though; make a note of that, or you too will be cutting holes in your firewall! About the piston/rod/wristpin being one assembly: it would be convenient to be able to replace individual components, but a staked pin is secure.

The hard anodizing is the hardness of sapphire (basically what it is), second only to diamond. If you replace the piston, it will wear in. The cylinder itself shouldn't wear enough to affect it. If you have enough time on one of these to need a new cylinder, you should probably go ahead and spend another $50 and get a new motor!

About hard coat; we used to run test panels to check the process; the lab used to set a test panel up, and spin abrasive on it until the coating wore through. That took several hours; you can read the Military Specification on hard anodizing to find out exactly, but it was pretty impressive to me how it held up. It does great on a 24-hour salt spray bath, too (Navy stuff).

About the carburetors; I was a bit dismayed about the single needle on the BigMig 15, having always preferred the 2-needle type like the AME has. But of the 10 or so Norvels we are flying around here, they all run great with no adjustment. I just put one in a Lazy Bee with an 8-3 prop, 15% nitro; it ran and idled out of the box!

On the 061, I was just glad it had an R/C carb at all! I flew Tee Dees for years, and even with the Tarno Carb (yeah, I still got one) it wouldn't perform near as well as any Norvel.

And almost every time I see a new Norvel, something is better. It seems Ed Stevens gets a lot of feedback, and actually listens! That counts for a lot. Anyway, that's my 2 cents worth, and it is just my experiences with these engines. Ron Ferrer

Dereck Woodward <  > contributed:

Well, the Lazy Bee continues. It's getting a load of "Fikes Tweaks", as in a nicad tray up under the wing, a cowled-in motor, torque rod Landing Gear and so forth. Necessary ? No - but after eight Bee variants, I gotta take a break!

My "Kolibri" indoor is back in the air. This is the poor country cousin to the Stubenfliege, but flies good. I got my taste for "Slowfly" when a German buddy brought his Stub' over on vacation just before Slowfly got to the US. Horst's was the fully-loaded ready- made, with wood carrying case and custom to the model Becker radio - servos and RX in a tiny 'brick'.

Building my own Kolibri was kind of like learning to drive on a Rolls Royce, then finding out all you could afford to drive was something heavy, clunky and not too good on handling - say, a Ford Expedition ;-^).

That old raggy Kolibri is now over three years old. I'd changed the motor/box when the original packed up, for an epicyclic unit I had from someplace, and needed a bigger prop. This has somehow got to be a permanent fixture on the propshaft and vibrated like crazy -- you could see the stabilizers flapping.

Well, I had a sudden dose of thinking - couldn't get the prop off to balance it, so stick a square of Scotch tape on the back of a blade, run it up. Oops - worse, move the tape to the other blade. Much better, add a slightly larger piece of tape, vibes are gone. Doing something simple beats thinking about doing something complex every time!

Rob Wesch wrote, "I recently completed a Stubenfliege electric plane made from carbon fiber rods. When I got ready to cover it with the mylar film, I thought I had better check on a good adhesive. I was told Balsarite would work fine. However, I cannot get any- thing but a very light adhesion -- not enough to secure the film.

"When trying to give it just a little more heat, the carbon rod broke. I can fix that, but need some ideas on what really works to stick the mylar (no adhesive on it) to the carbon rods."

Anyway, my Kolibri covering's still on after three years. Try this, Rob: Do not apply heating irons to the CF - it breaks. Okay, Rob, you know that already -- that information's for the rest. You don't need scientific explanations -- especially from me, don't have any -- they just break.

Get an office glue stick - the permanent sort. UHU is one brand name; I'd go with that rather than the supplier's generic brand. Cut out a piece of mylar a little larger than the component to be covered. Clean your work area and tape the mylar down flat, with the side to be stuck to the model upwards. Use lots of little tabs of masking tape; pull the mylar out until it is all under slight tension and evenly held.

Now - rub that gluestick around the outside edge of the wing or stabilizer - practice on the stabilizers; they are smaller and easier to mess with right now. You can safely rub glue all around the rods.

Glued up? Good - place the stabilizer onto the mylar. You can hold it level and just above the mylar, then drop it; or try and lower it down level. Spare mylar has been known to be handy here ;-^)

Now you go around the outline with your finger and gently push the CF rod onto the mylar - it sticks just fine. Take your modelling knife with new or really sharpened blade and cut the unit free of the rest of the mylar. Aim to leave enough mylar to just wrap around the CF rod. When done, pick up and gently work the mylar "border" around the rod and down onto the glue.

You should have enough working time here, but don't hang around, go for lunch or answer the phone. It is possible to work a little glue into a dry spot, by scraping a smidgin of glue off the stick with a toothpick and applying to the dry area.

When done, and happy with the result - leave it to dry! Overnight is always good. If you don't think this will hold - and mine's still stuck after three - odd years - carefully drip thin CA onto the join between the CF rod and the mylar covering. This will keep you quiet for hours, as you only want a miniscule amount of glue in there; so you have to apply a drop and gently tip the wing so it runs out as thinly as possible.

Keep at it, the Stub' is still the finest right-way-up indoor flier IMO. Indoor RC is so civilised, but there are many early dawns and late nights when you can grab a five-minute flight in the drive, or even on a quiet street under the lights. There's no finer way to forget the day at the grind than to walk out the door and fly in your yard or street for five minutes.

Regards, Dereck

David Bonin <  > covers the same topic:

JW is on track with the acetone. Wipe down your rods before you build to remove any molding/release agents. Wipe down your model before covering to remove finger oil. Denatured or rubbing alcohol works fine for this, as well as acetone.

One more thing to consider: If the rods have an epoxy-rich outer layer, then very lightly scuffing them with 0000 steel wool could give the covering adhesive and building glues a better surface bite. I'd suggest trying this on a little test frame first.

Dave

Adam Bryant <  > inquires:

I recall seeing a post a few months ago about using a 12-volt car battery charger to power a foam cutter, and would like to do this myself. If I recall correctly, it was a standard charger with a 10 Amp setting, connected to a standard light dimmer rheostat for additional current control, then from the dimmer to the bow. Can anyone point me to a reference for this kind of setup?

I have looked at several designs recently that use a 110 VAC to 16 VAC transformer in place of the battery charger, but being cheap I don't want to spend $10 on a transformer when I already have the battery charger.

Any pointers would be greatly appreciated.

Adam Bryant, Parker, Colorado

...Adam, two items in your query seem a little dubious to me. First, today's "lamp dimmers" are not rheostats, but pulsing semi- conductors. I've used these for years for precision heat control of soldering irons and MonoKote irons -- and they work on AC, not DC. To employ one of these to control the current from a car battery charger, you'd connect it between the AC power source and the charger, NOT between the charger output and the cutting bow.

Second, I've tried several times to use 100 VAC transformers with 14 to 16 VAC output, for illuminating the work tables on my scroll saws, bandsaw, and drill press, via automotive light bulbs.

Of maybe five or six transformers, only one survived more than a short time. They do NOT appear to be well-made! [JW]

Sam Brauer <  > wrote:

I've come across a new speed control that I think will be of interest to our crowd. I've installed Pixie-7s from Castle Creations (I picked up a bunch at the WRAMS show, and met Pat Castillo, who is a pretty nice guy.) in both an MTM S-300 (which uses a Speed 380 motor -- a well-done unit, although dealing with MTM can be aggravating.) and on a VL HY-50B.

This speed control is the most versatile unit I've come across, because these are two pretty disparate motors, and the controller seems to handle them both without any trouble.

In the past I've used the FMA Mini 5, which several people have told me is not a well-done unit and should not be used with a good receiver. The FMA unit is not rated to handle beyond 7 cells, so a 3-cell lithium pack could conceivably fry it.

The other unit I've used is the Kontronik 10 which will not work with a 2-cell lithium pack, because of a low voltage cut-off -- but works well with a 3-cell lithium pack.

The Pixie 7 has the following advantages over these two units it's lighter, will work with any reasonable pack for Speed 300 and smaller applications, i.e. both 2- and 3-cell lithium packs; won't fry a receiver; and is less expensive than the Kontronik 10 and can handle more current if you read the specifications sheets carefully.

If you go in through the E-zone web site, the Pixie 7's are selling for about $30. It's clear that this speed control is a great match for the VL HY 50B, since that motor will run on 5 to 10 cells. I've just finished a Skylark (the Lidberg kit) and I may try flying it with either the two-cell or the 3-cell lithium pack. (I'll let people know how it flies soon.)

One downside to the Pixie 7: the low voltage warnings are set up for nicads, not lithiums. Tom McAnn at Sky Hooks and Rigging has recently introduced a Speed Control that uses a voltage cutoff of 7.2V for 3-cell lithium packs.

This speed control is more expensive than a Pixie 7; but if you're not comfortable with timing your flights and landing while there is still something left in your lithium cells, it may be a wise investment, as a 3-cell lithium pack costs $50 and if you drain a lithium pack, you get to buy a replacement.

Sam Brauer
