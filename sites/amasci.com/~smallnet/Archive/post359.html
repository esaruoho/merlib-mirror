Several people I know use Rustoleum paints almost exclusively. It is easy to apply with their spray cans, inexpensive, and consistent. But! it is only good to about 10% Nitro levels!

Also, it never picks up a high gloss; it is sort of semi-matte. Also, the colors are wonderful for interior decorating, but not exactly the Knock-em-Dead bright colors favored by modelers.

Finally, I don't know how heavy it is compared to model dope, but I have my suspicions that weight was not considered in the formulation.

Jim Rundle <  > contributed:

Jim Tait asked about the nitro resistance of Rustoleum.

I had heard good reports about it and tried it on an airplane...bad idea. I used their medium blue over a nitrate dope base. On most of the airplane it was fine, but around the engine and fuel tank, and downwind from the exhaust, it started peeling after a few flights.

I used 15% nitro. Since then I've heard that different colors vary in their nitro resistance. Maybe so, but I'll stick with dope.

I also had bad luck with Pactra Poly-U black. It peeled instantly from 15% nitro. Twenty years ago I finished a flight box with Poly-U clear, and it still looks like new. So when the black paint started peeling I cleaned it up, sprayed Poly-U clear over it, and let it cure for two weeks. That didn't help at all. Finally I sanded it all off, re-painted with dope, and I've had no more trouble.

Jim Rundle

...Good to get a query on something off the beaten track, Alan!

There HAVE been rubber-powered R/C airplanes flown success- fully, and quite some time ago too. I've seen pictures and reduced- size plans for these in Aeromodeller Annuals from the 1960 era. One Japanese design was extremely attractive...

But rubber power has characteristics that make it awkward for powering R/C models, except as novelties. The motor needs a lot of space, for one thing. Not just length, either. In operation a powerful rubber motor "jumps rope" more than a little.

Of course, you could enclose the motor in a tube; and in fact my late friend Paul Plecan designed several scale rubber models around a central "tube", and on at least one of the full-size plans for these (don't ask!) is a suggestion for installing a single-channel radio system...

And don't forget that a rubber motor's power output varies continuously from fully-wound to limp.

However, there's a much better alternative available now for "lightweight, clean, quiet" power for small R/C airplanes.

CO2! Recently Stefan Gasparin has come out with a series of long-duration, high-efficiency CO2 motors that spin rubber-type propellers at almost constant speed for seven to ten minutes per charge. (Recharges require just a few SECONDS, too.)

Some of these new Gasparins come with throttles for R/C use! The smallest of these new controllable-speed CO2 motors is the G28BBRV. (The "BB" means "Ball-Bearing", and its tiny crankshaft runs in TWO of those!) The G28BBRV spins a 5-inch propeller at 2200 rpm for about seven minutes (at full throttle) before the power drops off noticeably. At part-throttle settings for R/C (or I/C) flying, this motor runs for over ten minutes. Yet the entire "power system", including the propeller and a full tank of liquid CO2, weighs only a quarter of an ounce!

"What size model could I fly with that tiny a motor?" you may wonder. Light weight, of course, is the primary criterion. A total flying weight of one ounce would be about the maximum you could expect good performance from. Some Peanut-sized, and most Embryo Endurance and Bostonian rubber-powered designs could probably be adapted.

(In common with rubber power, with CO2 power good flight performance requires large-diameter, high-pitch propellers turning slowly. As a rough rule of thumb, a suitable "conventional mono- plane model" size for sport CO2 flying would be one with a wing- span between three and four times the prop diameter.)

The largest (so far) of the new high-efficiency CO2 motors from Gasparin is a throttleable version of the GMW 73T Twin. On this a variable throttle assembly replaces the firewall-mounted "manifold" of the first model. This type of installation simplifies the speed control linkage.

The GMW 73T Twin swings a 7-inch prop at 2000 rpm for 6 minutes at full throttle; or a 9 1/2" P-30 prop at 1250 rpm for 7 minutes. A suitable R/C model for the GMW 73T should weigh 7 ounces or less, with a wing area somewhere around 200 square inches.

Not all of Gasparin's new long-duration CO2 motors have throttles. The G160 (which turns a 7" prop at over 1500 rpm for 3 minutes, and weighs 3/4 ounce complete with tank and prop) features an easy-to-adjust manual speed control that's part of the head assembly. A knurled adjustment wheel on top makes rpm adjustment for free flight use quick and convenient.

A close look at one of these new high-efficiency motors shows a big difference between the new design and that of older CO2 motors. Its exhaust ports are MUCH smaller. This points up the seldom-appreciated fact that CO2 motors produce their power through gas EXPANSION, not pressure.

Yes, pressure IS supplied to the motor from its CO2 tank. But because of the unchangeable physical properties of CO2, the pressure generated inside the tank is far too high for efficient use in small piston-type motors. Only moderate pressure is needed to provide adequate cold gas entering the cylinder at the top of the piston stroke for full-power output of the motor. High pressure is a handicap, not an advantage!

I well remember when the first OK CO2 motors came out. That was 1947, two years after V-J Day. Every model flyer in America wanted one. We bought 'em, too! But we didn't understand that high gas supply pressure was counter-productive; actually very harmful to CO2 motors.

I ruined four of my own early motors: three OK's and a lovely Campus A-100 that spun a 3 1/2-inch prop -- by mistakenly boosting their charging pressure through pre-heating the CO2 capsules.

(Lots of us did that. We used "common sense": these motors run on CO2 under high pressure, right? Therefore the greater the pressure, the more power, right?? So we went ahead and did things like laying a box of Sparkletts capsules on a blacktop parking lot in the sun. Or held a capsule in an armpit for several minutes before loading it into the OK CO2's cartridge holder. Result: blown sealing gaskets at the "output end" -- and sometimes even a ruptured side strap on the sheet aluminum holder body...)

Moderate pressure nicely does the job of spinning a CO2 motor's prop -- and high pressure is only harmful.

Here's an analogy: driving brads with a hammer. If a 6-ounce tackhammer works well for that, would a 12-pound sledge do any better?

All the new Gasparin motors employ quite small-diameter tubing for supplying CO2 to the motor heads. The flow restriction from the minuscule inside area of this tubing acts as a pressure reducer. The piston-actuated valve in the head is designed to lower the incoming gas pressure even further. The result is longer motor runs per charge, higher propulsion efficiency, and lengthened motor life.

In spite of its advantages over electric and rubber power for "quiet flight" model airplanes, CO2 isn't yet nearly as popular. That's partly due to "refueling" considerations.

For decades the standard source for model-flying CO2 has been "capsules". The original "Sparkletts" capsule size holds 8 grams of CO2. That provided power for a couple or three recharges of a Telco motor; perhaps as many as four or five for one of the exquisitely-made Brown A-23's.

Nowadays 12-gram CO2 capsules are readily available from Wal Mart, priced between 50 and 60 cents each (depending on how many there are in the package: 5 or 25).

However, the increasing popularity of Paint-Ball gun shooting offers a new option for CO2 motor tank refilling. Wal Mart sells a 9-ounce refillable CO2 paint-ball tank for about $25; and I recently bought a 16-ounce tank from a Paint Ball supply dealer. That cost me $40 -- plus another $3 to have it filled with liquid CO2.

That's enough for about 30 recharges of the biggest-capacity Gasparin motor tanks I have. And inexpensive CO2 "bulk tank" refills are available both at Paint Ball Centers and many welding supply dealers.

Now for the proper technique of recharging CO2 motor tanks! COOLNESS is particularly important, especially for summer flying. That's because above 87 degrees F, CO2 CANNOT exist as a liquid, no matter how high the pressure. At "room temperature", about 800 pounds per square inch (gage) inside a CO2 tank is enough to keep most of the contents fluid.

But once the temperature exceeds 87 degrees, the liquid CO2 all converts to gas, and the internal pressure jumps to over 2000 psig. Sometimes WAY over. (Steve Staples could tell us about an interesting occurrence of that...)

Experienced CO2 flyers take precautions against their tanks getting warm. No, they don't refrigerate them -- just keeping them out of the sun is usually all that's needed. On a hot summer day, though, carrying your "bulk CO2 tank" in a cooler with a little ice might be a wise move.

...All Gasparin CO2 motors come with comprehensive instruction booklets. Those describe the filling procedures in detail, plus much more information. I won't repeat that here -- but I do want to mention one very important item in recharging a CO2 motor's tank.

Fritz Mueller, Atlanta's CO2-power guru and an adviser of Stefan Gasparin's, spelled this out in a Flying Models article a year ago.

When you attach the propeller to the motor, position it so that one blade points directly away from the cylinder head with the piston at the bottom of its stroke. Mark this "bottom blade" with a dot of paint. Whenever you fill the motor's tank, always do that with the marked blade downward.

That's to make certain the motor's exhaust ports are open during filling. Otherwise, the high charging pressure can pass through the head valve (which closes from FLOW, not pressure) and damage the tiny O-ring on the piston.
