Gene LaFaille < glafaille@worldnet.att.net > contributed:

I'm new to electric R/C, having been a "wet" flyer for many years. Several years ago my job prevented me from participating in the hobby for a while. Recently the job changed, and when I found myself with a little time, I decided to give electrics a try. Now I'm hooked!

I've sold all of my wet power engines and supplies, even all of my standard size servos! I decided to build a Clancy Lazy Bee with a standard wing as my first victim. I used an inexpensive car motor from Tower Hobbies, Master Airscrew 3:1 gearbox, an Astro Flight speed control with BEC, and two standard servos. All-up weight with a 6 cell 1400 mAh battery pack is about 42 oz. Performance is great; the model will take off from short grass and fly for about 6 - 8 minutes depending on how aggressive I am with the throttle.

My thoughts about electrics so far are these:

If you stick with the stuff that the R/C car folks use, electrics can be downright CHEAP! I've heard SMALLsters and others moan about the costs of electrics and I guarantee you they are involved with Speed 400 or non-car-based components. As with other things, there is economy in numbers. There are a lot more R/C car modelers than electric R/C flyers!

I bought a tunable, ball bearing, "car" motor from Tower Hobbies on sale for less than $10.00; batteries on sale for $10.99 per pack (I bought 5!); gearbox $16.00, Astro 215 speed control $54.00. Although I have an Astro 110d which I bought used for $80.00, if you stick with my plan of using R/C car stuff, you can find a peak-detecting 6- or 7-cell charger on eBay for less than $25.00.

So all together you would spend a little over $120.00 for all of the electric stuff to get started including TWO battery packs. I'll bet many of us have spent more than that for just one "wet" motor! By the way, I don't use those high-dollar electric props; just stick a good old Top Flite or Zinger on there and it works fine!

Compare this with the popular Speed 400 craze. First you'll have to buy smaller servos and a small receiver. Speed 400 motors are usually more than $10.00, Speed 400 battery packs are usually 3 times more than 6-cell "car" packs. Speed 400 speed controls are about the same. Gearboxes probably a little more. Chargers are a different story: you MUST buy a high-dollar charger to charge 8 or more cells; the cheap 6-cell charger won't work.

The "car" motors are about the same as Speed 600. There are hundreds of different types of these motors, manufactured by a variety of companies, each of which has spent lots of $$$$ trying to squeeze the maximum performance from a cheap 6- or 7-cell battery pack. Why do electric flyers often ignore this source of inexpensive components?

These motors and batteries will fly most any model normally powered by a "wet" .09 to .25 motor. Lots of planes out there in that range! Plus weight is not quite as critical as it is with the smaller electrics, which means your standard servos will probably work fine.

Like any other hobby, you can spend as much or as little as you want. If YOU want to spend big money for the latest and greatest high-tech stuff, I and the hobby manufacturers salute you. You'll get a little better performance, but you'll spend a lot for that 10% increase. Watch eBay and RConline.com for bargains: last year's latest and greatest is this year's "cast off". It doesn't have to be expensive to be fun!

By the way, I have had no problems whatsoever using narrow-band AM radios in electrics. I have seen good narrow-band AM sets on eBay for less than $50.00. WOW! Have even seen data that indicates narrow-band AM is LESS vulnerable to interference than dual- conversion FM.

Total bill for a newbie's electric flight components: $120.00; radio $50.00; airplane kit $50.00; covering, glue, etc. $30 to $50. Total to give it a go about $250.00. And once you learn how to fly, you can fly it in the park!!

If we talk too much about how expensive electrics CAN be we might scare off someone considering this facet of our hobby. Come on in, the water is fine!!

Gene LaFaille, San Antonio, Texas

Steve Morris < mlbco@sirius.com > reports:

Eddie asked about my Junior Falcon: "Tell me, is the red and black trim enamel paint? How much weight would you say you added to the airframe by using that method?"

I painted the bare wood and tissue with 4 coats of Nitrate dope thinned 50/50, sanding between coats. Then I sprayed the whole thing with red enamel, and hand-painted the trim with enamel also.

The enamel paint is not really fuel-proof, so I sprayed a coat of gloss clear polyurethane over the whole model to seal it.

The Junior Falcon has a big wing for a 1/2A model, and even with the paint and a 4-cell AA battery pack it is not too heavy. It flies great with a Cox Medallion 049 set very rich. I didn't weigh the model but it's probably close to 18 ounces.

It could easily handle a modern 3-channel radio with a Norvel 049 for power. I only wish I had molded the canopy from clear plastic instead of carving it from balsa and painting it. I really liked the clear canopy on the original model.

Thanks, Steve

George E Pearce < gep10@juno.com > wonders:

Can't remember where I saw this, but someone mentioned sticking pieces of tape to a propeller blade to see if balance improves. If someone can elaborate on this process, I would appreciate it.

George Pearce

...George, the fundamental idea is to reduce vibration while the prop is spinning. On a single-cylinder engine it's not possible to eliminate all vibration due to the piston's motion. But it's been found by experimenters such as Don Garry and Herb Wahl that a model engine can sometimes be made to run more smoothly if it's equipped with a deliberately UNbalanced prop. (That is, one which has one "heavy" blade, as indicated on a "propeller balancer".)

This can ONLY be determined by trial and error. And one way to perform the trials is to stick a fair-sized piece of masking tape on the back side of one propeller blade; start the motor; and see whether the added tape weight has any effect on the smoothness of the engine's operation.

If the vibration gets WORSE, you stop the motor; remove the tape; then place it (or a new piece) on the other blade and try again.

Once you determine that you can minimize vibration with pieces of tape (it may take several) on your propeller, you remove that prop from the engine, first making a note of its position on the shaft, because that's a critical factor.

Now you put the taped prop on a balancer and determine (mainly by eyeballing the position of its heavy blade) how far out of balance it is. Finally, you peel off the tape, then reproduce the out-of-balance condition with coats of paint, or by sanding the opposite blade from the formerly-taped one.

This can become a LOT of work; but some modelers feel that it's worth the trouble. (I don't.) [JW]
