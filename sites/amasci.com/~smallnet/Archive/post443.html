Ian L. McQueen <  > contributed:

Most of my education in electronics came from reading copies of Electronics Australia when I arrived there in '73. I read back issues into the early 60s, bought the magazine from '75 on while in the country, and continued a subscription through my time in Japan and back to Canada in '93.

I finally had to give it up it up a few years later when it became cost-prohibitive due to air-mail only shipping. Recently I decided to see if EA was available on-line, and was saddened to learn that it had ceased publication (date not given).

One of my favorite sections was the Circuit Notebook, which gave experimenter's circuits. Usually these were fairly simple and very varied. Another magazine, Silicon Chip, appears to have largely taken over the role of EA, and I was happy to see that they also continued the tradition of Circuit Notebook. And what appeared in the January '05 issue but the circuit for a charger for Li-Polymer cells, designed to meet the various charging requirements!!

I was able to download the circuit diagram and text from the Silicon Chip website: < http://www.siliconchip.com.au/cms/archive.html >

If anyone here is looking for a LiPo charger, is handy with electronic components, and can design his own circuit board from a circuit diagram, try this URL. If you have any difficulty, contact me directly and I'll be happy to send the circuit diagram and related text.

The components are Australian (European) types; the transistors are not generally available in the USA. But component suppliers in Australia should be happy to mail anywhere in the world, and the required components are light, so postage would be minimal.

One of these electronics parts suppliers advertises on the same page as the Circuit Notebook, for a start.

The transistors are general-purpose types, so many common US types should be usable.

No ready-made printed circuit board is available, but component suppliers should have general-purpose experimenters' boards that have positions for ICs, transistors, resistors, etc., to be connected as you wish.

If you have an interest in DIY electronics projects, the website offers hours of time well wasted!

[...In an e-mail, Ian also made a correction to a statement of mine about the materials used in molded plastic fuel tanks:]

Polyethylene doesn't need plasticizers for flexibility. (It's PVC that does.) PE may crack in response to exposure to UV (the material eventually hardens and breaks up). Cracking may also be due to repeated stress.

Larry Renger asked whether anyone ever actually used a gelatin mixture to seal silk instead of dope.

I have very limited experience, namely on a single model, a 30" Easy-Built Sunbeam. I was in Japan and had access to Esaki silk at wholesale since I was sending some overseas, so I decided to give it a try.

I used the gelatin method, having read about it somewhere. It's Knox-type pure gelatin, of course, not Jell-O! I made up a fairly thick mixture, but you'll have to experiment for I don't have a clue as to proportions.

When I brushed it on, the silk sagged alarmingly (so be careful on back-to-back surfaces like thin stabilizers. Consider holding the part upside down while brushing.

After drying, the silk shrank back to its original tightness or better, and the weave holes in the silk were bridged with a thin membrane of gelatin. I put on a single coat of thinned dope to anchor the gelatin, for it would probably fall out over time. That was enough to seal the weave quite well, though it didn't give a glossy surface.

Gelatin is cheap, and you could experiment on a thin piece of fabric from a fabric store before committing to silk. I believe that dress-lining material is the lightest material available. I've read of it for covering R/C models.

Try it and report back! Ian

Dereck Woodward <  > philosophizes:

JW observed that "...woodworking magazines have either started up or expanded. With titles such as "American Woodworker", "Shop Notes", etc. -- containing both simple and quite elaborate projects; some even providing full-size patterns. Most of these woodworking projects require far more equipment, and take far more time to complete, than most scratch-built model airplanes. They demand considerable skill too.

"Yet build-it-yourself model airplanes seem to be dying out. It can't be from lack of time, space, or equipment -- though those are the excuses people make..."

If you insist on wasting time on line :-^) at places like E Zone Mag's discussion forums, you'll find the "build vs buy" argument rages continuously. While builders are lauded by other builders for producing unusual, well-crafted or developed ideas, the way a buyer gains laudability is to be first to buy the latest new toy!

How it actually performs appears to be fairly immaterial. As the buyers are most likely to be self-taught and not fly in a group situation, their knowledge of performance closely matches that of their flight instructor's know-how of teaching, and their assessor's knowledge of what to look for in flight performance.

Does anyone need time to think about that?

We're in an era of instant gratification, no doubt about that. "Consumers" are now well used to buying what they are told, and many folk who can only spell 'aircraft' slowly and while moving their lips can now claim to be RC fliers.

Probably best that we can't force these people to join the AMA and a local club, and hence make them fly off a regular runway, using an acceptable traffic pattern and perform landings back on that runway. Their concept of RC flight is more likely to be take off by handtoss into wind from where-ever is convenient, flying is done by avoiding nearby planets, trees, buildings or whatever, if possible, and landing occurs sometime shortly after the surprise event of the battery expiring.

When the model is beyond repair, another is rapidly bought, and life goes on. One day, they walk past another advert they can't refuse (in much the same way a dog can't refuse a doggy treat...) and they aren't an RC flier anymore.

That's life.

Funnily enough, my local hobby shop has RC flying toys and a few larger, more conventional Bought Already To Fly (BARF) stock at the back of the store, and some Guillow-type balsa wood kits right up by the checkout!

The display of the latter rotates through stock too, so someone must be buying models that require effort and thought.

JW noted: "We SMALLsters are holding the line (I think) against mass-produced flying toys and the Buy-n-Fly syndrome."

Looking around at the ages of folk at model meetings can sometimes get depressing ....

Randy Randolph mentioned the web site: < http://www.profili2.com/eng/default.htm > having a bunch of model plans from the old magazines that can be downloaded free.

There's quite a few folk out there in Webland who put considerable effort into acquiring plans, scanning or otherwise digitising them, and then giving them away! I found my "SquErt" S400 electric model 'out there' recently. The plan was initially a freeby in the now defunct Radio Modeller over in England, followed by US publication in RCM -- both plans produced identical models, of course, from different layouts. I think the web ripoff is from the RCM version, which means someone had to buy the thing in the first place.

The model was designed around the only batteries I could buy to match with a S400 at the time, which were "AA"-sized nicads. While it was pretty aerobatic all things considered, it is a little dated now. For one thing, the batteries lay across the fuselage, making it much wider than I'd go for now.

There are some folk who just have to have a web site!

As for the comment about the sameness of today's model magazines, one snag I've found, in the never-ending search to fuel a monthly print column, is that folk now can slap endless reams and photos up on a website themselves. Making your own site isn't all that difficult -- even I had one for a while; though I finally lost interest when the hosting service started asking for money.

Then there's always the many forums around - E Zone, Small Flying Arts, etc. Now that you can almost take and web-publish photos instantly, plus being your own author, using, I suspect, little beyond your mobile phone's camera function and a PC -- who wants to go to all the trouble of submitting shots to magazine columnists and have to wait months before one appears in print ?

Even worse: you have to buy the magazine that has your stuff in it.

One snag with designing is that there's a lot of plans out there, from the dawn of time, and it's really hard to avoid duplication, intentional or otherwise. I still get several UK based mags, and they have some spectacularly different offerings. I have half of a pair of plans given away at full size in consecutive months, for a twin-engined amphibious flying boat with retracts, 'imagineered' from 1920's full size aircraft designs.

The model is for two .25 glows, though electrocution would be easy. The model is pretty big at 65" span, and pretty complex -- though mostly in terms of numbers of parts; anyone on this board could manage one! I also get the odd repros via Gray, who writes the Sports Channel column in RC Model World. Gray is a true SMALLster, who manages to get French aeromodelling magazines. The free plans in those are worth a look-see, with bizarre and always SMALL inclined models, a mix of electric and glider types.

That weight of evidence suggests that the BARF aspect of the hobby is far more prevalent in the US than other places.

Who cares? What I do or say has little weight anyway.

Right now, I'm swithering between a Ford Flivver (the good looking 268, as modelled by Williams Brothers, not the incredibly ugly 3218 ;^) ); a Fisher FP303; and a Very Large Scale model, a 38" span electric version of the FROG 'Raven' 18" low winged rubber model -- all for S400 power, for a change.

The little S400 is still the best bang for the buck in small e-flight. It might lack glamour, but it works, is stinkin' cheap and can clatter a small scale or sports model around for a good while on cheap batteries -- no need to sit in the garage watching a LiPo pack charge in a fireproof container, while hoping it doesn't puff/explode/burst into flames.

The "Raven" is especially interesting, as I could never afford "FROG" kits as a kid! The name stands for "Flies Right Off Ground" by the way. It's a low-winger, and I'll be flying it with prototypical dihedral on rudder and elevator, no ailerons. This works just fine for relaxing clattering around low, with the odd loopy, roll-y tricks thrown in.

I got introduced to this by Randy Randolph's "BeeTween" 049 powered ship back in England. It was published in MAN, and was about as simple as a RC model can get. I flew my BeeTween for ages, gave it to one of her many admirers when I left England, and later built my own low-wing R/E ship behind a PAW 55 diesel with no throttle.

This model ended up in Eric Clutton's hands and was rumoured to have inspired his range of low-wing R/E semi-scale warbird plans. These were propelled by small PAW diesels, but that's no surprise, I'm sure!

I'm off to work -- you go build something different!

Regards, Dereck Woodward

Pat Tritle <  > observes:

I must agree that "modeling" has been on the decline for more years than I care to count. But it's a long way from dead. The difference is that now, the "real modelers" have gone into "the closet" and are, for lack of a better term, keeping it among themselves. There are lots of modelers out there; you just need to know where to find them.

When I walk into the LHS, I see shelves full of Shake and Bake ARFs, where the whole deal is built around instant gratification, and where the basic attitude is to get into the air quickly.

The problem is that the new "modeler" can purchase the whole setup in less then 15 minutes; have it ready to fly in less then an hour; then much to his chagrin discovers that he will need to dedicate a large portion of his "spare time" over a span of several weeks, or even months, to "learn" to fly what came to him with little or no effort. Or, he'll try the plane on his own and wreck it.

Then what's left ends up rat-holed in the garage long before the first Master Card payment comes due.

It's been my observation that in life, the less the investment, the less the return. The kind of person who requires the kind of model that can be "ready to fly" in a matter of minutes, usually sees learning to fly it in the same light; and in the majority of cases, will stop showing up before the third lesson. On the other hand, I've trained a few guys to fly on ARFs while offering what help I can as they build their first kit.

Sometimes, those guys get hooked on "modeling", and in a couple of cases have gone on to build some really nice models -- and from that, some terrific friendships have spawned. Others simply can't, or won't, spend the time to learn the skills; which like anything good, don't come overnight.

I also notice an unhealthy division at the flying fields between the "modelers" and the ARF crowd. Modelers are a unique bunch, and many times have a hard time relating to "non-modelers", who can't relate to "modelers" -- and the vicious cycle continues.

That's not as big a problem in the Albuquerque area anymore, as the ARF crowd has basically run the modelers off by generating an unsafe environment, with no regard for life, limb, or the models we fly!

As a result, those of us who still fly models that we actually build, are doing it in small groups under the cover of early dawn, in school yards which we don't advertise to what has become known in these parts as the "crash crew". I really hate to have to do it that way, but I simply refuse to risk a good airplane to a group of individuals who look upon a model airplane as a "throw-away" entity.

But there is good news. The cottage industry has picked us up where the mass market has pushed us aside. And fortunately, there are still a couple of the smaller, "large companies" out there who see a place in the market for good kits and are offering some very nice stuff.

The Internet has also served well to bring a relatively small, but very dedicated bunch of modelers and craftsmen together where we can feed off each others skills, this forum not being the least of them.

What I see as sad, is that there are many out there, who if handled properly and encouraged sufficiently, have endless potential, but can't deal with the "throw away" mentality of many of the newcomers to the "hobby". As a result, they're gone before they get there.

I find it equally sad that in today's marketplace, the newcomer feels he has to start with a half-scale Mustang equipped with a Super Tiger 10,000, and never gets to experience the joy and learning of a simple free flight model and all that can be gained from it -- or to fly a cantankerous 1/2A U-control model till you're so dizzy you can barely stand up.

Now, with that being said, it's our job as "modelers" to give back to those guys, that which was given to us by the people who were where we are now, as we came up through the ranks. If we are willing to do that, modeling will never return to what it was in its heyday, but it will never die!

PAT
