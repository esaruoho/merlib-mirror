Jim Porter <  > wrote:

Those who are using the exhaust ring/collector on PAW diesels may find that the problems they are having is due to a buildup of tolerances causing the collector ring to collapse and choke off the exhaust.

The main cause seems to be using two of the fiber washers to seal the collector to the cylinder base and the crankcase.

I had a 100 R/C TBR that ran fairly well initially, but the cylinder came loose. I tightened it, and the engine ran a little slower. After several iterations I had an engine that would barely get out of its own way. Some serious head scratching, a run on the test stand, and a disassembly turned on the light. The collector had collapsed and nearly shut off the exhaust.

Re-assembly with ONE gasket eliminated the problem, and all has been fine since.

....Mark, seems to me that back in the summer of 1998 you mentioned having to use "crankcase pressure" from your HP .20 in order for the fuel to feed from your Lazy Bee's tank. (I put "crankcase pressure" between quotation marks there because a 4-stroke engine doesn't produce the high net positive crankcase internal pressure that a 2-stroker does.)

The configuration of a Lazy Bee's nose makes for a rather low tank position, with consequent suction feed difficulties. Several SMALLsters in previous Postings have offered solutions to this problem, such as mounting the engine at an angle, or even sideways, to bring the carburetor spraybar closer to the tank centerline.

On your HP .20-powered Lazy Bee, the spraybar must be a good two inches higher than the tank center! No wonder you've had fuel delivery trouble... Still, your latest submission states that you cannot even PUMP more than about half the fuel out of your 4-ounce Sullivan.

That sure sounds like a slit fuel pickup line to me. Sullivan tank kits come with brass tubes that have EXCEEDINGLY sharp edges. If you use these in "stock condition", and employ thin- wall silicone tubing for your Clunk connection, you are guaran- teed to develop a "knife slit" in the silicone where it meets the brass tube end.

For years I've routinely modified the ends of ALL my Sullivan tank brass tubes. I tin the ends (after forming the tubes & inserting them through the rubber stopper); then add a copper wire "ring" around each end. Soldering that lightly in place both "breaks the sharp edge" and acts as a slip-off preventer. [JW]

Jarvis Lopez asked, "I want to convert from glow to electrics... I want to purchase an Astro 15g; and they are available with 2.39, 2.89 and the new 3.4x gear ratios. I want to be able to use the 15g in Lazy Bees, Kadet 25, Speedy Bees, ARF deluxe Telemaster electric etc. without the hassle of becoming an expert in gear ratios.

"With my Webra 19 using a 10/4 I can build any of the above without worry. Which version of the Astro 15g would give me the same versatility ?"

You'll want the sport or "cobalt" version -- the FAI one is designed for soarers to zoom off to a great height and then fly around in circles on the glide. The 15 is one of Astro's more flexible motors, though happiest on around 12 cells, looking to what folk have used it for mostly.

Having said that, it would work on 8 cells in your Lazy Bee case and take 14 in a very handy Kadet LT25. What you have to watch for is the current -- topping out around 25A, maybe 28A for short bursts, is good for long life.

You don't need to be an "expert" in gear ratios - think of them like car gears - you want to go up a steep hill with a lot of passengers or accelerate hard, you select a low gear. With a model aircraft, you only have one gear, so you compromise for the model. Electric power has a flexibility that wet power cannot match -- I have a "Pondside" flying boat -- a slow and gentle flier with an Astro 035 on direct drive, with 6 cells and a 7 x 4 prop.

That same motor can handle twice the power in my aerobatic model geared 2.9:1 on ten cells and a 10 x 8 prop. It is the ability to swap gears that gets much of that flexibility.

With all due respect, diesels and glows can't quite cover that range!

What's that? Yes, Smithers, cars have gears. Putting the lever into D (for dummies) doesn't mean GM and Ford did away with them ;-)

A good place to go for more information is Ken Myers' pages at < http://members.aol.com/kmyersefo/index.htm#TOP >

Another is < http://ezonemag.com > with the invaluable Eflight mailing list -- ask about every e-flier in the US in one message.

There's whole sections on folks' impressions of their models in there, lots of ideas too.

For what you want, get the 2.9:1 ratio, the cobalt motor and make sure you buy an Astro "Whattmeter" to tell you what's happening in there. It's not that I want to plug the "Whattmeter"; it is just the only game in town unless you are into electronics and can build something that can read currents up to 30 amps or more. It's a tool you'll find in 90% of electric flight toolboxes in the US!

One neat device I have is a belt drive from Modelairtech -- this allows me to change gear ratios at the field. It's a bulky thing compared to the Astro 'box, but I intend to fit it into a Sig LT25 for use as a sports model, glider tug and camera model; the ability to change a gear to the task in hand at the field might be useful.

One thing is certain - buy an Astro, you'll have it a long while. I ran my 035 at 300 watts -- about twice what Astro say it's capable of -- for three years. It got slightly disassembled when a mid-air took the wing off my best aerobatic ship. The commutator was fine, a replacement brush holder was epoxied in -- about $2.00 -- I put the original brushes back in and she's as good as before the crash.

A cheap ferrite motor wouldn't last a week at those power levels, let alone three years of constant flying. As Keith Shaw says, "Buy cheap, buy twice", and he knows about electrics.

Bernard Cawley, Jr. <  > also responds to Jarvis Lopez's "What's the best gear ratio...?" query:

You may not like this answer, but it is ANY of them, with appropriate choices of battery and propeller. As the gear ratio goes down, the cell count and/or the prop size must also decrease in order to keep the current (and running time) within reason. The high ratio gearbox will call for using a 13-15 inch propeller.

Of your listed planes, the LT-25 is probably the only one that even has enough ground clearance for that (I have plenty of clearance for a 12-inch prop on mine).

How fast does your Webra 19 turn that 10X4? What kind of 10X4?

What sort of performance do you expect from the Lazy Bee and the ARF Telemaster from this engine? (I picked those two as the extremes of weight/wing loading from your four planes.)

As an aside, the Lazy Bee/Speedy Bee are both on the lightweight side to carry a "normal" 12-14 cell battery for an Astro 15. You would want to use smaller than sub-C sized cells in those planes if you want to use that motor - either 800AR's or 1250SCR's at 1.1 - 1.5 ounces apiece, rather than the RC2000 sized cells at 2 ounces apiece.

The LT-25 will have NO problem with a full battery complement -- there are electric LT-25s flying with anywhere from Astro 035s with the high ratio gearbox and 10 cells up to Astro 25s with 16 cells.

The Telemaster, being about the same size, but heavier, will probably also work with anything in that range, but it will fly like it is heavier.

<  > writes about lightweight model covering:

For indoor (or cautiously outdoor) models of the relatively "heavy" type the easiest, very light covering I have found is the 2 um Mylar film available from David Lewis < www.homefly.com/ >.

This is 12 inches wide, for $0.70 per linear foot, 0.08 mil, and weighs 2.2 grams per square meter.

By "heavy", I mean planes weighing from 1 gram to a couple of ounces, not the almost weightless works of art, reminiscent of thistledown, created by the experts of indoor models.

My favorite method of application, learned from a book on indoor models, is to cut a piece slightly larger than the surface to be covered, wad it up in as tight a ball as possible; carefully smooth it out, then repeat.

Now take the carefully smoothed out piece and glue it to your framework with whatever is your favorite adhesive. A glue stick works well (UHU or Avery are good brands). CA can be used, but is a bit more permanent than I like. A glue stick allows you to CAREFULLY slide the film, and tighten it somewhat for several minutes. The film can also be peeled off later, if re-covering is desired.

Do not shrink the film! The very small wrinkles in the surface appear to actually improve the performance of the aircraft. It's "micro turbulence" or whatever else to which you want to attribute the added lift characteristic.

The film is really quite strong and holds up well to everything except sharp, pointy objects. The wadding-up also appears to eliminate "static cling" and make the material easier to work, since it's now not quite as invisible. (Watching someone work with this material must be like watching a mime.)

The same technique can be used with the 5 um or 4 um aluminized film, but the wrinkles will be somewhat bigger and not look as nice.

Be careful of any nicks or cuts along the edge of the film as you are smoothing it. An edge cut will propagate very easily. Use a very sharp blade when cutting the film to avoid such edge cuts.

If you really, really want to shrink the film, it will withstand an amazingly high temperature without melting -- but a framework of 1/16" square, or smaller balsa can be warped quite easily.

Paul Rodrigues <  > asks:

Can anyone help me with information on performance of the old "OK" engines from the '60's? I have just acquired a new 049; from where the supplier got it I know not, but it looks quite interesting none the less. It has a front intake like the TD's and it would be interesting to know how they compared with the Cox TD 049.

Paul

....Paul, the OK .049 was the second 1/2A model airplane engine on the market, arriving at American hobby shops in the late Spring of 1949. The original version was a long-stroke engine (stroke greater than cylinder bore) and would generally spin the 6-3 wooden props of the 1950's at about 9000 rpm.

A few years later OK changed to a shorter-stroke design, and that's probably what you have. MILLIONS of those engines were made, and when they eventually went off the market in the late 1960's, a considerable number of finished "surplus parts" were put into long- term storage.

Several years ago the son of the OK company's founder decided to assemble these old parts into complete engines and put them back on the hobby market again. Unfortunately, the quality of these "resurrected" engines wasn't up to the original standard...

The short-stroke OK .049 gained about a thousand rpm over the first model -- but it was nowhere nearly in the same performance range as the Tee Dee .049. The Tee Dee can develop almost twice the rpm of the best Cub .049 I've ever seen.

But that doesn't mean the OK Cub is an inferior power plant for flying model airplanes! I personally FAR prefer the Cub for sport free flight flying.

I'd NEVER install a Tee Dee in a Dakota or Sioux -- or even a Comanche. The drawback with excess power in a 1/2A engine is that it limits the places you can fly. High rpm makes for noise complaints! I flew OK Cub -powered free flighters and control- liners for years in residential areas with never a noise complaint.

But after the high-rpm 1/2A trend began (with the Wasp .049 of 1950), it became harder and harder to fly 1/2A models in school- yards without somebody calling the cops. [JW]

....Affonso, think of CO2 motors as being a kind of steam engine. In both, a fluid boils and produces gas under pressure that can be used to move a piston.

Now think about trying to pour water into a red-hot boiler tank. The liquid would flash into steam as soon as it hit the hot metal; and the pressure from that would make further filling both difficult and inefficient, at least until the tank metal cooled enough to permit the water to remain fluid.

Well, that's exactly what happens when you fill a room-temperature CO2 motor tank -- from any source. The liquid CO2 in the cartridge boils at any temperature higher than about 100 degrees F below zero! What prevents it from flashing into a gas in the cartridge itself is pressure. That's around 800 pounds per square inch gage -- commonly abbreviated psig.

When you first start to fill a CO2 motor tank with liquid CO2 from a cartridge, as soon as that liquid touches the tank wall it becomes a gas. That produces back pressure against new inflowing liquid CO2. But at the same time, the gas expansion cools the tank. Soon a state of equilibrium occurs, where the pressure in both the tank and the cartridge are equal.

By the time that happens, there's always a significant amount of gaseous CO2 trapped in the tank. That occupies volume that should have, for maximum motor run, been filled with liquid CO2.

A common technique for getting longer CO2 motor runs is to fill the tank, then run the motor without launching the model. As the tank empties, it gets colder and colder, from the expansion of the liquid CO2 into gas. Then when the motor stops running, a rapid refill of the tank will input a higher liquid content, because with a chilled motor tank not so much of the refill inflow gasifies.

But another problem occurs when the motor tank and the cartridge are of similar capacity. There's just NO WAY of filling more than a fraction of the motor tank before the pressure in that equals the cartridge pressure. Then inflow stops.

That's the reason for the "big and heavy" CO2 filling equipment. If the reservoir contains (say) 2 1/2 pounds of liquid CO2, it can push a far larger quantity of that into a 16 cc tank than could ever be achieved with a paltry little 12-gram cartridge. [JW]
