I'm not quite sure just exactly what Dean Moyer meant by low-cost R/C car equipment in the last posting, but I have been using three 27 MHz receivers for quite a while. A two-channel Hitec Shredder, a two-channel JR, and the new Hitec three-channel receiver that comes with the single stick outfit.

Bruce Stough <  > inquires:

I was given an old SIG 1/6 scale (72 inch wing) Cub J-3 kit, which I have to finish by April or pay $10.00. It is going well, but I am having trouble deciding on an engine. The box for the kit suggests a .25 to .40 2-stroke (the plans, which appear older, call for a .19 to .35). I currently have a .30 Saito 4 stroke, and a PAW .19 diesel.

The Saito is rated at .43 hp and is not known as a tremendously powerful engine, but sounds nice. The PAW (twin bearing) is rated at .5 Hp and shouldn't sound too bad. If these are inadequate, I will probably go to a larger PAW.

I would like to have enough power to fly safely, but not so much that it is not scale-like. The engine will be mounted inverted, which may give the PAW the advantage, since there will be no glow plug to get soaked at idle. If the .19 would be adequate, would a 10x4 or 11x4 prop be good?

The radio will be a HiTec 555 micro receiver, along with two standard servos and two micro/mini servos. The plane will be covered with EasyTex (which is the same as SolarTex). I have no idea what the final weight will be, but I am trying to hold it down. It will be flown from a grass field.

Bruce Stough

....Bruce, Sig's 2-inch-to-the-foot scale J-3 Cub is a direct descen- dant of Chuck Hollinger's scale model, originally published in Air Trails in January, 1955 -- then kitted by Berkeley... The original model was powered by a K&B .19.

Chuck's Cub flew extremely realistically -- and it was controlled with one of the first-model Babcock single channel radios. This was a "hard tube" system, with no less than four separate dry cell battery packs! (The 1.5V "A" battery lit the tube filament; a 45V "B" battery amplified the radio signal; a 15V "C" battery provided "bias voltage" for the tube control grid -- and a 3V battery was used to actuate the "compound escapement" that moved the rudder and "kick-up elevator".)

The R/C system in Chuck's .19-powered "Cub" weighed about 18 ounces. Yet the airplane flew just fine -- even as a seaplane with scale Edo pontoons to haul through the air too. Chuck told me that he thought his Cub looped better as a seaplane.

In view of all this, I'd say that the PAW .19 would fly your "Cub" in an exceedingly scale-like way. As for the propeller size, that would depend on the type you use. There can be a great difference in thrust output between various brands of props, of the same nominal diameter and pitch. Only flight testing will tell you which works best. [JW]

Adam Bryant <  > asks:

I recently purchased the ACE Simple Series Extra 230. This kit comes with a foam wing that is very thin and according to the instructions does not get sheeted. A balsa trailing edge is added to the wing and the instructions call for a strip of strapping tape to be run from end to end on the wing at the thickest point of the airfoil.

I would like to add some additional strength to this wing, and was wondering if anyone had ever experimented with Tyvek as a sheeting material? If you are not familiar with the brand name, Tyvek is the paperlike product used by all the overnight shipping companies for their large envelopes. It is very thin, white in color, and you can see fibers in the material itself. It is extremely strong and very resistant to tearing. It also seems to be very light weight.

My thought was to go ahead and add the balsa trailing edges to the foam wings; then use a spray adhesive like 3M 77 to glue the Tyvek to the foam wings. I shouldn't need the strapping tape as the Tyvek will add way more strength than the strapping tape would have.

Thoughts? Suggestions? Experiences?

Adam Bryant Parker, Colorado

....Adam, it seems to me that somebody recommended Tyvek for model covering purposes in an early SMALLnet Posting. As I recall, the advice was to "split" the material, to provide a fibrous inner surface for glue to bond to -- white or aliphatic glue was the recommended adhesive, I think.

In any case, it's always a good idea to test out new techniques on "sample specimens" before risking them on a model. [JW]

Roy Clough <  > contributed:

Mark Winstanley asked about radio glitches due to covering materials. I built a small .020 Cox job, single channel, (Fail- Safe radio on 27 MHz), covered the fuselage with white MicaFilm, and left the antenna inside.

The model would stay in control for a couple of hundred feet, then I'd lose it. After reading somewhere that MicaFilm had RF shielding possibilities, I led the antenna outside and it seemed to work fine after that.

Also, where we fly, from an inactive runway at Laconia airport, several of us have had problems in one particular spot which we jokingly (?) say has "bad vibes." Others blame distant microwave dishes. Test equipment has failed to show any source of spurious radiation, but three of us have planes go haywire in this area.

I read a piece in the paper tonight discussing the solar maximum that is due any day now. Many big scale problems have been caused by it. Would be interesting to see if the problem gets worse this year. Might be fun when anybody has an unexplained phenomenon to report here in SMALLnet -- and six months down the road somebody could review the data and look for correlation.

Speaking of razor blades: The best I ever used were the old Schick injector single sided carbon steel blades. They came 10 or a dozen in a cartridge you poked into the side of the razor to insert a new blade and push out the old one. I'd hold the blade at an angle in flat jaw pliers and snap it off at an angle with a quick blow. They'd fit an X-acto handle. These were the best I've ever used. When Schick changed over to stainless blades, they'd usually bend instead of snap, and I quit using them.

Roy C.

....Roy, a friend of mine in the Bath, New York area flies R/C from a field with a "dead spot" similar to what you described.

I've seen the same phenomenon myself at a model flying site in southern California, and another in Pennsylvania. The explanation given for this problem was that a large radio-signal-reflective metal structure near the field was responsible.

When flying in the "glitch region", the antenna in the model would receive "out of phase" simultaneous radio frequency signals: one directly from the transmitter; the other "bounced" from the distant "reflector". Together, these conflicting signals would cause momentary loss of control.

But when the airplane passed out of the "critical zone", its radio system functioned normally again. [JW]
