Dennis < Denoferth@aol.com > contributed:

Gene Smith asked if anyone had success converting Guillow WWI biplane kits to R/C. That brought back memories. Around 1962 or '63 I built a Guillow Nieuport 17 and a Fieseler Fi-156 Storch, and powered both with Cox .010's.

The smallest receiver back then was the Otarion, made by the hearing aid people. The Galloping Ghost actuator was a little red plastic-bodied one about 1\2" in diameter, but I don't recall its name offhand. Two button cell NiCads powered the whole shebang, and it was as small as today's gear.

Unfortunately, it didn't have enough power to overcome air loads without non-scale horns on the rudder, which worked with a pull- pull system guided with a Min-X transmitter. Basically I had a free-flight plane that could be controlled after the engine quit.

Still it was fun -- but short-lived as the .010 was a little hot and the planes a little heavy. All that is left is the Cox still mounted on the FI-156 firewall. My advice would be to use the Guillow drawings, but exchange the kit wood for lighter stuff, and with modern radio gear it should be a piece of cake.

Dennis in New Hampshire

Tim Dannels < ecj@chaffee.net > submitted:

Having been out of model-building for longer than I care to admit, getting back into it with the "modern" materials is, at the least, challenging. Being an old silk-and-tissue man from the beginning, I find the new iron-on coverings "different" at best -- lousy at worst!

Yeah, some of the effects one can achieve with iron-ons are downright fantastic -- especially the transparent coverings that really intensify the light shining through a wing. However, it is more than a bit intimidating to try and figure out what all of these various types and brands are for.

Heck, you used to use Jap Tissue for the small ones, Silkspan for the medium sizes, and silk (or sometimes nylon) for large free flight models or hot control-line combat models. When it came to applying trim stripes or letters or such, you just laid them in place and doped them on. Never a problem with keeping them from sliding around.

And compound curves such as wingtips and cowlings were a snap with wet silk or tissue. If it didn't go down right, a little thinner broke it loose and gave you a second (or third) chance at re-positioning.

I have just finished a struggle with Coverlite, and, if I could get it off, I would. It wrinkled and would not "shrink" smooth all over. The yellow does look like tissue OK, but the white looks like bond paper! I'm sure I've done something wrong, but can't figure out what.

I need practice, I guess. And if I want to add some trim or numbers or such, the instructions say I have to re-coat the area with the Balsarite. Which means if you put numbers on the wing, you have to paint it with that stuff. It's like trying to spread gorilla snot! Still the Coverlite just won't stick to anything unless it is coated with the stuff -- even itself.

What little bit of Monokote I've used seems to stick to plain wood OK, but still has trouble wrapping around the curves of a cowling or fuselage or wing tip.

Problem is that they have "improved" the model dopes today -- undoubtedly due to some regulations -- where these finished don't behave like they used to. Which means you are more or less stuck with the iron-on stuff.

Just had to get that off my chest. These coverings all come highly-recommended in the magazines, so it has to be something I'm doing. I'll whip it yet!

Tim Dannels - 8000 feet up in the Colorado Rockies.

...Tim, working from the bottom of your submission upwards, I can provide some enlightenment on your problems.

First, model magazines NEVER publish anything detrimental about products! Model Aviation censored a remark of mine in a recent column that said the GHQ engine was a hopeless dud.

There's a reason for this. Advertisers provide most of the magazines' income -- and those guys are really sensitive. Three advertisers cancelled their ad contracts with MAN because of me while I was a regular contributor -- and I never even mentioned their companies or their products! (These people felt that I had implied bad things about them.)

In view of this, DON'T depend on model magazine product reviews etc. for comprehensive information. They present the "pro's" -- but never the "con's".

Next: today's model dopes haven't been "improved" so much as they've been cheapened. I could go into detail, because a few years ago I researched this topic in depth. But all I'll say here is that "modern practice" for dopes is to thin their "vehicle" (resins and pigments) with JUST ENOUGH of the good old "volatile solvents" (such as acetone) to produce a kind of soft slurry. Then that slurry is thinned to brushing consistency with a cheap, derived-from-petroleum thinner such as Xylene and Toluol.

The fumes from those are TOXIC. (Voice of experience.)

The dope that Brodak sells is the best I know of. It's made by Randolph. They've been manufacturing dope for fabric-covered airplanes continuously since 1919. Sig's dope isn't as good.

For covering purposes, use only plain nitrate (NOT the low- shrink type: it lacks the adhesion qualities of standard nitrate) for attachment and for the first two BRUSHED-ON sealer coats.

After that, butyrate dope topcoats can be applied. (All colored dope nowadays is butyrate.)

BTW, AeroGloss isn't really "dope" any more. It's now an acrylic lacquer, and doesn't behave anything like the original AeroGloss.

There have been changes made in the "old-fashioned coverings" too, Tim. I won't use modern Silkspan ever again. Some alteration in its manufacture -- maybe the "sizing" -- causes it to become horridly brittle within a year or two after the dope cures.

As for silk, the best present source is Thai Silks! -- but even their lightest-grade silk has been woven with "clothing-type" techniques: pre-shrinking and sizing. The sizing does make Thai Silks! material easier to handle and cut than the old "soft" silk that was such a pleasure to use in he Good Old Days for covering compound-curved fuselages.

Yes, Thai Silks! CAN be used to cover models. But the optimum technique is different than the old silk required. (An article telling just how to do that is on the Special Edition SMALLnet CD that I made up for the 2001 Maumelle SMALL meet.) [JW]

There have been a lot of comments recently on "rudder-only" R/C flying; some negative and others positive. But some of the happiest flying days I ever had were with my rudder-only planes.

I have been reading this column for several years now. A close friend forwarded the Postings to me at work for a couple of years, when all I had available was the computer there. (Thanks, John.) I have now moved into the 20th Century by getting my own home computer -- and my first move was to subscribe to "SMALLnet".

This is my first submission, and it concerns small diesel engines. The ones I have had the most experience with are Cox reed valve motors converted with Davis Diesel Development's conversion kit.

I have found the .049 conversion to be absolutely superb. You must, however, order and use the heavy-duty crank, and follow the break-in procedure with care. If you don't, the ball socket in the piston gets loose, and the piston "hammers" the crankpin until it breaks.

When I'm breaking in a new engine, I'm careful not to overcompress it. I also run an 8-6!! nylon Top Flite propeller on it to keep the rpm's down! After one 8 cc tank (Golden Bee size), I disassemble the engine and re-set the rod. Two more tanks are then run through, and the rod is re-set again. After this, the rod is checked after each quart of fuel is run through it.

My first engine is going strong and has had two gallons of fuel run through it, turning an 8-6 nylon Top Flite at 8600 rpm with 4- to 5-minute flights on the Golden Bee tank! For what it's worth, I've tried one of the RJL conversion heads on a new Medallion .049, and after 8 minutes of running, it broke the crank!

I am pretty sure that if I had followed my own advice about break-in, the engine would not have been destroyed.

With such good luck on the Cox conversions, I purchased a PAW .049 R/C from Eric Clutton. I wanted to try a 3-channel Ace Whizard. This was a very successful combination except I could never get the throttle to work very well. "Idle" was about 6500 rpm's, and the idea of touch and go's with that airplane just didn't work out.

I have three of the PAW's (.049, .09, and a .19), and all of them have the airbleed carburetors. All of them exhibit the same type of behavior, with the lowest idle speed obtainable being about 65% of full throttle. I'm going to talk to Eric Clutton about them this year at Toledo!

...Chief, in past Postings we've covered "diesel technique" in great depth. PAW throttle problems were explored and solutions for them explained in detail.

The main difficulty with R/C PAW's is maintaining proper orien- tation of the two-hole spraybar. That can change all too easily. I routinely rework all my PAW carbs to eliminate such problems. (Explained in past Postings.)

My R/C type PAW's, from .031 through .19, normally idle well under 4000 rpm -- and with a 4- or 5-step needle-and-compression adjustment sequence to optimize the settings, will idle reliably under 3000 rpm -- except in "non-temperate weather". [JW]

Richard DeAngelis < rickde@optonline.net > asks:

I think SMALLnet is the best forum on e-planes and small planes. I appreciate your advice -- even to the slimy, greasy, goopy, gunky, sticky, diesel gang. But would like to see more e-stuff.

SMALLnet #408 stated in a response: "More recently, I told Expert Craftsman Dave Palmer about a brown-paper-and-acrylic-medium process for making strong, light moldings."

Are these paper moldings appropriate for smaller electric planes? Where can I get this information on paper and acrylic moldings?

Rick DeAngelis, Fairfield, Connecticut

...Rick, the method that I've been using for quite a while is not exactly new. Boat modelers have built lightweight, strong, thin hulls this way for maybe 75 years. My method differs mostly in the adhesive used.

Everything I've done so far in this line has been molded over a "male" form. Usually I only need ONE part, and it's easy to make a male mold out of white foam. I salvage that from electronic packaging inserts -- gluing sections of that together when added thickness is needed.

I apply the paper-and-adhesive directly over the foam; then melt out the "mold" carefully with a heat gun after the part has been laid up, cured, and sanded smooth.

But if I needed several identical molded parts -- say cowls for a 4-engined model -- I'd make a hardwood mold, and use the "inside-out balloon" method of applying a non-greasy "parting agent".

The materials I prefer are brown Kraft paper -- the plain untreated type, NOT the water-resistant kind that some super- markets use in their grocery bags -- and artists' acrylic "Gloss Medium". (Matte Medium isn't as strong; and a mixture of Gesso and Medium, though it fills in the crevices nicely, cures out rather brittle and HEAVY.)

I do NOT use "white glue" nor "aliphatic resin". The first isn't waterproof; the second cures brittle.

Acrylic Medium of the PROPER KIND becomes as waterproof as dope after it dries -- and retains its flexibility a very long time. However (probably for "artistic purposes") some brands of acrylic -- such as Liquitex -- do NOT cure out hard. Therefore some testing is necessary. (My own favorite brand was Hyplar, by Grumbacher. It's off the market now; luckily I still have some left.)

I wet the brown paper thoroughly. That allows it to stretch a little, and conform to curves. That way I can use fairly large pieces, and provide a smoother "as molded" outer surface that needs less sanding. (Silkspan can also be used, but I found that the finished molding comes out heavier, because it soaks up more Medium. It also needs more sanding to achieve a smooth surface.)

I apply ONE layer of paper at a time. Overlaps are OK as long as they're not wider than maybe 1/4". Then I let the paper/ acrylic dry out completely (a couple or three hours in a normal "warm, dry environment" is enough) before applying another layer. Three layers will do for some parts; I've even gotten by with two for a part like a turtledeck.

Practice makes perfect, and that's neither expensive nor time- consuming when you use salvaged brown paper and white foam. Only the Acrylic Medium is at all costly. Dick Blick is the best mail-order (and on-line) source I can recommend, but you must specify when you order that you need HARD-drying Acrylic Gloss Medium.

For applying the Medium to the mold and paper, I use white- bristled, rather stiff artist's brushes, made specially for use with acrylic paint. Those last much longer than, say, "camel's hair" and similar organic-bristle brushes; and can be used with hard pressure to "squeegee" the Medium well down against the under- lying mold and deep into the pores of the paper. [JW]

Orval Masse < orval.masse@sympatico.ca > proposes:

I found a way to unseize a rusted or gummed-up engine. Put the engine in a pot of boiling water that has vinegar added to it. Boil the engine till it will turn over. I had a Cox .020 that was gummed up and would not turn over; now it runs great.

Also, before boiling remove gaskets and reed to avoid damage. I had an Enya .60 that was badly-rusted and the boiling worked on that engine too.

I think the boiling softens the gunk and the vinegar worked on the rust. Remember not to force the engine while turning it over. Disassemble and dry after boiling, then lubricate well.

Orval
