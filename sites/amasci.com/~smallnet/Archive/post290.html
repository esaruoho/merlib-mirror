The queries about Tyvek have brought in several responses. Ian L. McQueen <  > starts off with:

Adam Bryant asked about using Tyvek on foam wings. I am building a house, and have plenty of the stuff. After a previous mention of Tyvek here or on FFML, I brought scraps home to look at.

The Tyvek I have does not split neatly down the middle. One side or the other delaminated and came off in pieces. I understand that the material used for envelopes will separate in the desired manner.

"Insulation Tyvek" has small, visible holes that allow water vapor to pass through, while it blocks rain and wind. The envelope material is designed to be waterproof, and thus has a continuous surface.

The envelopes that RCM used to mail their magazine in has thicker material and feels softer. I believe that there is more loose fiber inside, which presumably allows the outer faces to be peeled apart (and glue to stick better).

My sample of Tyvek house wrap weighs 72 grams/square meter (0.24 ounces/square foot). Ian M

Chuck Kriete <  > contributed:

The specifications for the Flutterbug are nearly exactly the same as for the Modelair Tech LeParquewatt I have been flying for a year now, and the performance is almost exactly that described. Mine weighs a little more (20 ounces) and gets about a minute less on a charge. I have also crashed it enough to have rebuilt the front of the fuselage with blue foam and strapping tape.

Reference strapping tape, IT WORKS. I am amazed at how much strength a 3/4 inch "loop" of strapping tape laid spanwise (care- fully, so as not to stretch it -- which will warp the wing and give you elliptical dihedral) will give a 1/4 inch thick fan-fold extruded polystyrene wing. The trick is to spray a light coat of 3M 77 contact cement along the line you want to lay the tape. Trust me, mine survive crashes.

On Tyvek, Don Ross touts it in his first book. It is easy to split once you get it started, and can be bought plain in most Office Max- type stores. The hard part is rubbing off the fuzz to glue it to the model surface. I use it to reinforce leading and trailing (sometimes) edges of foam wings, and in other places for reinforcement. It is also great cut into small triangles to reinforce strategic balsa joints.

Chuck Kriete

Kirk Kirkham <  > adds:

Adam Bryant's item on Tyvek caught the eye. Parker, Colorado? Heck, that's just up the road a piece.

My first experience with Tyvek was 25 years ago when the state used it for hunting licenses. More recently, control-line flyers have begun using it for hinge material. It just doesn't seem to weaken or deteriorate with frequent bending. We're still not sure about the long-term effects of fuel wicking into the material and weakening the glue. But TiteBond seems impervious to fuel. Only time will tell.

I've never used it as a covering material, but use it all the time for reinforcement, for access door hinges, etc. Any adhesive seems to work for the same reason fountain pens -- remember them? -- don't write well on the stuff. It's like a blotter, despite its appearance.

There is a lightweight grade of Tyvek, about half the weight of USPS Priority Mail envelopes, that ought to make an almost indestructible covering.

Kirk Kirkham, Limon, Colorado

Roy Clough <  > admits:

I've used Tyvek stuff for several years -- in fact it covered the wing of

my Faker D-8 (Model Aviation, November 1998). Tyvek can be heat shrunk with a heat gun, but the difference between drum tight and a hole melted through it is very very few degrees. I use any of the white or yellow wood glues, and it stays put.

I also use Tyvek when I've fit together the shattered pieces of a balsa fuselage, again white glue. Takes paint OK. I always use it as a binder for a plywood firewall to balsa or foamboard fuselages. It also makes good low-friction hinges for small models. Iron-on film sticks well.

Good tip on the radio glitching, JW, it makes some sense. Our "bad vibes" occur near a cluster of metal Tee hangars--but not always, so the angle the plane approaches them must have something to do with it.

Roy C.

Dean Moyer <  > clarifies his earlier query:

To Harry and others who might be interested: I am speaking about $10 to $20 R/C cars. Their radios do have limited range, and model airplanes that use them are flown almost exclusively indoors.

Obviously the control is not proportional. Transmitters and receivers are used with magnetic actuators one of our guys winds, for rudder and elevator control. Motor, rudder and elevator are simple or/off controls.

With motor only, that allows some control of altitude and turn circle. The $20 Tyco in a can provides the 3 controls. One of our members has developed both R/C and Infra Red Control proportional for very light indoor models; for example the Thistle.

You can contact Len Paciorek at <  > for more data, or Clarence Hurd at <  > about the proportional R/C. Dean from Central New York Indoor Flyers.

Mark Wayne <  > responds:

In response to Adam Bryant, I have built several Simple Ace kits. Strapping tape adds the necessary strength to the wing. The only wing that ever actually broke in half was the first one I ever made, and I forgot to put the strapping tape on both sides of the wing (top and bottom). Live and learn.

I have covered all of my foam wings with EconoKote. This is a relatively low-temperature iron-on covering material that holds nicely on the foam without melting it when applied. One of the planes I have is the Simple CAP 231. It has the same wing as the Extra. I have an OS 15 FP for power, which I think is perfect. The plane flies great. After 40-50 flights, I have ripped out the landing gear (I added) twice landing in grass. The wing tips are a bit scraped, but no breaks. I have an Ace Simple Ultimate with over 300 flights on it, with no wing problems.

Regards, Mark

Fred Reese <  > sums up:

Adam Bryant did not say what engine he planned to use on his Simple Series Extra. Unless you are going to use a .10 or larger, the strapping tape is adequate reinforcement for the Ace mini foam wing. The tape is recommended because it is strong enough, and is much lighter than the other options you mentioned.

The Ace mini foam wing is small in area, so weight was my primary consideration when designing the Simple Series. When I started the Simple Series, the Cox .049-051 was the only readily available engine. Now there are more choices. I have also flown different models of the Simple Series with Norvel .061s, a TD .09, and Thunder Tiger .10, all with the strapping tape wing. The wings bend in high-G turns, but I have never broken one, not in 25 years of using them.

I guess the choice is between looking better on the ground, or flying better. Fred Reese
