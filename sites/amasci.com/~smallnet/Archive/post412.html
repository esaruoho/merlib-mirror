Dave Campbell < cdcampb@ismi.net > submitted:

Sam Brauer compared the performance and costs of the Astro 010G, Speed 300, and Speed 280BB motors.

The game you play in e-powered models is one of tradeoffs. You juggle performance and run time to get the compromise that suits your flying style. Sam "didn't want to talk about efficiency", but the lower ampere draw of the 010 is where your performance lies, because that's your untapped reserve.

You have to play with props, gearing, cell count etc. When the 010 pulls as much current as the 280BB, I guarantee that it will be turning a lot faster. Also consider that the 280BB can't be run for long on 10 cells, and that the 720 cells you use can't handle those kinds of currents very well.

600AE's would be your next best bet, but those weigh more. That means that you are better off propping up or gearing taller to bring the current (and watts/horsepower) up.

Good Luck, Dave

Frank Weaver < goldguy@shaw.ca > wrote:

For cold weather starting and running of diesels, 2% amyl nitrate seems to do the trick. The opposite seems to be true of hot weather: less or none at all is best. Diesels do not like hot summer days anyway!

One of my fellow modelers modifies a Cox spring starter to fit his PAW 55 and it works great.

Me, I just get out my 1/2A starter. With the engine set very under-compressed and only TWO drops of fuel for prime, choke just enough to bring the fuel to the carb. Flip twice (at half- throttle), hit with the starter and SLOWLY up the compression.

Off with the starter as soon as it kicks and de-compress instantly. Let it sit there and PAW, PAW away to warm up, full throttle, up the compression and adjust the needle for smooth running. With diesels, once you get the needle setting you should not have to touch it again. A tach is very helpful with setting up diesels.

The rest of the day I can start my 55 with just the starter at idle, no prime, no adjustments. Amazing!!!!! And to think some modelers shy away from diesels because they think they are hard to start.

On all of my larger PAW diesels on which I never use a starter, I have had to change the rod/pin assembly shortly after being well broken in. On my PAW 55 which has approximately 200 flights and started many times with a starter, I have had no signs of excessive wear and have never replaced any parts.

One other important consideration is the alignment of the two holes in the spray bar. If you missed Dave Larkin's comments, check though the bottom of the carb to see that both holes are visible at full throttle through the barrel. If they are not, expect one hour of fiddling to get it right. I take out the stop screw, turn everything 180 degrees, and do it through the top. The hole positions are easier to see that way.

Next to my MP Jets 061, the PAW 55 is the easiest starting of ALL my engines, including glow.

Fly SMALL, have a ball.

Frank Weaver -- The Balsa Beaver

Dave Larkin < dslarkin@blvl.igs.net > responds to a variety of queries and topics:

There's no special fuel necessary for diesels in winter. Just go to the field and be first in the air. I've known people to use two engines in their planes, swapping out the glow for a diesel for winter flying.

When flying off a lake in Nova Scotia with my club, and using an old ED Bee in a Tomboy with an ACE Pulse Commander R/C system, I could get the Bee going while everybody else was playing around with blowtorches, car exhausts, etc. Remember that the active ingredient in car-starting spray is ether.

Yes, you can use model aircraft diesel fuel to get your glow motor going just as quickly! I've never seen a need to butcher an engine just for winter use. You should certainly be able to fly in temperatures down to 20 degrees (C) below.

George, I would be strongly inclined not to modify the Mambo's stabilizer. In any case it would be no big deal to retrofit a modified tail. My experience is that lifting stabilizers in rudder-only planes minimize 'zooming' and help penetration into wind.

I did have one rudder-only model go straight in, but think that I let the tail incidence get too close to the wing incidence. I froze when it started the dive, and never thought of the old trick of alternately applying left and right rudder to lift the nose.

Despite impacting on a concrete runway, the Jackdaw lived again to teach me 6-channel reed flying, but with the addition of elevators.

The model was a FROG Jackdaw, with a symmetrical stabilizer. I just found the plans for this plane, and there isn't much incidence difference between the wing and the stabilizer. There may have been even less in my model, as I was using a lighter motor than the plane was designed to take.

The design had had a great contest record with rudder and throttle control, but I never flew mine enough that way to give an assessment -- and besides I was very much a beginner.

(I was, I think, using a Kraft receiver and Graupner Unimatic servos.) There is quite a bit of downthrust. I do know that as I progressed to reeds with Kraft and Bonner equipment, I destroyed vast numbers of 10 X 3 1/2 inch Top Flite nylon props with ground loops. I projected my rate of loss and bought accordingly; consequently still have plenty!

Why I didn't just hand launch is beyond me. I probably thought a 60-inch span plane with an Enya .19 was just too big!

At that time I was flying with a friend who had a Mambo, but he was experimenting with home-built Galloping Ghost with little success. There were other Mambos flying at a nearby club, as far as I know quite well, using escapements. But when I showed up at their field with reeds (by this time in a Bob Palmer Gee String) there was a revolution and all the Mambos soon disappeared. It was like selling whisky to the natives.

Mind you, I don't think I told them that a spectacular spiral dive was caused by my losing orientation for a moment.

Dennis, Your comment on the Master plane leaves me seriously disturbed. I had one and didn't like it and now I'm wondering... Anyway, I've since discovered the David Plane, made in The Netherlands, which is a far superior device and is something no modeller should be without. I used to stock them, but the folks at Aeroenvironment found out I had them and cleaned out my stock. Must re-order sometime. There's a distributor in California, and I think SIG also stock them.

Best regards, Dave Larkin

Mike Gillihan < GillihanMike@JohnDeere.com > contributed:

I have been playing with the 1.5 mil Doculam, and so far I am very impressed. Most of you already know Doculam makes a great, lightweight covering that is undeniably among the least-expensive model covering materials on the planet.

The 1.5 mil version seems amply strong enough for fast 1/2A models, and weighs much less than MonoKote. The material seems flimsy at first, but once heated with an iron becomes quite resilient.

I've recently taken to covering plans with Doculam. I spread the plans out over my building board, roll the material over the plans (overlapping the edges of the plans), and iron the whole lot down to the board. Every glue that I've tried peels off the material easily; and when finished building, I simply peel the whole lot right off the board.

Once the plans are peeled from the board, I trim the Doculam to the edge of the paper, and end up with a nicely-laminated plan that can be rolled up for later use. Magic. Note that the stuff looks "frosty" prior to ironing down, but goes clear when heat is applied. Doculam handles like MonoKote, and your iron should be set to about the same temperature.

I ordered 1000 square feet of the stuff (two 12-inch by 500-foot rolls) for less than $30, delivered. That works out to less than three cents per square foot! I have also heard that Doculam can be lightly spray-painted on the inside surface, or colored with permanent magic marker prior to ironing on, without affecting the adhesive (I have not tried this yet).

I'd like to toss in my two cents worth on this apparently wide- spread practice of using anti-freeze in a crockpot to clean model engines. A friend of mine had a nice old Pierce .29 cleaned that way, and the result was a clean but very dark- stained engine. The stain is permanent and affected the cast (aluminum alloy, I suppose) crankcase.

Perhaps the Pierce's case is made of an alloy with a lot of zinc in it. Whatever, the engine definitely does not look as it should after this treatment. I have had excellent results using Zip-Strip Paint and Clear Finish Remover. I brush it on, wait 30 minutes, and then scrub it off with a toothbrush and warm water. Really stubborn cases generally require a second application.

I have never had an engine damaged in any way by this method. Caution: It no doubt will attack the painted finish on parts such as old K&B Greenhead cylinder heads. Zip-Strip works very much like that great old Sunbeam Metal-Kleen stuff (long since banned by the EPA and ??).

A number of us bought the Cox Droid RTF C-L "planes" a year or so back from Tower on their closeout sale just for the Surestart .049 engines. I found that the two I got were reluctant to start initially and that the rod-to-piston fit was very loose. I gave them the calibrated swats with a tack hammer and the Cox rod- resetting tool, and was rewarded with excellent runners which start easily. These seem to be very satisfactory engines in all respects after the rod tightening.

The "plane" is something else. It's as close to unflyable as anything ever sold as an RTF. Anyone out there get one to fly? I'm still reasonably proficient in C-L stunting, but I couldn't get this thing to just go around without wild pitching motions which always resulted in a crash. I can offer one positive comment about the "plane" -- it's rugged!

Phil Oestricher

Those lacking patience for stewing engines in 50% antifreeze might wish to try the brute-force approach of putting roughly two tablespoons of dishwasher detergent in a half-filled 3-lb coffee can of water and bringing it to a boil.

(Note: That's dishWASHER, not dishwashing detergent.)

Disassemble the engine as much as possible and pop it into the boiling water. Let it rip for about 5 minutes and then rinse very thoroughly. Final disassembly (if needed) and another trip through the process is usually enough to make things like new. Use scalding hot water for the rinse -- that will heat the metal and make things dry out faster.

A note of caution: boiling dishwasher detergent is caustic. Avoid prolonged skin contact and protect your eyes. Don't boil the engine for long periods of time, and do be sure to rinse away all traces of the detergent.

After rinsing and drying, a water-displacing lubricant (especially around bearings and the wrist pin) followed by liberal application of your favorite after-run oil is recommended.

Richard Pawloski asked about equivalents to the 1524 motor. A widely used replacement for the DC1524 is a GWS IPS DX- (version suited for your application).

The IPS motors come in a variety of gear ratios to suit the correct propeller size and pitch for the model they power, as the DC1524 also comes in 2 ratios for prop size fitting.

Example: I used a GWS DX-A and 10x4.7 GWS prop in my MINI=IFO with a 7.2V Lithium battery, and it flies great. When I tried to use the same combination on the full-size IFO, the motor overheated. So I went to the GWS DX-D with 12x8 GWS prop, and it really flies great. After 20 minutes the motor is barely warm. VERY IMPORTANT: Use a heatsink with any of the IPS motors regardless of the application.

You can find data on the DC1524 here: < http://www.toddsmodels.com/corelessmotors.htm > < http://www.homefly.com >

You can find data on the GWS IPS here: < http://www.gws.com.tw/english/product/powersystem/ips.htm >
