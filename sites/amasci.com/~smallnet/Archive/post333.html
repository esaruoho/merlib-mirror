...Back safe & sound from the all-Dakota event at Muncie. I'd say that it was as much fun for me, for the brief time it took, as I've had at many a SMALL meet. I won't inflict a full report on you here -- but here's a brief summary.

There were something like a dozen models flown. Most had .049's up front, and climbed swiftly. None were lost, regardless of altitude gain: the Dakota's high-drag airfoil brought the high flyers back to earth with great reliability.

Bob Langelius (from White Plains, New York -- he's the man who had replicas of the K&B Infant .020 manufactured a few years ago) flew a lovely painted-like-a-scale-model Infant-powered Dakota. I brought along a 36-year-old Pee-Wee .020-powered model -- and Bob's and my airplanes made the most impressive-to-spectators flights.

Bob's was lighter than mine, and flew in almost rubber-powered style. Bob launched his plane exactly as if it were an Earl Stahl R-P scale model, with just a gentle push. Because of the wind direction, both he and I had to launch towards the "pits", where a mob of spectators had gathered to observe the fun. No one was endangered: our planes climbed reliably, and didn't fly fast.

...Though I hadn't planned it, things worked out with my own Dakota to make its three flights at Muncie demonstrate the design's strong points dramatically. Flight # 1 lasted mere seconds, because I launched away from the spectators that time, and didn't heave hard enough to make up for the downwind direction. The plane hit the ground under power & cartwheeled. No damage; not even a broken prop.

My second flight exhibited the typical Dakota pattern: TIGHT left-circling climb and a no-altitude-loss transition to a right glide turn. With maybe a 25-second engine run, I was able to walk downwind after my model and recover it myself.

For the third attempt the "gang" talked me into letting the engine run a while -- one of the Skyscraper club waited on his motorcycle to retrieve my plane for me. Alan Abriss (Brooklyn Skyscraper president) stood by with his big professional-grade videocamera to record the flight -- and I got stage fright or something & made a clumsy launch.

The Dakota stalled and sank; tipped way over on its left wing(s); then the mighty Pee Wee's power took over and the model flew a couple of circles no more than eight feet in diameter and less than a foot off the ground! (The bank angle was close to 80 degrees -- yet the airplane's nose never dropped.) After that, the little biplane spiraled on up "in the usual fashion". I think that Dakota was the tightest-circling engine-powered free flight most of the folks at the field had ever seen...

Alan said that he got the whole thing on tape. "I did that specially for your film," I modestly admitted. [JW]

Pat Tritle <  > addresses some of the recent adverse comment in SMALLnet Postings on Dare kits:

About the Dare Triplane kit. I hope I can explain how the kit came to be, and steps taken later to improve the situation.

When these designs were first discussed we decided on a couple of things early on. First, we thought the types of models we intended to do would be avoided by beginners, and that designing a line of "advanced" kits was the way to go. Boy were we wrong! Low-time builders bought these things like there was no tomorrow.

As a result there arose two basic comments. First was, of course, "No instructions!". The second criticized the wing drawings - only one side shown. Remarkably, the complaints have been lower than I would have expected.

As a result of the feedback, all of the new kit releases include full wing plans, plus radio and power system details (both for installation/location, and recommended equipment). Also, we are slowly but surely getting instructions written for the older releases.

In addition, details like machine guns and wheels are being added to the existing plans. Since these models were never intended to be anything but "schoolyard scale", higher-level details were not included on the plans -- but can be added by those who seek that type of detailing.

I don't fully understand the complaints concerning the quality of the wood used in Dare kits, or the drawings. However, I will be happy to discuss any problems, and do whatever I can to solve them, and to improve future Dare kit offerings. (E-mail me directly.)

As to the "pirating" of the old Flyline kits, nothing could be farther from the truth. I designed both of these models from the ground up, and there are no interchangeable parts from one to the other. Originally the intent WAS to re-issue the old Flyline designs, and arrangements were made to that end; although all-new designs were ultimately used for a variety of reasons.

Pat Tritle, Pat's Custom Models

Sam Brauer <  > reports:

I've put a couple of flights on my Dare Sopwith Triplane over the weekend -- just a few circuits around the field, but it's clear this airplane is a winner. The airplane flies slowly and majestically -- very similar to the 1 foot = 1 foot versions I've seen up at Rhinebeck. Since the airplane is 1/9th scale and probably flies between 12 and 15 mph, perhaps this realism isn't so surprising.

The airplane is very easy to fly; there's just a high pucker factor (high emotional debt factor) associated with first flights, but it's clear that nearly anybody should be able to fly this airplane. Building it on the other hand.....

Anyhow, here are the specifications: Overall weight is probably 16-17 ounces (my kitchen scale is giving up the ghost) with an MTM S-300 motor with an 8:1 reduction drive turning an 11 x 7 APC electric prop. The motor uses a Castle Creations Pixie 7 controller and a 6 cell, 350 mAh nicad pack. Radio gear is two FMA S-80 servos and an FMA Fortress micro receiver, with a Multiplex Cockpit Transmitter (no glitches so far!).

One annoying trait of the speed control is that once the servos are waggled, it seems to throw enough juice to the receiver to keep the prop turning. Haven't figured that out yet. Covering is Balsa Products Litespan. I went to a set of 2 1/4 inch wire wheels found at my local hobby dealers to dress up the airplane. They're made in Czechoslovakia and cost $20, but they're light and definitely add to the charm of the airplane. I also used a Major Decals 1/12 scale WWI sheet. These look OK on a flat surface, but they've wrinkled on the top wing. Larger decals wouldn't fit on the airplane real well.

This combination gives between 2 to 3 minutes of full throttle use, which sounds like very little flying time. However, the airplane takes off quite readily at half throttle, and cruises around on very little power. I suspect flights will be in the 7-8 minute range once I quit being so nervous.

Takeoffs involve going to full down to lift the tail (how often do we get to do this?) and gently advancing the throttle. One of the advantages of the electric is you don't have to worry about saggy mid-ranges on engines...

Ground-looping has not been much of a problem at all. I've got a couple of degrees of toe-in on the wheels and I'm taking off asphalt. I've been getting the airplane off in 50 feet or so, but you could use more power and probably do it in 5. Glide path control is easy, if you're going to overshoot, dive the airplane a little. Since it's so draggy (I left off the flying wires, it's still draggy), this works.

So if you like building, and want a small WWI airplane that looks very realistic in the air, I can recommend this kit. While I haven't seen Pat's Triplane fly, the powerplant in mine is clearly a good match for the airframe.

Sam Brauer, Norwalk, Connecticut

Al Lidberg <  > puts his oar in the water with:

Regarding the Sopwith triplane, someone wrote: "By the way, for those who think four-strokes sound "realistic" for such planes, I can tell you that the sound of a nine-cylinder rotary whizzing and buzzing and blasting out clouds of castor oil has absolutely nothing in common with the laid-back putt-putt of a model four-stroke."

The 'brrrt', 'brrrt', etc. effect of a rotary is more easily achieved with a spark ignition engine, especially one with marginal plug and floating points. A similar effect is possible with undercompressed diesels. If you add some extra oil to either one, the effect is even closer, as you'll find out at clean-up time!

A BRIEF HISTORY OF THE NORSEMAN

The Norseman was the first aircraft specifically designed as a bush plane for the Canadian north, with a useful payload and STOL capability to land and take off from primitive fields and from small lakes. The aircraft is a high-wing cabin monoplane, powered by a 9-cylinder Pratt & Whitney of 550 hp. Cruising speed is a leisurely 134 mph (215 km/h). Wingspan is 51' (15.5m). It first flew in 1934, and the aircraft has seen service all through the North, both in Canada and Alaska, as well as in many other countries around the world, where its capabilities had no equal for many years.

During WW2 the US military bought 767 "Norsemen" as utility cargo aircraft with the designation UC-64. It is the aircraft that Glenn Miller was a passenger in when he disappeared, so this is an aircraft of interest to more than just Canadians. Modellers in the USA have a wide variety of color schemes used by American companies to choose from, plus olive drab for military aircraft. (A friend wrote recently that the Norseman looks so cool with D-Day invasion stripes!)
