...Little material has come in here since the previous Posting. That provides me with the opportunity to contribute some long-winded stuff of my own. Try to bear with me on this!

The remarks in recent Postings about laser-cut kits remind me that about a dozen years ago I at last realized that I'd wasted countless model-building hours by following "traditional methods". That is, trying to cut parts to exact shape before assembly.

Today's laser-cut model kits are based on the same building & assembly methods of the 1930-era Comet, Megow, and Peerless kits. The major differences are precision pre-cut parts -- and PRICE.

For us scratch-builders today, a different approach can be used -- one that's simpler, quicker, more accurate, neater, and stronger. (It does waste a bit more wood, however.)

The drawback I've too often found with pre-cutting model parts (such as vertical tail outline pieces and wing ribs) was that regardless of how carefully I aligned and glued the balsa pieces together, some sanding of the final assembly was always necessary.

And that meant that some of the pieces ended up undersize. Sometimes WAY undersize -- so that I'd have to add a strip of balsa; then after the glue dried, carefully sand the addition back down to the as-designed contour.

I changed my methods! First, for wing and tail outline pieces, I started off with oversize, rough-cut balsa blanks, shaped accurately ONLY at the joints. After all of these had been glued together, I'd transfer the desired contours -- inner and outer -- to the assembly. Then I'd cut and sand the parts to exact shape.

This made for a faster and neater assembly, because there were no long, thin, and uncooperative tapered joints to coax into alignment; and the cut-to-shape-AFTER-assembly technique got rid of almost all the ugly glue exudations.

I used somewhat the same methods in adapting balsa lamination technique to my models -- see my article in the SMALLnet web site for full details about that.

Another "radical" technique I came up with was for built-up "box fuselages" -- like Lazy Bees use. Instead of assembling two sides one atop the other in the "usual way", I tried something different.

I constructed ONE fuselage side, using wood three times as thick as usual. This had several advantages. One was needing only half as many parts -- and half as many joints to fit and glue.

(By the way, using this new "triple-thick technique", I made good use of the old, original design Master Airscrew "Miter Master". That was a great tool. I don't know why they quit making it, and later issued a different, less "friendly" configuration.)

After my triple-thick fuselage side was complete and dry, I block- sanded both its outer surfaces smooth and flat. That got rid of the glue blobs and slight irregularities. THEN I used my bandsaw (with a home-made "high fence" for support, and a thin, low-set blade) to saw the triple-thick "fuselage side" exactly in half.

Then I placed the two halves smooth-side down on a flat surface, aligned top-to-top for the first sanding; then bottom-to-bottom, and block-sanded them simultaneously to remove the saw marks and fuzz.

This also allowed me to do a little "taper sanding" -- making the sides thinner towards the tail.

One big advantage of making a box fuselage this way is EXACT matching of the corresponding wood parts on each side. There's no chance of one longeron being stiffer than its mate, with the consequent misalignment that happens when such box fuselage sides are assembled with their crosspieces.

...Another weakness of traditional "built-up box" fuselage construction is the use of uniform-dimension stripwood for the longerons. Because factory-cutting 1/8" square balsa strips (or 3/32" X 1/4" or whatever) is easy, that's what model designers have called for on their plans since 1913.

But tapered longerons make more structural sense. The stress on "cantilever beams" isn't constant. In a fuselage side, the longerons are far more highly stressed at their midpoint than at their aft ends. Therefore if a box fuselage is built strong enough to withstand "normal stresses", using longerons (or sheet balsa) of uniform cross-section -- then it's heavier than necessary at the aft end. Such a fuselage often needs nose ballast when the model is complete. That adds MORE useless, performance-robbing weight...

Here's a way to use tapered longerons in a built-up box fuselage, as for a rubber- or CO2-powered model. If the plans call for 1/8" square longerons top and bottom, use 1/8" X 1/4" instead.

Taking the bottom longeron as an example: Pin it down over the plan so that in the "cabin" region the wide longeron's bottom edge just barely overlaps the bottom outline shown on the fuselage side layout.

Then at the tail, align the longeron's TOP edge with the original design longeron's top edge. If much of a bend is called for in a "wide longeron" such as I'm suggesting, soak the wood in plain water (NOT ammoniated -- that may affect the strength of the glue bond) an hour or so before assembly. And use Monokote backing film between the wet wood and the plan. Even CYA adhesives won't stick to that -- but be sure to let the wood dry thoroughly before gluing!

After the fuselage side is complete, the excess longeron material can be planed and sanded away to the design outline. That will produce a neatly-tapered and immensely strong assembly.

Now for wing ribs! Those MUST be accurately shaped on their undersides, and the spar notches also must be precise. But the UPPER contours of wing ribs should have at least 1/32" surplus material. That allows block-sanding (CAREFULLY, with a long, straight sanding tool) to bring all the ribs into a smoothly- faired and uniform spanwise contour.

...So much for construction technique.

We SMALLsters are among the last of the build-it-all-yourself airplane modelers. There are others: the Blacksheep and the Flying Aces Club, for example. But D-I-Y model building is NOT a profitable activity for hobby shops and the big mail-order houses to cater to.

That's why all of today's model magazines (except Flying Models!) are so saturated with garish BUY BUY BUY BUY advertising. It's model flyer's wallets that interest those advertisers, not our desire for a constructive pastime.

And that's why I think that the continuing availability of old-time build-them-yourself model plans is such a boon to us. By now there are several suppliers of those, with thousands of model designs available: FF, C-L, and R/C (or R/C adaptable) of every size, type, and degree of complexity.

One of the earliest suppliers of out-of-print model plane plans was the late John Pond's Plans Service. First located in San Francisco, it later moved (with John, who got transferred from place to place by his U.S. Navy employers); ending in San Jose at the time of John Pond's death. Now it's re-established in Washington state, operated by John's grandson Kevin.

Brian Fairey's interest in the Tipsy/Fairey Junior reminds me that this aircraft was the subject of one of the first R/C scale low wing designs. Howard Boys built one of around 54 inches span for an Allbon 2.8cc (0.17 cu. in.) diesel. It was unusual in that it was controlled only by aileron control (single channel, of course). This was in the '49-'50 time period.

Reproducing this model would be an interesting project. As far as I know, the R/C design was never published, but his 22-inch span free flight version (available from Nexus and Ben Buckle plans, and presumably from Bob Holman) could probably be scaled up and the rest concocted from the photos in Aeromodeller. It is a pretty aeroplane, though not quite as pretty as the prewar Tipsy Trainer (side by side seating) with a more complex wing planform.

Howard Boys was a remarkable individual who was a columnist on R/C for the Aeromodeller magazine at the time. He was also known for his flying wings (rocket and pulse jet powered) and for his home-made cars of the cycle-car ilk.

All the best, Dave

...Jim, colored Silkspan (tm) was a proprietary product of the Whitfield Paper Company. It was available in red, yellow, and orange; at Veco we included lightweight yellow Silkspan in Comanche (1/2A PAA-Load FF) kits, circa 1951.

But after Whitfield's patents expired in the mid-1950's, other companies -- oriental, maybe? -- came out with their versions of this covering material. But only in white: evidently Whitfield had developed a "trade secret" method of dyeing Silkspan.

I do know from personal experience that hand-dyeing the original Silkspan (with Rit) had adverse effects on it. [JW]
