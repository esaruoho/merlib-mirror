Ed Booms < BoomsE@EagleOttawa.com > asks:

I was wondering if anyone could give me their experiences and recommendations in regards to the various wire-bending tools available. I was looking at various models made by K & S, Micro-Mark, Higley's, etc. I will be using it for small R/C (.15 size and smaller) scratch building.

If the tool can handle heavier wire than found on the .15-size aircraft, that would be a bonus. Not sure if any one tool can do this though. Also, does anyone know what size wire the Micro-Mark model is designed for?

I really appreciate the SMALL site. Sites like this make the Internet worthwhile.

Thanks, Ed B

...Ed, I've done a LOT of wire bending, and I use two types of wire bending tools. One is the die-cast zinc alloy type that you called a Micro-Mark model. This has been around for a LONG time -- I bought mine for a dollar, roughly 30 years ago. It will accept any size wire used in model airplanes.

However, I don't recommend this tool for precision wire bending! Neither do I recommend the Higley wire-bending tool: it's merely a more limited version of the Micro-Mark thingie.

A far better tool for making accurate and repeatable wire bends is the one sold by K&S. I've been using an early model of their "Mini Wire Bender" for ten years, on wires from 1/8" diameter music wire on down. (For up to 1/4" diameter music wire, K&S has a "Mighty Wire Bender" -- but who uses that heavy-gage wire in SMALL models?)

The K&S Bender wasn't truly useful to me until I added a simple modification. I cut an 8-inch circle from a FLAT piece of 3/16" aircraft plywood, then in the center of that cut a SNUG-fitting hole to fit the "base plate" of the K&S Mini Wire Bender.

I then pressed the (cleaned of oil) bender baseplate into the hole in my plywood circle, and glued it (with CA) exactly flush with the top surface of the baseplate.

I finished the job by adding self-adhesive shelf-covering plastic on top. This is white with an imprinted black grid of 1/2-inch squares -- mighty useful in making duplicate bends, such as for landing gear axles...

The reason for my plywood "bender base extension" is that the K&S tool base is too small for good support of items such as model landing gears. It's hard to keep all the bends on the same flat "plane". But with my table addition, the job is EASY.

The K&S baseplate is steel, about half an inch thick. It protrudes below my plywood table just enough for a good firm grip in my workbench vise jaws. And I added a hole near the rim, so I can hang the tool on a nail in the wall, and thus avoid having to hunt for it...

Using this bender is not difficult. I mark the "central position" of each bend on the wire with a dab of machinist's grease pencil. I do this progressively; marking, then making, one bend at a time, using the full-size pattern of the landing gear on the model plan as a guide.

I've found that as the wire is bent, it "wraps" and "travels" equally around the bending pin. That lets me position the pencil mark on the wire JUST tangent to the pivot pin before starting to make the bend. And as the wire bends, the grease pencil mark moves around the pin. It remains in the middle of the bend radius, so that I've never found it necessary to calculate extra material length for the "wrapped" portion.

Now back to the Micro-Mark tool! I use that to UNbend music wire. Here's why. Bending music wire inevitably traps stress. If you make a bend exactly to a pattern, the wire wants to relax if it can -- and in model airplane landing gear it gets many good opportunities to do so.

Therefore I OVERbend my music wire parts a little -- say 10 or 15 degrees -- and then UNbend them to exact shape with my little die-cast $1.00 wire bender (that Micro-Mark now sells for $8.50 plus shipping). That relaxes trapped stress, so that my carefully- formed wire landing gears retain their intended shape. [JW]

Fred Sanford < sanford@paulbunyan.net > wrote:

The NiMH "9V transistor radio style" batteries work well in motors of the GWS size and down. Make sure you buy the NiMH's that put out 8.4 volts, as some brands have less voltage.

At Radio Shack pick up some of the 9-V style connectors, and solder your choice of connector on the other end. (Soldering directly to the NiMH battery is difficult, and can easily damage it.) You can carefully remove the metal case, which will save a few grams.

I charge these batteries at 200 milliamps on my AstroFlight 110D. Flight duration on my Horizon J-3 Lawn Cub is 6 minutes!

Fred Sanford

John Whitsitt < JWKWAB@cs.com > contributed;

Regarding proper balance of an R/C airplane, just roll it over and fly it inverted. If it needs forward stick, it's nose-heavy. Ideally, the plane should not change its own pitch upright or inverted. Once balanced, adjust elevator sensitivity to your taste. The preceding primarily applies to symmetrical and semi-symmetrical airfoils, but flat-bottomed wings can nearly be set up for the same effect.

John Whitsitt Merriam, Kansas

...Luke, one problem with the later-made OK Cub .049's is that their needle valve assemblies weren't made or installed with consistency. For that reason it's not of much help to say, "Open the needle ___ turns from fully closed to start."

Here's how I find the starting position for OK Cub needles.

For an engine I haven't handled before, I make certain that the needle CAN be closed to seal off the spraybar orifice. Some can't, because their needles haven't been pressed deeply enough through their threaded housings. (Most of those can be fixed by pressing the needle a little further through.)

The ideal position for the Cub needle is for it to shut off flow JUST before the threaded knob sleeve bottoms. But as much as 1/8" "clearance space" is acceptable.

For starting, close off the needle; then prime with a drop of fuel into an exhaust port WITH THE PISTON COVERING THE PORTS.

With the plug connected, flip the prop; the engine should start and run for about a second. If it doesn't, either the fuel or the plug is bad. (Cubs are like Coxes: they thrive best on glow fuel with a MINIMUM of 20% castor oil lubricant.)

If the engine DOES start on prime, open the needle about 1 1/2 turns; choke JUST enough to fill the fuel line; then prime again and flip to start. If the engine dies, open the needle another half turn and try again.

Most of my Cubs require a rich start, with the needle open maybe 3/4 of a turn from the best running position. I let the engine warm up a few seconds, then SLOWLY screw in the needle just until smooth running occurs. I DO NOT attempt to "peak" Cubs. [JW]

Mike Banke < banke@whitelion.net > sent:

If for nothing else than to fill up space, let me tell youall about my foamy called "Son of Tuit". Randy's "Small Talk" column of a couple of months ago had a picture of some old codger holding my pride and joy.

It consists of a 16" circle, a 3" X 6" rhomboid-shaped fin and a 1" X 15" keel, all of 1/2" insulating foam board. A GWS D motor, 8 120 mAh nicads, Feather receiver, and two Pico mini servos make up the power and direction complement.

Direction control is accomplished by elevons which are cut from the circle and taped back on after chamfering their leading edge about 20 degrees. A 3/8" X 2" spruce motor mount suitably trimmed to fit the GWS motor is epoxied on top of the circle, and nicads and receiver are held on with Velcro. The servos are press-fit on either side of the fin. It really does fly, and axial rolls like crazy. Mike Banke
