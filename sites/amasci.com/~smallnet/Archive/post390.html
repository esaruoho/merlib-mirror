Al Lidberg < AALmps@aol.com > wrote:

Just got to looking at the Joe Ott drawing for the 'dime scale' Fokker Triplane a little closer. Various theories tell us to put multi-plane wings at slightly different incidences to minimize stalling.

Joe has all three wings at the same angle; but here, on this mid-30s plan, he shows a different airfoil for each wing: flat on top wing, mild undercamber for the middle, and considerable undercamber for the bottom one.

I'm guessing that this would be effective, but it adds a bit of work, even on a dimer with just a few ribs.

Comments? AL

A. A. Lidberg model plan service FF-R/C kits and plans < http://www.aalmps.com > now with on-line credit card ordering

...Probably the reason for the varying airfoils was to reduce drag above the thrustline. The Fokker's top wing is the largest of the three, and WAY above the centerline. On the "real airplane" the stabilizer was set at "positive incidence" (with reference to the fuselage centerline) to help compensate for nose-up tendencies under power.

But on a small-size model the efficiency of flying surfaces decreases -- which is why (e.g.) Earl Stahl and Sidney Struhl used enlarged tailplanes on all their scale rubber-powered model designs.

I think Joe Ott took a different approach. [JW]

George Penniket < penniket@netidea.com > contributed:

Someone recently enquired about methods of sealing strap-on mufflers to the exhaust port. One method I used years ago on an O.S. H 40P which had a strap-on muffler was to clean off the exhaust port and muffler thoroughly with alcohol, to remove any traces of oil, then applied a bead of silicone seal around the exhaust port, and installed the muffler. No more leaks !

Silicone seal will withstand the temperature of exhaust gases without breaking down. It also is an excellent cement for applications where it doesn't need to be sanded -- it doesn't sand worth a darn !

I once used it to build a fuselage for a Sterling Lancer. It did a great job of gluing balsa, but left ridges at every joint when sanded, which I had to remove with a sharp X-Acto blade.

Have a great summer! George

...Thanks, George. Your contribution reminds me of a point that probably ought to be made. I should have done this in response to the original "leaky muffler" query, which was about the clip-on mufflers used on Norvel 1/2A's.

The implication was that imperfectly-sealed mufflers wouldn't produce reliable "muffler pressure" for fuel tanks. Not so!

Muffler attachment leakage makes NO DIFFERENCE WHATEVER to the pressure available. After all, every muffler has a big fat intentional leak, at its exhaust outlet.

As far as I've been able to tell from feedback from my Engine Shop column in Model Aviation, the major concern that most model flyers have with muffler leakage is CLEANLINESS. They dislike the oil seepage into their airplane nose compart- ments -- and they HATE oil oozing out onto the outsides of their mufflers and cylinder fins and baking there into an ugly brown "varnish".

Returning to the "insecure Norvel muffler" topic: I found an easy fix for that, using a turn or two of wire looped around the ends of the spring wire "muffler clip" and wrapped tightly around the cylinder below the muffler. [JW]
