Ian L. McQueen < imcqueen@nbnet.nb.ca > recommended:

"Nero Wolfe" asked about a good supplier of 1/16" and 1/32" balsa. He wrote, "The stacks at the local shops are trashy at best."

There are some good mail order suppliers. I just bought some from Easy Built. It was all good-looking wood, white, straight grain, etc. The 1/16" wood was 6.5 lb/cu ft, the "1/32" was 8.9. {The quotation marks around 1/32 are because the wood was a true 1/25" (1mm). The 1/16" was exact in size.}

Since Easy Built is a relatively small company, you could ask if they could try to pick wood in a specific density range -- if you needed extra-light wood, for example. I'm not sure if they have the time to do that, but it would be worth a try if this is important to you. Ian

...Bob, you have a point there. I've seen discussion recently about whether model airplanes are a Sport or a Hobby. Of course they're both -- and an Art and a Science and a Craft too. But one aspect of modeling that seems to be forgotten these days is its importance as a PASTIME.

Magazine ads tell us, "We've taken the hard work out of modeling for you, so that only the FUN remains!" The implication there is that only model FLYING is fun, and everything else is drudgery.

However, model flying is largely limited to daytime hours in mild weather. We can BUILD any time, in any weather...

And yet I can see a need for prefabricated kits! For one thing, it's become quite difficult to buy supplies for scratch-building model airplanes. Case in point: last summer in southern California I wanted to construct a half-size Dakota biplane, powered with a motor/prop/battery combination taken from a foam toy FF airplane that Steve Staples gave me at the Maumelle SMALL meet.

I went to three formerly well-stocked hobby shops in the San Fernando Valley, but was not able to buy all of the materials I needed for that little project.

Things are worse here in Alabama. There used to be a fairly well-stocked hobby shop about 20 miles from here. They closed in September; now there's no model plane hobby shop worthy of the name closer than 75 miles.

Long gone are the days when I could dash down to one of the local shops in my Home Town to buy a length of aluminum tubing or a fuel tank or a tube of glue. I won't even mention trying to find sheet balsa that can be picked through to find JUST the right density and strength... [JW]

Dean Owen < de.owen@morehead-st.edu > advises:

I'd like comment about the use of commercial grades of Kerosene when blending your own diesel fuels. Many of them available from auto retailers contain quite a number of additives, which may change throughout the year, depending upon temperature and anticipated use.

I've had great success going by the local airport and buying a gallon of aviation jet fuel: Jet A. Generally this grade of Kerosene is quite pure and has very few additives. It is refined to FAA standards and is the highest grade of Kerosene one can buy.

As a pilot I'm often at the pumps buying 100 LL (low lead) aviation gasoline for one of my planes, so it's no big deal for me to pick up a little Jet A. But if you live anywhere near an airport just go by and ask for a little Jet A.

Here's another tip: By regulation, most FBO owners need to purge their fuel trucks and their fueling systems regularly, and in doing so they will draw off several gallons of fuel. Just ask them to put it in a bottle for you and you'll get the stuff free of charge -- although it's not expensive anyway....

Cheers, Dean Owen

"Nero Wolfe" < wolfe19@lightfirst.com > suggests:

Has anyone considered adding a flywheel to their small engine to help with its idle? An ounce or two of flywheel might just keep it sustained at really low rpms where you can't expect a Cox to run right. The weight could be anywhere, either as a small disk behind the spinner, or little weights at the propeller tips (where you could use less weight for the same effect)

Another idea along the same lines: Do the engines idle better with larger and heavier props? Does anyone have an engine they'd be willing to run an experiment or two on to that effect?

..."Nero", low-speed idling problems mostly involve "keeping the fire lit". That's why some R/C model flyers use on-board glow plug power supplies. Those turn on at low rpm, and keep the plug element hot at slow speeds.

Slow idling is more of a problem with small engines because as size decreases, the ratio of cooling surface area to combustion chamber volume INCREASES. In other words, the smaller the engine, the greater the cooling effect.

As I've mentioned in earlier Postings, excessive cooling in small model motors can be overcome by partially wrapping the cylinder with aluminum foil. A similar technique helps prevent fuel condensation in the crankcases of small diesels which tend to "load up" during slow speed operation.

As for "running an experiment or two" on model engines with various sized props -- that's been done many times! Modelers are forever experimenting with fuel blends, propellers, throttle designs, and different types of glow plugs. And the results of all these tests prove conclusively: If a certain combination works acceptably in YOUR application, it must be right -- for that application !
