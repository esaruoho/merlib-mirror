Chuck Clemans <  > inquires:

It's great to have SMALLnet back in operation! Thanks guys! I'm scratch-building a Mini Mambo from Sterling kit plans and will be powering it with either a Cub 049 or 074. Does anyone remember the correct prop size for these two engines and the nitro content of fuel for Sunday flying?

Also, what would be an appropriate glow plug? Finally, I've seen a few Cub 074 diesels on eBay. How were they?

Chuck Clemans

...Chuck, I did a lot of flying with Cubs in the 1950's, and generally used a 6-3 or 6-4 prop on the .049 and a 7-4 on the .074. The 1/2A glow fuel most commonly available when the Cubs came out used nitrobenzene rather than nitromethane. That's where the "shoe-polish" aroma came from.

Modern glow fuel with 15% to 25% nitro should work in most Cubs, but be sure there's at least 20% all-castor lube content.

Ron Clem told me a while back that modern Fireball plugs are made by the same company that produced the original OK plugs. Those OK's were good ones, all right -- but as yet I haven't run any Cubs with Fireball plugs. (I still have some of the original OK's.)

As for the OK .074 diesel, all I can tell you about that motor is that it uses an O-ring on its contra-piston. That's over 50 years old now & can't be reliable. You can get replacement rings (made from Viton rubber) from Small Parts, Inc. [JW]

John Rossetti <  > eulogizes:

We can't pass on enough tributes to Hal deBolt. He did as much for R/C as Jim Walker did for U-Control. My first successful R/C plane was a Livewire Trainer, which I flew more times than I can remember at Delta Park in Portland, Oregon in the late 1950's.

It was the plane of the day for many. Of course it was rudder- only, but so stable and forgiving that you could learn to fly and keep your airplane in one piece.

Later, I had the opportunity to watch Hal fly with Ron Chapman in Toronto, Ontario in the late sixties. Not only was he a great designer, but his flying ability was matchless. He would come up from Buffalo on the weekend, and he and Ron would fly together using the latest R/C radio equipment.

Once you saw them fly, you had to get multi-channel. We are fortunate to have been in his generation. John

Roy Clough <  > writes:

In the summer of 1961 control-line models were big in my New Hampshire area. Guys were building super streamlined and racy-looking stuff that was all the rage. My different drummer told me it would be cool to design a control-liner with the look of a gentle old free flight floater, but with greatly enhanced stunt capability. The result was "Nervous Nellie" which I published in the March 1962 issue of Model Airplane News.

This model had a full-floating pivoting elevator that produced an extremely short turning radius. To fully implement its lively maneuverability I chose a new McCoy .09 Redhead Diesel.

The motor proved extremely powerful and spun the recommended propeller at (for those days,) gas-engine speeds. I was very pleased with the combination, but after about a dozen flights the Redhead broke its crankshaft.

I had been very careful never to run it in the knocking mode, so that hadn't been the cause of its failure. (In the days before anti-knock components were added to diesel fuel, I suspect, but can't prove, that the life of some control line diesels had been shortened by leaning out in the air with no decent way to stop them before the fuel ran out.)

I took the Redhead .09 apart and found it undamaged except for a broken crankshaft web. Probably it did not look "right" to me because I elected NOT to order a replacement. I disremember whether I junked it or gave it away, but collectors might be advised to treat this engine gently.

As for the modified "Streaker", I found when developing the wing-warping "Stringer" (Model Airplane News, August 1995) that twisting the wing tips in opposite directions resulted in lots of drag but no turn. To successfully turn with wing warping it required washing out just one wing tip at a time and leaving the other at its nominal pitch. This incidentally was the plane in which I discovered that enclosing the Cox Failsafe antenna inside the Micafilm-covered fuselage caused loss of control after a couple of hundred feet. Fishing the wire outside fixed that. Roy C.

Al Lidberg <  > contributed:

About the Scientific Varsity and the McCoy .049 diesel:

I built a Varsity from a Klarich kit, and have since built a few more of his kits. This was my first old-timer for SAM R/C OT.

The Varsity uses what I'd call an unconventional fuselage construction, and any difficulties I encountered were due more to that than to Harry K's interpretation for kit purposes.

The flat sheet balsa crutch wasn't too bad to work with, and by the time I'd cut away a lot of it for R/C and spark ignition bits access, it was not a problem at all. The wood beam mounts were also tapered way back to save weight and give more room in there.

Regarding flight qualities: the O&R.23 sideport (actually 2 of them, suggesting that the coil or transistorized coil driver was at fault) gave me a lot of trouble with inconsistent running, and for whatever reason the R/C gear also seemed to experience interference.

The model's final flight was at SAM Champs Claremore, Oklahoma, where it caught full "down" and "left" signals from about 200' -- hitting hard under power in dirt, and breaking an ear off the front cover of the '47 'beamless' .23 (which had the accessory sheet metal mount plate). Anyhow, the Varsity is a nice-looking plane, and you won't see many others.

I flew a McCoy .049 diesel quite a bit when they were new, and was fairly happy with it -- sure wouldn't give $275 for one, however! A friend here in the Phoenix area, Bill Langelius told me that his brother Bob in the New York area will be marketing a reproduction McCoy .049 diesel sometime soon. Bob's the same guy who reproed the K&B .020 some time back.

On that subject, I talked with Ed Seay who has a hobby shop in Irving, TX. He did a K&B.020 project more than a few years ago -- reproducing the tiny sheet balsa FF models of the time -- and selling new K&B .020's put together from spare parts!

Where he found all the parts I don't know, but he claimed to have sold hundreds of the engines. Back to the McCoy .049 diesel. The only difficulties we had in the 50's was keeping the contra- piston's O-ring alive.

I finally found suitable and cheaper ones at a hardware store, and they allowed us a lot of run time. Nowadays, I'd say we must have been overcompressing, but failure of the O-ring is a better outcome than bent connecting rods, I'm sure.

Someone asked about Testors 39 fuel, and how it was named. It sold for 39 cents a can -- probably a half-pint. That price was a bargain for a teenager!

AL Lidberg

Dave Hewett <  > wrote from Vermont:

I've been a model-builder since I was 13, and am now almost 67. As a young teenager I used to fly at the fairgrounds in Windsor (behind our house): U-control stunt, and even combat with the only other flier in town -- and free flight.

At 14 and 15 we brought along my stepdad's .410 single barrel shotgun and shot our planes out of the tall fir trees that surrounded the fields. The theory was we always could build another wing or fuselage, but we couldn't replace the hung-up Cub .049's and ex-ignition Bantams, so shoot we did.

I've been building on and off for all the intervening years; now just build and hang from the rafters in the cellar. Hanging there are nine with glow motors, one electric, a rubber FW 198 pusher (doubled Megow kit). Five scale models, including a big Schreyer Northrop Gamma and a scratch-built Lincoln bipe hang in the tiny office where this was written; a Megow double-plan size Waterman Aircar sits on the TV set, two ignition U-C's (a Berkeley Bee and an Eagle Dreamer) sit on a bedroom bureau.

I discovered eBay a couple years ago and now have a bundle of kits waiting to go. The trouble with solitary building is there is no one to show or talk to about progress or problems. I re-covered the wing of a 1940 Scientific Whippet yesterday, for example, and got a third coat of clear dope on the wings of the Scalemaster F4B-4 that is still a ways from completion.

I think a lot of us discovered model airplanes in our youth and fell into a love affair that wives, kids, jobs, and all the myriad losses we're prone to, never lessened. Dave Hewett

Richard Szanti <  > contributed:

Source: Doculam
The Doculam Film mentioned by Bob Peterson sounded interesting, enough so that I went looking for a source with the intent of buying some to experiment with. Didn't find any at Office Depot, but did discover a source at < www.laminatorwarehouse.com >.

Duralam is a polyester thermal-laminating film available in 1.5 and 3 mil thickness. Minimum order size at laminatorwarehouse is two rolls that are 250 feet each in length. A 3 mil, 27-inch-wide roll goes for $22, so the minimum purchase would be 500 feet at $44 (and you still need to add shipping). That's a bit pricey for playing with an unknown (for me) quantity.

I also found GBC film, apparently equivalent to Doculam, at $43 (includes shipping) for a 3 mil, 27 inch by 250-foot roll. Still a bit high. Perhaps there is another source with prices more in line with what Bob mentioned. If so I'd sure like to hear about it.

Don Snowdon <  > adds:

I have been reading about it for a while and finally bought some Doculam. It does come in two thicknesses: 1.5 and 3 mil. I don't know about the 3 mil stuff, but the 1.5 mil is supposedly about 1/3 the weight of Monokote.

Most people with smaller models use the 1.5 mil stuff. It's available in 500-foot rolls from < http://www.laminatorwarehouse.com/doculam_coverlam.html >.

There's a whole lot of information about using it in on RC Groups in a thread called "Doculam Discoveries" at: < http://www.rcgroups.com/forums/showthread.php?t=274520 >.

One interesting fact there is if you paint it on the adhesive side before you apply it, it is more glossy. As soon as I get my Stevens Aero RV4 (laser cut balsa kit) finished, I'll be trying it first on that.

I forgot to mention that usually there's a two roll minimum order from Laminator Warehouse -- unless you tell them you're covering model airplanes with it. Then they'll let you order one roll.
