"Micromall" <  > wrote:

IN REMEMBRANCE OF HAL "PAPPY" DEBOLT

We have lost one of the truly great R/C pioneers of the CITIZENS BAND R/C ERA. Hal "Pappy" deBolt has passed away. It was the inevitable outcome, after reading on SMALLnet that Hal had terminal cancer.

Although I never knew Hal personally, I felt like I did, after reading about "Pappy" in the model magazines for over half a century. Today I remember how eager I was in the 1950's to get to the school library to read the latest model magazines. Often there would be pictures of Hal and inspiring news about his exciting R/C exploits.

In the summer of 1952, my oldest brother took my other older brother and me to see R/C models that were being flown on the new CITIZENS BAND examination-free 27 MC R/C spot frequency that had been authorized by the FCC early in 1952.

I will never forget the awesome experience I had, as a pre-teen, when I watched model airplanes being mysteriously controlled by invisible radio waves high in the wild blue yonder. It was pure magic for a nine-year-old kid! Afterwards I was hooked on R/C, even though we continued to fly less costly free flight and U-control models. Nevertheless, my older brothers, try as they would, could not talk their very young kid brother out of wanting to fly R/C.

Therefore, they finally gave in to me and asked John, who owned the local model shop, which R/C model airplane he would recommend for their twelve-year-old younger brother. I remember John reaching up to a display rack on the wall and taking down an R/C kit. He bent over and set the kit on the shop floor, got down on his haunches, opened the box, took out the plans, and then proceeded to tell me about all the great design features of Hal deBolt's R/C Live Wire Trainer: the knock-off landing gear in case of a crash...the easily removable "crash-proof" cage that housed the R/C equipment for fast and easy servicing, etc., etc.

To this day, the Live Wire Trainer is unique, by being the very first model kit specifically designed and engineered for R/C flying on the then-new 27.255 MC R/C frequency.

Later, Hal made numerous other material contributions to our R/C hobby -- including selling over a half million kits of his model airplane designs! Hal's Golden Age of R/C column, which began in 1986 in Model Airplane News, ran for several years. Without a doubt, Hal's "Golden Age of R/C" is the most comprehensive and greatest historical record of our R/C hobby ever undertaken and published. Just one more lasting treasure that Hal contributed to our R/C hobby.

After over half a century, we are going to miss you, "Pappy"! May your family and friends be comforted by their memory of your kindness and proud of your many great accomplishments and contributions to our wonderful R/C hobby.

Roger

Have a friend who's had a bit of trouble in applying adhesive- backed, pressure-sensitive trim to his MonoKote-covered models.

Will your earlier recommendation to me for working with old time, water-softened decals, using detergent and water, also work for him (and me...) in positioning and applying various "stickies" to our planes? I was going to suggest it, but haven't tried it with other than just decals (which worked really great!), so have not yet offered him your method.

Jack

...Jack, you're right ! Wetting the "main surface" with a solution of water & dish detergent works for all kinds of appliques. I used it with instant success in positioning VinylWrite letters & numbers on a model about 10 years ago. When dry, they looked like they'd been molded into the surface.

I'd advise using the cheapest "generic" dishwashing detergent your supermarket carries. That's because the NEW & IMPROVED modern brands with their perfumes, disinfectants, and who knows WHAT all -- may not work well in this application.

The original detergent-in-a-squeeze-bottle stuff makes as tenacious an adhesive as I've ever seen. Here's how I found that out.

In the mid-1970's, when I was a mad bicyclist, I was given a set of "Grab-Ons" as a gift. These were dense foam-rubber tubes that slid over bike handlebars to act as a cushion grip. (VERY effective on long-distance rides !)

But when I first tried installing them, the tubes wouldn't slide up and around the bends in my racing handlebars. I thought, "AHA !" and mixed up a slippery slurry of dishwashing detergent and water.

I doused the inside of the tubes with that -- the handlebars too -- and slick as ski wax, the Grab-Ons slid right into place.

But after a few years and many thousand miles, my Grab-Ons had deteriorated. I wanted to replace them. Ha ! I had to cut them off with an X-Acto carving blade, then scrape and scrape and SCRAPE to get the residue of the dried detergent off the bars. That stuff is tenacious !

...As I always do, though, I recommend that your friend do some practicing first on a mockup of some kind. It's NEVER a waste of time to perform a preliminary testing of a new-to-you technique.

One further detail about using water-detergent as a positioning aid in attaching pressure-sensitive trim: It takes a while for ALL the water to evaporate from underneath the trim. How long depends (of course) on the humidity, and width of the trim piece.

When I applied the VinylWrite trim to my Typhoon, I waited three full days before doing any "handling" of the trim area. I probably wouldn't have held off that long, but Art & Cynthia (originators of VinylWrite) advised a 72-hour minimum wait.

Coincidentally, that's the same time it takes for butyrate dope to attain maximum fuel resistance. [JW]

Pete Redhead <  > contributed:

For Harry Barr regarding 'Fan' Props for rubber power:

I've been using these for over 40 years now, on everything from indoor 'Pistachio' scale, upwards to O/T 40" competition fliers. The 1/3rd span rule for diameter gives safe flying. Too big a prop causes torque problems and ruins the glide.

There is another good rule regarding choice of stick size for the laminations -- use a plank with a 1:4 ratio width/thickness, e.g. 1/16" x 1/4" for small 4- or 5-inch peanut props, 3/32" x 3/8" up to 8" or 9" props, and 1/8" x 1/2" for anything up to a 12/13" freewheeler for an old-timer or outdoor scale.

Take your chosen strips, mark the centre spot, and drill a hole using the piece of wire that you will use for the shaft. I try to keep to a stack thickness of 8 strips, since in most cases that is enough to give a nicely proportioned blade shape after carving.

For the laminating process, use only balsa cement. Nothing else will sand away as cleanly, regardless of what the ads claim, and it's lighter. Glue the laminations together, one at a time, using the prop wire as a pivot, and fan each lamination half of the plank width at the end -- pinning along the length as you go to keep things together.

Using this set of proportions will give you a finished prop with a pitch/diameter ratio of around 1:1.25, e.g. 10" diameter, 12.5" pitch -- which most rubber fliers of my acquaintance seem to regard as an optimum choice for low to medium power freewheeling props on models up to about 40" span.

Carve the hub away by half of the stack thickness (normally from the rear, but there's nothing to stop you taking material from the front of the stack if noseblock clearance behind is going to be a problem, e.g big, flat-faced radiator like the SE5A !). This will make it easier to blend the blade shape into the hub. Use a ply facing front and rear to strengthen the hub; bush it with a piece of brass tube for smooth freewheeling, and fit your preferred clutch mechanism -- another handy reason for the ply facing!

A last tip, which also applies to any other time you need to sand a balsa structure: Where possible, dope it (or use sanding sealer) before you start sanding. That stops the 'whiskers' and makes it much easier to eyeball the progress, as you can definitely see a change of colour where you've already been.

It's also an excellent primer if you're going to use tissue or silk and dope for covering. I apologise if everybody out there already knows this stuff -- but somebody had to tell me all those years ago, and it seems a shame not to pay it back....

Pete Redhead

Charlie Fries <  > sent in a puzzler:

Here's a problem to get your teeth into -- and let me know your solution.

Several of us have built (from plans) the "STREAKER" featured in Flying Models, the October 2002 issue. It's powered by a Speed 400 motor mounted as a pusher. Flight control is by ailerons and elevator; no rudder. Wing is 36" flat span with full-length ailerons. It flies like a little jewel: slow or fast, it's not squirrely at all.

So ? So one builder decided to add 5" rounded tips to each end of the wing. They are angled up about 40 degrees. He thought he may be able to make it more of a floater by doing that.

Well ! Surprise !! That little ship was the most stable critter you have ever seen. Ailerons had almost no effect -- even when throws were doubled. Ability to turn was almost non-existent. I opined that even if he enlarged the width of the ailerons, it would be more likely to add a speed brake effect. He had his mind made up that polyhedral made it more stable but was not the reason for the loss of control.

I feel that the absence of a rudder with its long moment arm is the cause of the problem. OK: Let me hear from some of youse guys.

Charlie Fries

...Charlie: As I see it, the Streaker with added polyhedral tips is experiencing a most unusual type of "adverse yaw" effect.

Down-moving ailerons produce more added drag than up-moving ones. Therefore with ailerons using equal up and down deflection, the usual effect is that a bank in one direction is accompanied by a yawing effect the other way. (That's the reason many R/C flyers use "differential aileron" setups.)

The original Streaker design was developed to minimize the adverse yaw effect; and your friend's added polyhedral tips seem to have brought it back. [JW]
