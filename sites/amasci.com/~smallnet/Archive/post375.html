Andy Woitowicz < ajw@ntl.sympatico.ca > asserts:

Regarding small engines, I would very much disagree with Mark Vance's assertion that we are asking a lot for a 50 dollar engine to have a 4000 RPM idle along with a 5/1 speed range. It can be done. See Randy Randolph's column in the September 2000 issue of Flying Models. The engine mentioned is the second Revlite I've modified, and both will do 4000 up to 19000.

Both will idle indefinitely; then response to full throttle is instant with no smoke or spitting. The stock plug is used. Different plugs afford varying characteristics, but the steady idle and response are retained.

All the modifications I made could easily be included by the manufacturer with very little extra cost. It's just a matter of getting all the elements together in the right place that enhances idling and throttling. These include a sealed, bolt-on muffler with pressure to the tank, an adjustable airbleed throttle, and a unique set of timing numbers on the intake, exhaust and by-pass.

All the old ideas about idle-bar plugs, sub-piston induction or flywheel effect actually have no real bearing on idle or throttle response.

Another problem with the Norvel throttle is the possibility of slop between the threads of the barrel where they engage the nipple. Because the needle valve is carried by the barrel, too much slop in this area will make setting the top end erratic, and idle performance will also suffer. Spring-loading the barrel, either externally or internally is the cure.

Regarding the Aquastar, see the review done by Flying Models in the November 2000 issue. Unfortunately, with an idle of 8000 on the Norvel Revlite, the plane wouldn't slow down enough to land. Not a big deal over ground but surely not a lot of fun on water.

On the subject of Cox engines, I too regret seeing their apparent loss of popularity. Great work by David Felin in adapting a Wasp .061 throttle to a TD .09. I'm gonna hafta try that!

To David: you might consider installing the barrel from your .074. This will fit right into the Wasp throttle. The barrel has a slightly larger bore, which should give you even more power without affecting the idle much. Worth a try, I think.

Many years ago, Cox came out with a reed-valve engine that had a tank mount and used a clunk set up. Inside was a small clunk along with thin, flexible, silicone tubing. This, really, is the secret to making a clunk work in a small tank.

The tank makers haven't given too much thought to our needs in this area. Instead of sizing the components to suit the task at hand, they've simply used the same fittings for the smaller tanks as used on 16-ounce tanks, for instance. There are a number of silicone tube manufacturers out there who offer small tubing, so it could be done.

I've got some with a 3/32" ID x 1/8" OD that has just the right flexibility with a small clunk. Along with two lines and pressure to the tank, I've never had any problems.

Andy Woitowicz

...Andy, I did a lot of foozling around with the three Cox engines of the type you mentioned. One was the Dragonfly. A later version was called by a different name, which I forget -- as well as that of the first model; the only Cox .049 with a die-cast case...

The silicone tubing was indeed extremely flexible. Too much so. It allowed the clunk to come all the way forward if you picked up your airplane by the tail -- and sometimes the clunk jammed in the tank front and kinked the fuel line.

Also that thin-wall tubing worked its way off the nipple quite easily. I found a cure by installing the tube with about an eighth of an inch of each end turned "inside out", to double the gripping power.

In fact, I wrote a detailed article for Model Airplane News on de-bugging the Dragonfly. It was fundamentally a pretty good little engine, and I still have one of the test prototypes in the nose of my Cleveland Cloudster (now sadly tattered & in dire need of re-covering). But there were some detail weaknesses, and my article explained (and showed in pictures) how to fix those.

WAY back when clunk tanks were the latest thing out for R/C -- Veco made the first commercial clunk tank kit that I know of -- we had the mistaken idea that the fuel tubing should be as limp as possible, so that the clunk could travel ANYWHERE within the tank interior, and follow the fuel wherever it might slosh.

Bad idea. I never saw this myself, but friends of mine swore that their clunks managed to tie a knot in the surgical rubber fuel line we all used then... [JW]

Ed Booms < BoomsE@EagleOttawa.com > needs information:

I am looking to purchase a scroll or jig saw for use in scratch building R/C aircraft. I will not be building the larger scale aircraft, but expect to be working on planes from 1/2A up to .60- size aircraft. Would welcome opinions/recommendations in purchasing a scroll or jig saw for typical R/C scratch-building use.

I know Minicraft sells a Fretsaw, and Dremel also carries a line of scroll/jig saws, but would like some help in this area. I will be using it to cut balsa ribs, small plywood, etc. Also, would a scroll saw be preferred over a jig saw? I am only looking to buy one unit at this time and would like something that would handle most jobs.

Thanks in advance, Ed B

...Ed, I've used several jig saws and scroll saws in my long modeling career, and my all-round favorite is the original Dremel. That's because its cam-tensioned blade connections make it ideal for "piercing work" -- i.e. cutting interior openings.

The newer, more expensive Dremel saw is better-made, and does an excellent job on outline sawing. But changing blade connections is slower, and requires re-setting the tension each time.

The biggest factor in successful jig (or scroll) sawing is the blade. Those come in many styles and tooth numbers -- but they're invariably stamped from steel and supplied "as stamped".

I learned almost 50 years ago while making steel-rule die-cutting dies for Veco, Kenhi, and Babcock, that stock jigsaw blades can be greatly improved, and without much trouble.

Briefly, I place each blade between two pieces of flat tool steel, and whack it hard with a rubber mallet a few times. The idea is to remove most of the "set" in the teeth. Then I place the blade, teeth up, in a machinist's vise, and file each tooth "gullet" with exactly three light strokes of a triangular needle file.

That sounds like tedious work, but it goes quite quickly. When the "working teeth" have all been filed, the blade will then produce an exceedingly smooth surface. The burrs left by the filing act like sandpaper, and provide side clearance for the cut as the "set" is supposed to do, but without leaving a shaggy cut surface.

Also, it's important to keep firmly in mind while scroll sawing that the blade ONLY cuts on its front edge! NEVER try to bring a jigsaw blade back "on line" by pressing the work sideways against the blade. If your sawcut drifts, back up and return to the pattern line before renewing the cut.

On capacity: It's possible to cut a stack of balsa wing ribs as tall as 1 1/4" on either Dremel saw -- but not advisable. The sawdust tends to accumulate in the cut and drags on the blade. I try to limit my balsa cutting height to under an inch on my little Dremel jigsaw, and hardwood no more than 3/8".

For thicker cuts I use a heavily-modified Sears scroll saw -- took me almost a year of fiddling until I managed to get that working smoothly. Quality scroll saws cost plenty; far more than I can afford! [JW]

Don Bailey < Donald.Bailey@PSS.Boeing.com > comments:

Curt Hulett wrote to complain about the all-up weight of his 4-pound E-powered Aspire.

I've found that any e-powered 2 meter poly sailplane that weighs much more than 3 pounds is a bit of a disappointment. Not that they won't fly, but the lift has got to be well-defined if prolonged flights are the goal.

My Minimax 700 weighed 44 ounces (7 x 2000 mAh and AF05G), but I added two rib bays to the wing, for a wing area of 780 square inches (WL = 8.1 oz/sq ft). It was a nice flying sailplane, and I got many one-hour-plus flights with it.

My Electra weighed in at the same 44 ounces, but the wing area was only 630 squares, so at a WL of 10 oz/sq ft, it was a slogger in light lift, unless I used a small battery pack, in which case I had to settle for just one good launch.

This got me to thinking about the philosophy behind electric- powered sailplanes. Do you want to just cruise around, like a park flyer? Or do you want to get up there where the strong lift is happening?

If the lift is well-defined at altitude (a typical spring day here in the Pacific Northwest), you only need one shot at it. So if you don't plan on poking around a lot under power, why go with a heavy electric system?

I saw a guy fly a Bird of Time once with a TD 049 mounted on the nose. No kidding. It climbed rather slowly, but after a motor run of maybe three or four minutes he was way up there. He landed an hour later, only because he had to get back to work. I'll bet that little motor and balloon tank added less than two ounces to his all-up weight.

Go with a Norvel .061 non-R/C and a short tank on that Aspire, Curt. You will like it.

Don Bailey Seattle Area Soaring Society

Dave Segal < DDavesegal@cs.com > adds:

I am sympathetic to Curt Hulett's worries about an overweight ARF electric glider. A wing loading of 12.9 ounces/square foot is not the path to floating about the skies. I once bought a Thunder Tiger Windstar EP which had 574 square inches of wing area. It weighed 54 ounces, with a loading of 13.5 oz./sf.

What a dog. It could barely climb. The structure was a good design and carefully built, but the materials were suited for something like a .40 powerplant and the "floor linoleum" covering didn't help either.

I did some calculations and decided to convert it to wet power. With an O.S. .10 engine and transparent Monokote covering on the wing it weighed 42 ounces, and was a delight to fly. Loading was 10.5 oz/sf.

I have just completed building Randy Randolph's Twiliter II motorglider with a Hornet AP.09 engine. It weighs just 29 ounces and has a wing loading of 7.5oz/sf -- so you can see that this is the way to go. I can't wait to give it a try.

No one should get the idea from this discussion that electric models can't climb well and be floaters, too. My favorite electric model was the Amptique (now available as a Spirit of Yesteryear kit), which had 518 square inches of wing and weighed 37 ounces, for a loading of 10.4 oz/sf. The pack was 7x1000 cells and it climbed like a fighter plane on a geared Leisure motor and would float around until my neck got stiff.

Dave Segal
