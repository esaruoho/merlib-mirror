To the Rescue: Climbers Brave Peril to Save Lives

© Copyright 1996 by S.A. Stolnack

A child wanders away from the family campsite and into the forest. A plane crashes into the side of a mountain. A climber on a remote peak in the Cascades slips and tumbles into a crevasse.

"It's usually Sunday night or Monday night," says John Wick, 41, of Seattle. "Someone hasn't shown up from a weekend trip. It can happen around midnight or one. The pager goes off. You stumble over and pick it up and turn on the lamp."

Fifteen minutes later his climbing gear is packed and he's out the door.

Wick is a member of Seattle Mountain Rescue, an all-volunteer organization dedicated to saving lives in the backcountry. Working under the direction of the King County Police, and together with other search and rescue organizations throughout the state, these altruistic climbers put themselves in harm's way to help others.

If you think it sounds glamorous, the reality is something else: "More often than not it'll be dark and raining," says Wick of a typical mission. He's a tall, fit-looking man with a bushy mustache and an air of quiet competence. On this slow Tuesday night, he's sitting in the Ridge Pub in North Seattle, back by the dart board, a half-empty pint of stout and an untouched plate of French fries in front of him.

"You're almost always wet," he says. "Always dirty. Between the dirt and the bugs and the wet you're physically uncomfortable."

Seattle Mountain Rescue (SMR) was the first volunteer mountain rescue group in the U.S. It was officially organized in 1948, modeled on the Bergwacht rescue team of Austria, a group famous for saving lives in the Alps. Over the decades, SMR's members have included such local climbing legends as Ome Daiber, Wolf Bauer, Ira Spring, Jim and Lou Whittaker, and Willie Unsoeld. It has gone from conducting a few missions each year to its present annual average of 40 to 50.

One needn't be a Himalayan climber to be a member, says Wick, a veteran of Alaska's Denali as well as many local peaks, though a good, solid climbing background is a must. To be considered, a prospective member must have at least three years of intermediate-level climbing experience, be comfortable leading class-five rock pitches, and be a competent snow and ice climber. (Class-five climbing involves using a rope, metal chocks and other hardware to protect yourself and your partner from a serious fall.) All members must be over 21, and all must hold current first-aid certification.

Once accepted, the new SMR member acquires further training before participating in his or her first mission. There are technical outings on rock and ice, classroom sessions, and training involving safe conduct in and around helicopters.

"Beyond that," says Wick, "all members share the ability to smoothly and maturely think through problems."

In the dark. In the dirt. In the rain. While hanging on a rope and maneuvering an aluminum stretcher down a 90-foot cliff.

Though Wick and his fellow climbers are ready and able, most of the operations SMR participates in are non-technical -- no rappelling out of helicopters, no daring rescues from the edges of precipices.

"We only have a handful of missions a year that turn into hard-core rock or ice work," he says. "We have some on Mt. Rainier, and other steep mountains, and some on rock faces. But mostly we're just counted on as people who can handle ourselves in awful terrain -- sheer cliffs, steep hillsides of loose rock, heavy forest, or wet, slippery moss."

Nevertheless, the complexity of even the simplest mission can be staggering. On a recent weekend, a party of climbers was caught in an avalanche on Red Mountain near Snoqualmie Pass. One man suffered serious injuries and needed immediate medical care. Nearly 100 people responded to the emergency, including King County Police, SMR members, a county rescue helicopter crew, a ski-patrol rescue team, Explorer Scout search and rescue members, search-dog teams, and 4X4 units providing ground transportation from the rescue base to the trailhead. The man was flown to Harborview Hospital, while rescuers helped the rest of his party out by foot.

And the reward for this ugly, dirty, back-breaking, gut-clenching work? Nothing but the respect of your fellow rescue workers, the gratitude of the person you've aided, and the warmth and satisfaction of being able to help.

"I never get the sense that any of them are looking for glory," says Wick of his colleagues in Seattle Mountain Rescue. One has the clear impression that they would say the same of him.

"They're all pretty rock-solid folks. They're people who have a lot of mountain experience and a lot of skills and I think get some satisfaction out of helping others."

"When you're on a mission and it's a save, you're all pumped up for days."

Mountain Safety 101: How to Avoid Becoming a Customer of Seattle Mountain Rescue

"Use good judgment," says Seattle Mountain Rescue's chairman Douglas Gantenbein. "We see poor judgment more often than anything else. People don't plan their trip properly, leave too late in the day, try to do too much, split a group up."

"People talk about the ten essentials, he says, but the most important essential is between your ears."

Before you go, let a responsible person know where you're going, and when you'll return. Be clear about where you're going and leave an alternate plan in case you change your destination.

Always take the ten essentials, whether it's a hike up Mt. Si or a trip up Mt. Rainier. (The Ten Essentials: map, compass, knife, waterproof matches, firestarter, extra food, extra clothing, sunglasses, first aid kit, and flashlight.)

Before venturing into the mountains, learn as much as you can about backcountry safety. Learn proper techniques from respected outdoors organizations such as the Mountaineers. "Mountaineering: The Freedom of the Hills" is the essential reference book for wilderness travelers.

"Part of our mission is mountain safety and education," says Gantenbein. "We're always available to talk to groups about anything related to safety in the mountains."

Seattle Mountain Rescue can be reached at P.O. Box 67, Seattle, WA, 98111.

If Disaster Strikes

If you're lost, stay put. Sit down and think matters through: can you backtrack to a known location? Make an effort to keep warm and dry.

If you're involved in an accident, approach the scene safely. Don't make matters worse by getting someone else hurt. Perform urgently needed first aid (you did take that first aid class, didn't you?) Then sit down and make a plan. If you need outside help, send someone -- ideally, two people -- out to the nearest phone. Include a map marked with your location, names of people in your party, injuries sustained, and type of help needed.

When you reach the trailhead, contact the local responsible law enforcement agency, usually the county sheriff. In King county, dial 911. Don't call SMR directly -- the group can only operate under the direction of the King County Police.
