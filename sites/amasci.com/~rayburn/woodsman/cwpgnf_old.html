SERIES AND MODELS

There are three series of Woodsman pistols, corresponding to three basic frame designs. First Series refers to all those built on the S frame as it existed prior to and during World War Two. Second Series includes all versions built on the second S frame design from late 1947 until mid 1955, and Third Series means the third S frame design as used from 1955 to the end of regular production in 1977.

Each series had a Sport Model with a 4-1/2 inch round barrel, a Target Model with a 6 or 6-5/8 inch round barrel, and a Match Target Model with a heavy, flat sided barrel. For the first series Match Target that flat sided barrel was 6-5/8 inches in length, while in the post war versions it was either 4-1/2 or 6 inches.

There were also three very similar economy models during the post war years only: the Challenger, the Huntsman, and the Targetsman.

A BASIC WOODSMAN COLLECTION

The following models comprise a representative collection of the Colt Woodsman. In all of them the barrel length is measured from breech to muzzle; ie, from where the bullet goes in to where it comes out. It is NOT measured from where the barrel emerges from the receiver.

First Series (1915-1947)

The first series Woodsman can be easily recognized by its distinctive profile, which resembles the German Luger in the rakish grip angle. The serial number also provides a sure means of identification, since only the first series has no alphabetical suffix to the serial number.

The earliest versions of the first series Target model were originally called simply "Colt Automatic Target Pistol." The Woodsman name was added to the side of the receiver in 1927, so collectors today generally use "Pre-Woodsman" to refer to the earlier versions.

  1. Pre-Woodsman, serial number under 31000, with the thin, 6-5/8 inch pencil barrel; this barrel has a pronounced shoulder that steps the diameter down to .500" just forward of the receiver, then tapers slightly to a diameter of .475" at the muzzle (1915-1922).
  2. Pre-Woodsman, serial numbers 31000-54000, with 6-5/8 inch medium barrel; this has a less pronounced shoulder than the pencil barrel. Muzzle diameter .525" (1922-27).
  3. Woodsman medium barrel Target Model; same as number 2, above, except marked THE WOODSMAN (1927-34).
  4. Woodsman Target Model with 6-5/8 inch barrel, straight tapered from .600" at the receiver to .525" at the muzzle; ie, no shoulder forward of the receiver (1934-47).

FACTORS AFFECTING VALUE

 In this limited space it is not possible to list all the factors that affect value, but the following general guidelines apply:

  • First series Woodsmans (ie, pre-WWII) cost the most money, followed by second and then third series.
  • Match Target models bring the highest prices, followed by Sport and then Target models.
  • Huntsmans are the most readily available of the entire Woodsman line, and bring the least money.
  • A Targetsman will command about 10% more than a comparable condition Huntsman, and a Challenger about 20% more than a comparable Huntsman.
  • A 4-1/2 inch Match Target sells for about 10% more than a comparable 6 inch Match Target in the 3rd series, and about 20% more in the 2nd series.
  • Pre-WWII Match Target Woodsmans are often fitted with replacement stocks, due to the original "Elephant Ear" stocks having been lost or broken. The original stocks bring a very good price by themselves, so a "bargain" in a Bullseye Model with any type of replacement stocks is probably not a true bargain. Except for military guns, which are government marked, nearly all First Series Match Target Woodsmans were equipped with Elephant Ear stocks when they left Colt.

Condition

While the value of a collectible gun is affected by many factors, condition and originality are among the most important. A Woodsman can be a fine shooter, in excellent mechanical condition, but have little collector interest if the bluing is worn or if non-factory modifications have been made. While a well used gun may have a great deal of character, particularly if it has been associated with a person or event known to you, that association is generally transitory. Unfortunately, the rust, scratches, and dings are forever.

For higher grade Woodsmans the value depends on the exact condition. A Woodsman that is 100% new, in the original box, with all original papers and accessories, is worth a LOT more than one that is "almost" as nice. The more collectible the gun is, the more difference the extra fine condition and the accessories make. The difference can range from 50-100% for an early pre-Woodsman or Bullseye Match Target to 20-25% for a 3rd series variety. For a Woodsman that is merely excellent, but not new, the original box and papers alone will add about 20-25% to the price of a pre-war model, and about 10% to a post war version.

Because of the critical effect that condition has on value, a gun that is described as 100% (or New In Box) should be exactly that: absolutely new and perfect in every way, with no blue wear, no so-called box marks, and no imperfections whatsoever.

TAKEDOWN and ASSEMBLY

 The takedown procedure remained essentially the same throughout the 62 years of Woodsman production, with one minor difference between the pre-war and the post-war versions. A mainspring housing retainer pin was added shortly after introduction of the post war Woodsman, and remained a feature until the end of production. For those pistols the retainer pin must first be removed, as per the following Colt instructions from 1948:

Before disassembling your Woodsman...read this

To allow for the extra firm grip some shooters find necessary for accuracy, a main spring housing retainer pin has been installed. Therefore, in disassembling the arm for cleaning, it is necessary to take the following steps before proceeding as outlined under DISASSEMBLY FOR CLEANING:

  1. Remove stocks.
  2. Press in on upper radius of main spring housing and, using stock screw, push out housing retainer pin.
After the retainer pin is removed, the takedown is the same as for the pre-war models. The following instructions are taken from a 1915 Pre-Woodsman instruction booklet.

To Dismount:

Remove magazine by pressing the magazine catch.
(After removing magazine, look to see that there is no cartridge left in the chamber.)
With pistol in left hand, grasp slide between thumb and middle finger of right hand and pull to rear as far as it will go.

Click on the picture for a larger illustration

Then with index finger of right hand, push down assembly lock plunger (on top of slide,) and while holding pressure on plunger allow slide to move forward to closed position. When fully closed, pull trigger and release pressure on assembly lock plunger. (Note: All Second Series Woodsmans, and some Third Series guns made in 1955, have a magazine safety. With them the empty magazine must be inserted before pulling the trigger.)

With thumb of right hand press on housing at point where same is knurled; pressure should be exerted upwards as well as in.

Click on the picture for a larger illustration

This will release housing from receiver and it may be withdrawn; at the same time magazine catch and sear spring (which are pinned together) are released and can be taken from arm.

Slide can now be drawn rearward from receiver.

To Assemble:

Place slide on receiver and push forward to closed position. Insert magazine catch and sear spring in position, being sure that lug on magazine catch fits into slot provided for same in handle, sear spring resting on sear and under hammer strut. The hammer must be in forward position with hammer strut resting on sear spring; (pull trigger to insure hammer being fully forward.)

Place top of housing under end of receiver and resting on sear spring.

Click on the picture for a larger illustration

Press on lower end of housing, causing it to snap into place.

The foregoing is all the dismounting necessary to clean and properly care for the arm.

NOTE: If, at any time, during the taking apart or assembling, the recoil spring should jar off from the assembly lock, it should be again pressed into its forward position and held secure by pressing down the assembly lock plunger.
