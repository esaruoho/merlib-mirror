CURRENT GAIN IS NOT FUNDAMENTAL

The number one reason I could never understand transistors: I was wrongly
taught that the basic operation of transistors was to multiply current. 

Put a milliamp through the base of a transistor, and ten times more
current appears in the collector/emitter circuit.  But try as I might, I
could find no clear explanation of HOW the transistor accomplished this
multiplication. Some textbooks explained it as being a consequence of the
thinness of the base region, but somehow this did not satisfy me at all. 
Other textbook chapters dribbled off into details of holes, fields, and
particle statistics, and never specifically answered the question. 

Years later I realized the truth.  It happened after I had made it a habit
to look for errors in textbooks.  I wondered if the traditional
explanation of transistors might be misguided.  I hadn't thought much
about the subject since college, and had learned quite a bit about solid
state physics since then, so I posessed some new tools for attacking the
topic. 

I found that yes, the traditional explanation is wrong.  The operation of
transistors is NOT based upon current gain.  While it is true that I(b) 
is somewhat proportional to I(c), this is more of a happy accident than a
clue to the workings of the transistor.  It is an artifact of the physics,
and if we could SEE into a transistor and watch it "transist", we would
become aware of how transistors really work. 

Bipolar transistors contain a thin layer of insulator between the base
region and the emitter region of the semicondictor crystal.  This layer is
called the "depletion layer."  The thickness of this insulating layer can
change.  The thickness is controlled by the base-emitter voltage, by the
voltage applied across the layer.  The more positive the voltage, the
thinner the layer becomes, and if the voltage polarity is reversed, the
layer becomes very thick.  When it is thick, almost no charges can flow
from emitter to base.  When it is thin, the layer hardly presents any
barrier to charges at all.  So, raise the Vbe, and you reduce the
thickness of the depletion layer, which makes it less opaque to charges,
which "opens the valve" inside the transistor.  Set Vbe to zero, and the
layer thickens again and closes off the flow. The bipolar transistor is a
valve that's controlled by voltage. 

This is different than I was taught, I was taught that the transistor is
controlled by current.  In fact, when designing a transistor circuit we
assume that Vbe approximately equals 0.7v, never changes!  How can Vbe be
the transistor's controlling input?  What's going on here?  Simple.  When
Vbe is raised a little, the thickness of the insulating layer decreases
almost to zero, and both the current through that layer and also the
LEAKAGE CURRENT THROUGH THE BASE WIRE both increase enormously.  The base
leakage is proportional to the Vbe voltage, though the current increases
very nonlinearly with voltage.  The collector current is also proportional
to Vbe, and is also nonlinear.  And therefor the base current and the
collector current are proportional to each other.  This is where the
seeming "current amplification" comes in to play: 

   The base leakage and the collector current both happen to be
   nonlinear in the same way, so if we ignore Vbe entirely and
   simply compare Ib to Ic, we find that Ic/Ib equals an
   amplification factor.

The nonlinearity cancels out, leaving us APPARANTLY with a current
amplifying device.  The transistor SEEMS to be amplifying current.  But
the main transistor current Ic is really controlled by the width of the
depletion layer, which is controlled by the base-emitter voltage and not
by the base-emitter current.  The base current is a leakage current, and
in an "ideal" transistor the base current could even be zero Raise the
Vbe, the base leakage current goes up a little, while the collector
current goes up a lot. 

The textbooks make a simple mistake: they assume that the base current
somehow controls the thickness of the depletion layer, which then controls
the collector current.  This is wrong, it is backwards.  In fact, the
thickness of the depletion layer controls the base current.  The thickness
of the depletion layer also controls the collector current.  But then what
controls the thickness of this layer?  Our old friend Vbe, which we keep
(wrongly) assuming never changes from 0.7 volts. 



PNP COMMON BASE IN TEXTBOOKS

Some textbooks introduce transistor operation by analyzing the transistor
configuration called PNP COMMON BASE.  Yet the single most common
transistor configuration in use today is the NPN COMMON EMITTER
configuration.  Common-base circuits are extremely rare, and most
electronics people encounter them only in their textbooks. 

I see that I myself had a hard time understanding transistors right
from the start, because I was taught to understand them in terms of
a grounded base and an emitter which emits charges.  This might help
in learning how common-base amplifiers work, but it did me very little
good in understanding most of the real-world amplifiers which did NOT
use the common-base configuration.

Now, if the BASE of a transistor is supposed to be the input, and the
emitter is supposed to be grounded, how can we visualize the "emitter"
as emitting?  We cannot, and hence we cannot visualize how common base
amplifiers work.

And so one key to understanding amplifiers is to give up on the
"common base" viewpoint and "emitter emits charge" concept, and
learn transistor operation in from other standpoints.
