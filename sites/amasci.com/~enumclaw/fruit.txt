 Fruit trees generally need
different growing conditions than rhododendrons, so we use them
in companion beds rather than as companion plants. We have two
orchards (one for rare old apples, and one for trees especially
suited to western Washington) surrounded by rhododendron beds
and connected to the rest of the garden by paths and lawns. Nearby
are the herb and vegetable gardens. Though the plantings are different,
the orchards have the same flowing shape as the rest of the garden. 

 Lady apples, also
 called Pommes d'Api, date back to at least Mideaval times in
 France, and were possibly developed much earlier by the Romans.
 The one to two inch fruit have been used for centuries as Christmas
 decorations, but also add bright color to the garden throughout
 the fall. The small stature and limited root growth of Lady Apple
 trees make them ideal companions for rhododendrons. 

 Other old apples
 in the Anderson Garden include Cox's Orange, Roxbury Russet,
 Fameuse, and Blenheim, and Sheepnose.
