Boo and the Llama

By Nancy J. Hewes
Greetings Llama Llovers: I don't own any llamas, but I greatly admire their grace and beauty. Though this is more a horse story than a llama story, I think you might enjoy it.
I've had horses for about 25 years, and as you may well know, horses don't like llamas unless they have been habituated to them. Their usual first reaction ranges from intense nervous interest to terror. My 16.3 hand (67") Appaloosa gelding Boo, then about 7 years old, had never seen a llama before I moved him into a new boarding stable in Graham Washington. Just down the road was a llama ranch. The first time he caught sight of them grazing peacefully about two hundred yards away, he stopped dead in his tracks and just stared at them in seeming disbelief. After a few moments of pop-eyed staring and sniffing the breeze he began to tremble. I tried to get his attention by "feeling" the reins and squeezing him with my legs. Though well trained, he seemed not to feel anything. I spoke to him gently, stroking his neck and was surprised to find he had broken out in a sweat of pure fear. Though he was standing rooted to the spot I began to wonder if I had made a mistake riding him to the llama farm. I sat the English saddle deeply, waiting for him to explode.
Then one of the llamas moved. His reaction was instantaneous. He spun around like lightning in order to run away from "those monsters", but I applied leg and rein so that the 180 degree spin became a 360. More leg advanced him toward the llamas by about 30 feet. He apparently hadn't realized he had performed a complete spin and his reaction seemed to say, "Oh, no! They're all around me!" He stopped again, snorting and trembling, and I now thoroughly regretted riding him alone to see the llamas. I should have brought along a calm buddy horse or led him from the ground. Lest you think this horse was a chicken, I have to tell you we had been on trail rides into the forest and seen deer and coyotes, and he was totally unafraid of cattle and dogs. Also, Boo was a very intelligent horse most of the time.
The sequence of freeze, snort, 360 spin and advance closer to the llamas was repeated several more times until we got to the fence separating us from them. The llamas, meanwhile, were all staring at us, seemingly mystified by his antics. When we got to the fence he stretched out his neck and sniffed a tuft of wool caught on the wire then seemed to calm down considerably as he tried to eat some grass by the fence. It was then that the group of llamas decided to come over to see what all the fuss was about. I decided that discretion was the better part of valor and withdrew before they got too close. I fully intended to return to the llama ranch at a later time better prepared to accustom Boo to them, but I moved him to a new stable that was unfortunately completely lacking in llamas.
It wasn't until about 6 years later that I was able to introduce Boo to a llama face to face. I had just moved him to a stable near Bridle Trails State Park in Kirkland Washington. Down the road was a small backyard llama breeding operation. I intended to go, sans horse, introduce myself to the owners and ask if I could come and let Boo see their llamas close up. But before that could happen, Boo and I were on our way home from the park one summer evening when I spotted a man with a llama in a school parking lot. It was one of the llamas from down the road. He was letting the llama graze on a patch of grass and Boo hadn't spotted it yet. That day I was riding with a bareback pad with no stirrups so I jumped off and asked the man if I could come closer and let my horse see his llama. He said yes, so I led Boo to within about 50 feet before he saw it. He stopped, head in the air, eyes bugging out, nostrils flaring. Boo stood very still until the llama raised it's head to look. My big brave horse was caught between intense curiosity and gibbering fright. He danced sideways because I wouldn't let him back up or turn around, but this brought him closer to the llama and further away from me, his "herd leader". Then boo changed tactics, he put his head down and tried to hide behind me. The man and I burst out laughing. Here was a 1000+ pound horse trying to hide behind a 100+ pound woman. The look on the llamas face was like, "What's his problem?"
With Boo cowering behind me I stepped closer to the llama. Boo jumped sideways and almost puffed himself up trying to look big and scary. He blew whistling rollers through his nose, bringing peals of laughter from the hominids and disdain from the camelid. The llama, now only about 40 feet away refused to react to the equine display. Boo hid behind me again. I was laughing so hard I couldn't speak, but I continued urging Boo to approach his nightmare. He advanced by degrees, alternately trying to appear fierce and abjectly hiding behind me. When I had worked him onto the grass, to within a few feet of the llama, I reached out and touched the llama on it's back. Boo seemed reassured by this because he suddenly walked right up to the llama, stuck his nose deep into the wool on it's shoulder and took a big whiff. That was surprising enough, but then he dropped his head and began to graze right beside the llamas foot. Will wonders never cease. Boo became completely nonchalant about the proximity of an animal that only moments before had him convinced he would be gobbled up post haste. I was further surprised when the llama swung it's hind quarters, bumped Boo on the shoulder and Boo didn't move. He was no longer afraid of llamas.
Regards, Nancy J Hewes March 15, 1997
