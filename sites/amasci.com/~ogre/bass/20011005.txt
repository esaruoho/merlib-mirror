 Bass for Beginners # 10 
 Published October 05, 2001 
 By Dan Lasley 

 Print this page 
 Email to a Friend 

 Bookmark 
 this page 

 At the Riverside Jam 2001 this summer, I had the pleasure 
 of meeting up with my bass mentor, who I had not seen in 20 
 years. Back then, he was the best bassist I had ever heard, 
 and I would still place him among the best around. So I asked 
 him if he still played, and he said &#147;I don&#146;t have 
 the time to practice enough to do it right.&#148; But sitting 
 next to his wonderful wife, listening him play with the same 
 intensity and confidence as 20 years ago, I felt it sad that 
 he had stopped. I think his wife felt a similar feeling. 
 Coincidently, I recently got into an &#147;argument&#148; 
 over at another bass site, where we were discussing the merits 
 of jamming vs practicing. I proclaimed that &#147;one hour 
 of jamming or gigging is worth 10 hours of practice&#148; 
 by which I meant that you learn more playing with others and 
 really get a good feel for the rhythms and melodies. And 
 besides, it&#146;s more fun, which means it&#146;s inspiring 
 too. Well the reply was &#147;Yup, I practice about 10 hours 
 for every hour I gig.&#148; I decided that this was not a 
 happy person. 
 I have also been in long discussions about how the only way 
 to get better is to learn all the scales and modes, and then 
 &#147;practice in the shed for 2 hours minimum every day&#148;; 
 they even gave it a cute name &#150; &#147;shedding&#148; 
 &#150; as in chopping wood in the woodshed. But a lot of 
 these bassists never get out of the shed. 
 But in all the lessons I have written so far, I have never 
 discussed serious &#147;shedding&#148; or doing scales. I 
 have always advocated playing songs and using &#147;tools&#148; 
 to create your basslines. 
 So let&#146;s have a sanity check&#133; 
 If you can&#146;t find all 12 notes in at least two places 
 on the fretboard, you need to find them. That requires a 
 bit of learning and practice, and some simple scales or songs 
 can help. Good old &#147;Do-Re-Mi&#148; is a wonderful way 
 to learn the notes of the major scale in a melodic fashion. 
 Disclaimer: This is the work of the author and is intended 
 for learning purposes only.

 Don&#146;t forget that a lot of rock music prefers the dominant 
 7 th over the major 7 th . But this is 
 good, as it makes the pattern easier to remember &#150; the 
 ring finger never plays anything. 

 And let&#146;s not forget the minor scales &#150; there are 
 3 of them, called the natural, harmonic and melodic. The 
 natural A-minor has the same notes as the C-major scale, which 
 means that it has the dominant 7 th (G). The harmonic 
 minor uses the major 7 th (G#). The melodic minor 
 uses F# instead of F, which means that it&#146;s the same 
 as A-major except for the 3 rd being C instead of 
 C#. In 90% of the music out there, the natural harmonic will 
 be fine. But if you are curious about how all this works, 
 check out David&#146;s columns on Theory 
 (that&#146;s what I do).

 Now, from a practicing point of view, you should be able 
 to play these 2 scales (3 if you count the different 7 th s) 
 forwards and backwards at a reasonable and constant speed. 
 You don&#146;t need to play it fast, nor should you beat yourself 
 up to play it over and over for hours on end. However, playing 
 scales is a good way to focus on your technique. Practice 
 your hammer-ons by going up the scale and only plucking each 
 new string once. Practice your pull-offs by going down the 
 scale the same way. Practice your rhythms, etc. The idea 
 is that once your fingers have memorized the patterns, you 
 can work on other aspects of your playing. 
 I can hear you all screaming: &#147;Wait a minute, you said 
 we don&#146;t have to practice scales and such, but that&#146;s 
 all you&#146;ve written about!&#148; 
 Let me ask you, is there a difference between &#147;learning&#148; 
 and &#147;practicing&#148;? The answer is yes, your brain 
 can learn something without practicing it, but your fingers 
 can&#146;t. So learn these patterns and teach your fingers. 
 But the real answer is that these things are important for 
 playing songs! And not just &#147;Do-Re-Mi&#148;. The ELP 
 classic &#147;Lucky Man&#148; has a wonderful descending octave 
 scale leading into the break, and Cat Stevens&#146; &#147; Wild 
 World &#148; has one too. Many of the transitions discussed 
 in earlier columns were made using the scale (eg &#147; Somebody 
 to Love &#148;). 
 And the last part is that melodies come from scales, not 
 chords. And melodies make wonderful basslines. In the song 
 &#147;Moondance&#148;, there is a transition from the Am7/Bm7 
 verse into the pre-chorus (or whatever you want to call it) 
 which is usually played straight by the bassist, but I decided 
 that I was going to follow the melody, so I looked at the 
 Am scale and figured out a bassline that goes with the melody.

 So now when your guitarist buddy starts talking about practicing 
 scales, you can say &#147;Yeah I do that too&#148; - for about 
 30 seconds! Practice and learning are related, but they are 
 not the same. You should know how to play a major and a minor 
 scale, and you should practice often enough that your fingers 
 are confident when you play. But don&#146;t feel that you 
 must play scales for 20 minutes every day. Your motivation 
 should be to be able to play the songs you like as well as 
 you want to. Scales are just another tool you need to make 
 you a better bass player. 
 I will repeat what I have said all along, the most important 
 thing is to play songs, and to play them with others. That 
 way you can show off how well you can play your scales &#150; 
 heehee! 
 Dan Lasley 2001-10-05
