An Interview With .Matt Butler

Published April 26, 2002
By David Hodge

Print this page
Email to a Friend
Bookmark this page




.Matt Butler and the Matt Butler Quartet are based in Jacksonville, Florida. To read more about his groundbreaking guitar work, hear his MP3s and find out where and when they are playing, visit .MAtt at www.biodigitalmusic.com.

Who is .Matt Butler? Well, I could tell you that he is the latest addition to the writing staff at Guitarnoise. Or I could simply quote the biographical content from his website:

Most musicians simply play. Most composers simply write. But few musicians, few composers have the audacity, the balls, to question their sonic environment so thoroughly as to doubt it all together, forcing themselves to deconstruct existing musical theory to its most fundamental elements, then, from the ground up, rebuild a new sound world, with elaborate new concepts and philosophies to suit their unique needs. John Zorn, Elliott Carter, Edgard Varese, Miles Davis, Frank Zappa, John Cage, Harry Partch and Phillip Glass are a few names that come to mind. It is in this tradition that Jacksonville musician/composer Matt Butler creates.

First introduced to music by his mother, who would play Rachmaninov on the family piano, Butler began playing keyboard instruments at age 9. With heavy doses of classical music festering in his blood, Butler started composing at 14. His interest soon expanded to guitar, leading Butler down a winding path. His was the way of the fledging renegade composer, shirking classical theory in favor of the more organic need to unleash what was banging around in his head. Though Butler engaged in some "formal" education (he was kicked out of a Jacksonville art-oriented high school, twice, then attended the Grove School of Music), the headstrong guitarist was going in other directions. He was, unwittingly, laying the foundation for an impressive body of work.

The terrain Butler navigated as a young artist was unpredictable as he investigated jazz fusion (Allan Holdsworth, Miles Davis), traditional jazz (John Coltrane, Keith Jarrett), progressive rock (Rush), East Indian music (Ravi Shankar) and the obligatory list of shredders that included Steve Vai and Scott Henderson. As Butler's interests grew, so did his ideas about music and composition - and about performance. In his 20s, Butler began piecing together bands to perform his compositions. Though functioning within a jazz framework, Butler's music never quite fit the mold. Heavy on thematic improvisations, Butler's live gigs became workshops of a sort, where musicians could breathe within Butler's expansive compositions. With the Matt Butler Quartet, the Matt Butler Quintet and the End of the World Orchestra, Butler leveled his sights at the senses of those willing to listen - and he proceeded to obliterate them.

Butler's temperament paralleled his visceral approach to music, and he put his players through a meat grinder. His love for music, and his devotion to its passionate representation, led Butler into disturbing realms. Butler became known for walking away from the bandstand mid-performance leaving a bewildered ensemble - and audience -- behind. His explanation was simple: "I had nothing more to say." Butler would rather preserve the beauty of the moment and leave in tact the integrity of the live performance, than taint it with superfluous nonsense. Groove and feel, sincerity and meaning are of utmost importance when working over a Butler composition, no questions asked.

Butler would often walk away from live performance and even composing as well, frustrated with the lack of appreciation by local audiences and numbed by the purveyance of bad music on the scene. But he'd inevitably resurrect himself time and time again, writing for his smaller groups and even staging concerts of his "classical" compositions at the University of North Florida.

This ideal, this dedication to pure musical performance, continues with Butler's latest ensemble. Comprising Butler on guitar, Joseph Yorio on saxophones, Sean Tarleton on bass and Tim Stombaugh on drums, the 2002 version of the Matt Butler Quartet is twisting Butler's compositions (new and old) into new shapes and forms. It's the melding of years of composition, and reams of Butler's musical concepts and theories (much too detailed to talk about here) into a new assault. Butler's music is as delicate as a butterfly and as intimidating as swiftly approaching cinder block. And as Butler turns yet another corner, don't be surprised if that butterfly just happens to be flinging that cinder block right at your face.

If you've already read his article on Thematic Improvisation, you know that, beyond the standard PR hype, .Matt has some wonderful ideas about music. In order to give our readers more insight into and his thoughts about music in general, and his approach to music in particular, we had a little on-line interview.

Guitar Noise: When you took up the guitar, you had already had several years of piano. Do you feel that this helped you at all?

MB: Well, those years were not 'formal' shedding/study years. The time that I spent on the piano prior to picking up the guitar was more a 'symptom' of the calling as opposed to the beginning of the disciplined path. I would pick out fragments of themes that I liked and improvise with them for hours but I never approached it the way I have the guitar. My approach to exploring the guitar has been very methodical, but structured in such a way that I am free from clichés when I improvise.

GN: Do you use a single guitar in concert? What type? What kind of equipment?

MB: My primary guitar is a Godin Multiac Jazz which runs through a Roland GR33, Boss GT3, and into a Deville.

GN: To someone such as myself, who pretty much absorbs much of his music through song, the idea of totally improvised music seems both enchanting and dangerous. I mean, there must be some structure to start with, right? Could you walk us through what might typically (if you can call this "typical") happen in one of your pieces?

MB: Well, there are a few ways a gig can go: one is that we work from my compositions and develop the improvisations from the thematic sections that these provide. Another way is that we choose very short tone rows and develop those. Finally, the group may take to the 'stage' with no idea what is going to happen at all and we just play.

Now, none of this is to say that we don't exercise good judgement in taste. We don't just play crap, we have a mutual goal within the group and that is to make a cohesive, beautiful, if somewhat dark, musical statement.

The structure of the improvisation is determined by the direction in which the prominent voice is moving. It's up to the other musicians in the group to decide what kind of impression to create around the voice based on what they are hearing.

GN: I'd like to be able to ask you more questions about this on the basis of your answer in order to flesh things out. To play in this style one must have an incredible empathy with one's fellow musicians. How does an individual go about learning this skill?

MB: I don't know if anyone can quantify something that is so intangible, but I can tell you some of what I did.

First, understand that anything you play causes a 'ripple' to occur in the space that you are sharing with the other musicians. They are going to be affected by it, and their reaction will, in turn affect you.

I have always made a clear distinction between what I shed and the actual music that I improvise/play. Early on in my life, I picked about 5 things to shed and have not deviated. I have played these same 5 things in my practice sessions for my entire musical life. My approach is to perfect playing these things, being able to control the tone, dynamics, rhythm, articulation, etc. These are the same musical 'elements' that I would have had to deal with if I was shedding 500 things. I don't know that this is advisable for everyone, but I knew from the time I picked up the instrument that I would only play my own music, so I didn't have anything to lose.

Another thing I do is improvise with CD's of orchestral music, string quartets, solo piano, rap, jazz, etc. I never make an attempt to fit in stylistically, rather I just play something that makes melodic and rhythmic sense. Sometimes this sounds perfectly in step with what is going on, other times it clashes and presents more of a challenge to make music out of it.

On a macro level, I learned to play the guitar without learning any specific music on it so when it comes time to make music I am free to just deal with what is my head as opposed to what is in my fingers.

I also had a vague idea of how my music would sound from the beginning; I just didn't know that I did. In my head I had this hazy, romantic vision of some guitarist's group improvising amazing music. I always thought that was my vision of those 'great unknown' players that you hear about in music stores and colleges… you know what I mean: "yeah man, there's this 15 year old kid in Alabama that is amazing! He's so this and that…!" Then your head fogs over and you start to see a vision of this amazing guitarist playing sounds that you have never heard before. Guess what. That's you! Just as it was me in my head! Those sounds in my head were mine, they didn't belong to the 15 wonder kind in Alabama. Over the years that vision has become as clear as looking in the mirror.

I was also lucky enough to grow up around musicians whose sole aim was to develop their own voice and play improvisational music, and that has continued to the present time. (Jacksonville, Florida, man… You would be surprised at some of the talent that has come through my quartet!)

GN: How on earth did you find a theremin player? In Florida no less?

MB: Well, Joseph (Yorio) plays many, many instruments. Theremin being one of them, but he hasn't played it with the group, yet.

GN: Do you have any current projects in development?

At the bottom of the queue is a view into the realities of war that will probably make most people yack. Other than that I am working on a book that will explain my approach to improvising music and a guitar non-method. As always I play and compose when the mood strikes.

David Hodge 2002-04-26
