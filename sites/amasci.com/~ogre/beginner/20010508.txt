 One of the easiest ways to quickly improve your guitar playing 
 is to start thinking of your instrument as being more than 
 just a single guitar. What am I talking about? Well, once 
 you&#146;ve gotten the strumming part down and have a fairly 
 good grasp (no pun intended) of the various chords, you can 
 start to put together actual arrangements of songs. 
 Even a relative beginner can get into this in a big way and 
 you will be amazed at the simple things you can do to dramatically 
 improve your playing, simply by approaching a song with a 
 little thought and a little theory. 
 These files are the author's own work and 
 represents his interpretation of this song. They are intended 
 solely for private study, scholarship or research.

 So as you can see, for all intents and purposes, we&#146;ve 
 already got this song down cold. Ah, but that would not be 
 much of a lesson then, would it? 
 As I (perhaps cryptically) mentioned at the start of this 
 lesson, if you begin to think of your guitar as &#147;more 
 than just a single guitar,&#148; then you can begin to reach 
 a new and exciting level of playing. As beginners, guitarists 
 tend to worry about one aspect of playing at a time and that 
 is probably a good thing. But there comes a point where you 
 have to branch out a bit and today we&#146;re going to take 
 a step in that direction. 
 The guitar is what we call a &#147;polyphonic&#148; instrument. 
 This is a fancy way of saying that it is capable of playing 
 more than one note at a time. It&#146;s like a piano and most 
 other stringed instruments and unlike, say, a trumpet or a 
 flute. 
 But it often takes the average guitarist a while (and sometimes 
 quite a while) to figure this out. We are too busy learning 
 chords and strumming and riffs and single-note leads and we 
 do not always appreciate that our guitars can provide us with 
 as deep and richly textured an accompaniment as a piano. 
 The easiest thing to do, as a beginner, is to learn to develop 
 a bassline as part of a strumming pattern. We did this right 
 from the start in our very first &#147;Easy 
 Songs For Beginners &#148; piece, Horse 
 With No Name . Take a second and look that over if 
 you&#146;ve forgotten. 
 It would be a fairly easy thing to add 
 a bassline (more accurately a bass note) to our strumming 
 pattern. The most common thing to do is to play it on the 
 first beat of every measure. Since we have a lot of space, 
 though, let&#146;s use it on the third beat as well. Here, 
 then, is our &#147;bass enhanced&#148; pattern. I have replaced 
 the downstrokes on the first and third beats with the letter 
 &#147;B&#148; which means that we&#146;ll play a bass note 
 instead of a full chord on the downstroke. Our bass note in 
 this instance is simply the root note of what- ever chord 
 we are playing. For a D chord, it will be the open D string. 
 On the A (or A7) chord, the open A string. The G note on the 
 third fret of our low E string will serve as bass note for 
 the G chord. Go on and give them a try:

 Once you&#146;ve got this down, the next step is to give 
 your bass line a bit more spice. The simplest (and yet very 
 cool sounding) way for a guitarist to do this is to develop 
 what is called an &#147;alternating bassline.&#148; This means 
 exactly what you&#146;d think it might. You alternate the 
 root note with a different note in the bass. Traditionally, 
 the fifth is the note that alternates with the root. In the 
 strumming pattern that we&#146;re using on Margaritaville , 
 it is easy to play the root on the first beat (followed by 
 the chord on the second) and the fifth on the third beat (again

 Playing the D and A chords should be fairly easy, since both 
 the roots and fifths are simply open strings. Even better, 
 the fifth is the open string below the root (you&#146;d almost 
 think that someone planned the guitar that way, wouldn&#146;t

 Now the G chord is also easy, although it might seem a bit 
 confusing at first. Whenever possible, it&#146;s a good idea 
 for the root note to be the lowest tone (of that particular 
 chord) that you can attain on your guitar. If your fifth happens 
 to be lower than that, as it was on both the D and A, then 
 great. On the G, our root is the third fret on the low E string, 
 no problem. But our nearest fifth is above the root. 
 In fact, it is the open D string. So be it, you have to take 
 what your guitar will give you (unless you decide to play 
 this in a &#147;Drop D&#148; tuning and that&#146;s a whole 
 other lesson!). 
 But I&#146;ve decided something else &#150; since the G only 
 appears in the chorus and it is always followed by the A, 
 I am also going to play those A chords in the same style, 
 that is, with the higher fifth. It should make for an interesting 
 contrast and also keep me from playing the same thing over 
 and over. Here&#146;s our TAB for the G as well as the &#147;chorus&#148;

 Since I&#146;m already tinkering around arranging things, 
 let&#146;s do two more easy steps. First off, on the penultimate 
 line in the chorus (&#147;&#133;some people claim&#133;&#148;) 
 I am going to simple hold the last three chords, just like 
 on the recording. Two beat hold on the D and A chords with 
 a whole four beat hold on the G. This way I do not have to 
 worry at all about a bassline! 
 Secondly, I am going to do a short &#147;walking&#148; bassline 
 between the D and G chords in the chorus, like this: 

 This also offers a bit of variety and keeps the bass part 
 from being too static. The trick of any walking bassline is 
 to make certain that you arrive at the proper note at the 
 proper time. No problem here. Since the G is on the first 
 beat, I simply play F# (second fret on the low E) the previous 
 (fourth) beat and play the open E string the beat before that. 
 Piece of cake. 
 So you see, with a minimum of effort, we&#146;ve taken a 
 song that, in all likelihood, we would have simply strummed 
 and given it some added depth and color. 
 Okay, this covers about everything. Oh, wait a minute - as 
 a bonus I wrote out the &#147;signature riff&#148; that is 
 played as the intro. You can also use this to end the song 
 if you want to sound like the recording. Just tack it on to 
 the end off the last chorus. The reason I&#146;m doing this 
 is a bit of a tease. It&#146;s a very simple thing to do and 
 we soon will be covering the theory behind it and a lot of 
 other &#147;quick and easy lead licks&#148; over on the Guitar 
 Columns page. Most likely in June or July. Please don&#146;t 
 think that it will be too hard for you; I think you will surprise 
 yourself at how easily you both understand and master some 
 of these simple tricks. It&#146;s like almost anything with 
 music theory - once you 
 get over the fact that it&#146;s neither as hard nor as boring 
 as you thought, there is no end to the fun you can have. Or 
 to the good things it can do for your playing.
