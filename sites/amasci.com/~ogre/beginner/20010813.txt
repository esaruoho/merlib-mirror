 I Shot The Sheriff 

 Easy Songs For Beginners # 12 
 Published August 13, 2001 
 By David 
 Hodge

 Sometimes I look at these &#147;Easy Songs for Beginners&#148; 
 and I have to wonder if I&#146;m going to get tarred and feathered. 
 Oh, it&#146;s not that they are hard (they aren&#146;t) or 
 that even the theory involved is too difficult (I truly hope 
 it hasn&#146;t been), but I just hope that everyone is having 
 fun with them. And is patient with me. You may have noticed 
 I have this tendency to try to cram a lot into these lessons. 
 It&#146;s not always easy explaining some of the things we&#146;ve 
 covered purely with words and diagrams and I am not always 
 certain I&#146;ve gotten things across in the best possible 
 way. But you can always write me for more details. Ah well, 
 enough digression... 
 August is &#147; musical genres &#148; 
 month here at the Online Guitar College and I have to admit 
 that my picking this topic was a sneaky way to slip some lessons 
 into the mix. Today&#146;s song is Bob Marley&#146;s I 
 Shot The Sheriff , which will not only serve as a delightful 
 introduction to reggae rhythms, but I&#146;ve also managed 
 to get very sneaky and throw in some transposing as well. 
 Okay, mon, where&#146;s the disclaimer? 
 These files are the author&#146;s own work 
 and represents his interpretation of this song. They are intended 
 solely for private study, scholarship or research. 
 Before we go on, you might also want to read this little 
 bit on &#147; Reggae Style 
 on Guitar .&#148; It contains a bit of history on the genre 
 (and before you note that it&#146;s not quite in my usual 
 style, let me say that I lifted it straight from the Encyclopaedia 
 Britannica) as well as some ideas on rhythm that we will expand 
 upon later. 
 STEP 1 &#150; CHORDS 
 Let&#146;s look at the chords and tranposition first, shall 
 we? On the CD that I am using (&#147;Legend&#148;), this song 
 is in the key of G minor. How do I know that? Well, if you&#146;ve 
 read some of my past columns, particular the trio on ear 
 training , you&#146;d know that I sat down with my guitar 
 and the CD and played around until I got the chord progression. 
 In terms of song structure, Sheriff can be broken down 
 into two parts: the chorus (the &#147;I shot the sheriff&#148; 
 part) and the intervening verses, which tell the story. There 
 is also a little instrumental riff which separates the two, 
 but we will come back to that later (good lord! More 
 things to learn!!) 
 The chorus has two alternating chords, Gm and Cm, and goes 
 like this: 
 Gm (4 beats) Cm (4 beats) 
 Gm (8 beats) 
 I shot the sheriff 
 but I did not shoot the deputy 
 Gm (4 beats) Cm (4 beats) 
 Gm (8 beats) 
 I shot the sheriff but 
 I did not shoot the deputy 
 The verses have the following chord progression (the number 
 of beats is in parenthesis): 
 Eb (2) Dm (2) Gm (4) 
 All around in my home town 
 Eb (2) Dm (2) Gm (4) 
 They&#146;re trying to track me down 
 Now I don&#146;t know about you, but this does not at all 
 look like an &#147;Easy Song For Beginners!&#148; But take 
 heart! You have all read my column on transposing called &#147; Turning 
 Notes Into Stone &#148; (and if you haven&#146;t now&#146;s 
 your chance to sneak out and do so! Don&#146;t worry, we&#146;ll 
 wait right here for you&#133;), so you&#146;re all whizzes 
 at the art of transposing. 
 Ready to make this a bit easier? Alright, then, since I have 
 no intention of playing this as nothing but barre chords, 
 I have to come up with another key in which to play it. To 
 me, there are two obvious choices &#150; A minor and E minor. 
 As you&#146;ve read, I prefer use a key lower in pitch whenever 
 possible so that, with the aid of my trusty capo, I can still 
 play along with the CD. So I opt for E minor. E is one and 
 a half steps down from G, so all my other chords are going 
 to have to be one and a half steps down as well. Let&#146;s 
 figure it out:

 my capo on the third fret (that&#146;s one and a half steps, 
 remember?), then I am playing in G minor again. So here are 
 the chords we&#146;re going to use: 

 Let me point out that there are a lot of ways to play the 
 Bm chord (yes, not to mention every chord!). For this 
 song, I prefer to use this voicing and we&#146;ll look at 
 the reasons for this shortly. But first, let&#146;s get our 
 rhythm down. 
 STEP 2 &#150; RHYTHM 
 Having made the chords user-friendly, let&#146;s see what 
 we can do about the rhythm. Like all the things we&#146;ve 
 been doing, we&#146;ll start out fairly simply and then get 
 a bit more complicated as we feel the confidence to do so. 
 Playing a reggae rhythm guitar involves playing on the offbeat. 
 Just what does that mean? Well, let&#146;s say we have a song 
 in 4 / 4 time (and, thinking about it, I can&#146;t remember 
 ever playing a reggae song that wasn&#146;t in 4 / 4 time), 
 you would count it out like so: 

 Nothing could be simpler, right? This is how we count the 
 beat of any song, at least any song done in 4 /4 time. But 
 we already know from our past lessons that we can subdivide 
 this into eighth notes or into triplets: 

 Here you see that we have notes in between the &#147;beats,&#148; 
 between the actual number count if you will. This is the &#147;offbeat.&#148; 
 To get the reggae rhythm, we will strum our guitar only on 
 the offbeat, that is we will strum on the &#147;and&#148; 
 or the &#147;and a&#148; parts of the measure. 
 And let&#146;s take a moment here to talk about tone. Reggae 
 guitar tends to have a very clipped sound (or &#147;chunky,&#148; 
 as the good book tells us). There are several ways to get 
 this effect. The easiest way is to use an upstroke when you 
 strum. When I play reggae in an all upstroke style on my guitar, 
 I will actually slap my palm on top of the strings after the 
 upstroke. This accentuates the pause that takes place on the 
 beat. When I do use downstrokes, I find I get good control 
 of the tone by resting my palm directly on the strings when 
 I stroke. This is particularly useful in a triplet rhythm. 
 Upstrokes also bring out the higher strings, or the treble 
 part, of the guitar and most reggae guitar has a nice treble 
 tone to it. This is also another reason I like to use the 
 capo to position my chord voicings higher up on the neck, 
 thus bringing more treble into the chords. 
 If you&#146;re using an electric guitar, try to keep your 
 tone as clean as possible. Avoid effects such as distortion. 
 Even chorus or echo can really clutter things up a lot, so 
 try to listen carefully to what you&#146;re doing. Reggae 
 should have lots of breathing space. 
 And don&#146;t think you can&#146;t play reggae on your acoustic 
 guitar. The percussive sounds you can get from strumming an 
 acoustic are wonderful for this style of music. 
 Before you even get going on the rhythm, take some time to 
 experiment with the sound that you want. You really should 
 do this for any song you work on. The tone you achieve with 
 your guitar, with or without an amp and effects, will be a 
 big factor in how your rhythm sounds. 
 Okay, having said that, let&#146;s give the rhythm a shot. 
 Let&#146;s start out simply with the eighth notes. Remember 
 to take it as slowly as you need in order to do it right. 
 Once you get the hang of it you&#146;ll be amazed at how fast 
 you can go. All right then, we&#146;ll use the Em chord, one 
 upstroke on each offbeat. Ready? 

 If you want to see if you&#146;re doing this correctly, try 
 counting the beat aloud while you strum. Seriously. When you 
 count the beat (just the &#147;one, two, three, four&#148; 
 &#150; don&#146;t say the &#147;ands&#148;) you shouldn&#146;t 
 be playing at all when you speak. Once you feel that you&#146;ve 
 got a handle on this, do it again using only downstrokes. 
 Remember to keep them short and clipped. Then mix it up &#150; 
 try both upstrokes and downstrokes. See what you like, where 
 and how you get a sound that you like. 
 When you feel comfortable play the rhythm in eighth notes, 
 then you can give the triplets a try. There are lots of ways 
 to approach this. Personally, I tend to do a downstroke ( D ) 
 followed by an immediate upstroke ( U ), like this: 

 Okay, you&#146;ve got both of those rhythms down cold, right? 
 Now comes the fun part &#150; mixing them together. Up &#145;til 
 now we&#146;ve been doing this with only one chord but you&#146;ll 
 see that these exercises will get you going on changing chords 
 as well. Again (always), start out as slowly as necessary. 
 Don&#146;t move on to the next line of the exercise until 
 you feel that you&#146;ve mastered the one you&#146;re on: 

 That&#146;s a lot of fun, isn&#146;t it? That last exercise 
 pattern, by the way, is the pattern I usually use to play 
 I Shot The Sheriff . Okay, one more step to go and we&#146;re 
 ready! 
 STEP3 &#150; BASS and FILL 
 You&#146;re actually ready to go on and play the song right 
 now if you&#146;d like. The purpose of this section is to 
 give you some added pizzazz should you want some. 
 If you&#146;re playing solo, reggae can be very disorienting. 
 There is no bass to root your rhythm. But in learning to play 
 the way we have, you can see that are spaces in which to fill 
 in the bass. Do you see them? The rests that we don&#146;t 
 play provide a natural place for it. In other words, we will 
 play a bass note on the beat while playing the 
 rhythm on the offbeat . I told you I can be sneaky 
 sometimes... 
 Using an alternating bassline, as we did in Margaritaville , 
 will work very well in the chorus section of the song: 

 Now take your time with this. And don&#146;t get frustrated 
 if it takes you a number of attempts to get it right. After 
 all, you&#146;ve just learned how to do the rhythm and I&#146;ll 
 be more than willing to bet that you haven&#146;t even thought 
 about the alternating bassline since we saw it last. If it 
 helps, revert to doing a straight eighth note pattern until 
 you feel you&#146;ve got it down. 
 The verses are a little trickier, and now you&#146;ll see 
 why I wanted to use this particular voicing of the Bm chord. 
 What we&#146;re actually doing is a &#147;Bm/D.&#148; When 
 you see a chord with a &#147;/&#148; in it, it is normally 
 understood that the actually chord is in front of the &#147;/.&#148; 
 The note behind it is supposed to be your bass note, or the 
 lowest note in the chord. Since we are going from C to Bm 
 to Em, and since the D note is part of the Bm chord, I have 
 decided to do a straight ascending scale from C to E. Then 
 I revert back to an alternating bass pattern on the Em: 

 But here&#146;s something interesting - when I do the alternating 
 bassline on the Em, the last voiced bass note is a B which 
 naturally leads us back to the C. Almost as if someone had 
 planned it that way. 
 Another way of going about playing these rhythms, believe 
 it or not is in fingerstyle. Using your thumb to play the 
 bass parts while employing your three fingers to pluck the 
 first three strings for the accompanying chords gives you 
 a great sound and excellent control over the rhythmic pattern. 
 If you&#146;re into this sort of thing, go back over the last 
 two examples (the chorus and the verses) using this technique. 
 Your thumb will play the bass notes (downturned stems) and 
 use your ring finger (1 st (high E) string), middle 
 finger (2 nd (B) string) and index finger (3 rd 
 (G) finger) to pluck out the chords. 
 Okay, one last thing. At the end of each verse, there is 
 a riff that is played by pretty much all the instruments in 
 unison. You&#146;ll see that I&#146;ve TABbed it out right 
 after the first verse. Having the guitar with the capo on 
 the third fret certainly helped to make things easier, didn't&#146;it? 
 Well then, you&#146;re ready to go and play! 

 I hope you&#146;ve enjoyed this little excursion into reggae. 
 Next time out we&#146;re going to learn an old Dylan song. 
 But before you start groaning, let me add that it&#146;s my 
 way of teaching you the basics of slide guitar&#133;
