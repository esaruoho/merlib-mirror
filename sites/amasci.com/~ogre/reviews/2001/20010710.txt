 Authors: George Forrester and others 
 Title: The Show That Never Ends 
 Website: Helter 
 Skelter Publishing 
 You can find several books out on Jethro Tull and Yes. Oddly 
 enough, there wasn&#146;t one on ELP. I say oddly as they 
 were the biggest-selling band in the &#145;70s. They were 
 also the most bashed-upon band of their time. Critics loved 
 to hate them. 
 Considered by them as &#147;pretentious&#148; and &#147;self-indulgent&#148;, 
 most of them, the British in particular, just didn&#146;t 
 want to see into the music. Why did they seem to be pretentious? 
 Emerson explains it rather well. &#147;You have to be self-indulgent 
 to do something new.&#148; 
 Finally, there is a book on the band. An interesting one 
 at that. This books starts off before ELP were formed. Obviously, 
 most people know that Emerson came from The Nice , that 
 Lake was with King Crimson and 
 that Palmer was with Atomic Rooster . And that they 
 broke up three bands to form one. Yet, beyond that, very little 
 of their past history is ever discussed. 
 So, it&#146;s nice to find out that Emerson was into classic 
 Jazz music, that Lake&#146;s former bandmates from The 
 Gods ended up as Uriah Heep and that Palmer 
 was also into Jazz and not into Rock and Pop at all. 
 The book is a concise look into the band&#146;s history. 
 Don&#146;t look for dirt on the band members here, you won&#146;t 
 find it. Enough has been said in the past, anyway, about all 
 the goings-on during the tours, etc. 
 Instead, you&#146;ll find a lot of facts about the band and 
 learn how they came about making such music as they did. 
 There are also very interesting passages. A couple in regards 
 to comments made from Lake regarding 
 Eric Clapton . &#147;Cream missed me by a mile, and 
 I think that was because once you&#146;d seen Hendrix you&#146;d 
 seen the thing done properly, if know what I mean&#148;. And 
 this more interesting one where &#147; Lake 
 made a historic, if tongue-in-cheek, faux pas by saying 
 he&#146;d love to play with Clapton &#147;just to see if [Clapton] 
 could keep time&#148;!&#148; 
 All in all, the authors do a fine job of sifting through 
 thirty years of history to come up with a comprehensible story 
 of the band, it&#146;s members and their motivations. 
 Every album is examined in detail and every song is also 
 detailed. The authors&#146; treatment of the albums and songs 
 is excellent. They really know the material. With only one 
 exception: &#147;Still... You Turn Me On&#148;. The author 
 states &#147;While the song is musically uncomplicated&#148;. 
 Try playing it on the guitar. Lake &#146;s 
 fingers must be at least six-inches longer than mine in order 
 to play the damn piece! And the song has so many textures 
 from keyboards, strings, acoustic guitar and electric guitar, 
 that it is perhaps one of the most complicated pieces the 
 band ever did. 

 I must, however point out, that I found it odd that the Montreal 
 Olympic Stadium show, deemed by the band as their best show 
 ever, turned into a live album and a live video, played with 
 a 60-piece orchestra, is hardly discussed beyond the point 
 that the band still hold the attendance record at Olympic 
 Stadium, having a bigger attendance than the Olympic games 
 themselves. I have to add that the attendance was even larger 
 than when the pope went there. 
 A-J Charron 2001-07-10
