## Whole Lot o' Shakin'....

\

Things were a bit up in the air Wednesday... for about 40 seconds while the ground heaved as if it wanted to brush us all off its back. I suppose it must have seemed prophetic to some, the date being Ash Wednesday, a holy day which I am assured is quite solemn and important. (Being not even remotely Christian, much less a Catholic, I'm really not at all sure, but my friends of the church-going variety assure me this is true.) It probably came as a bit of a shock to see what Nature (or whatever) had wrought by the time they exited their churches.

In my own neighborhood, the apparent damage from our 6.8 magnitude earthquake was one broken plate glass window. Cell service immediately went south and my own service in that department remained down for 12 hours (if you're ever in an emergency situation in the Washington area, don't bother with Sprint PCS: they suck.) This pissed me off as my husband was in California and I had no way to reach him and tell him I was fine.

(The Germans probably have a word for the special type of frustration that enfolds you when you cannot reassure someone of your own condition. The gods know, they have words to describe all sorts of other ridiculous emotions.)

But, aside from that and the presence of my Mother, who drives me batty, this little bit of rock and roll was of little impact for me and I assumed that this was typical.

I was wrong. Seattle was hard-hit in places, even though it is miles from the epicenter. This town is full of old, brick and stone buildings and many didn't weather this storm of seismic violence with perfect aplomb. There's a lot of damage in the historic district. One building that survived even our famous fire of 1898, is going to be torn down because it couldn't shrug the damage off, this time. Most of the front wall of the Fenix collapsed onto the street.

The train station, which represents Seattle's epic battle with the Union Pacific railroad (we decided to build our *own* damned railroad rather than buckle to the UP, though, in the end, Seattle joined up to the Great Northern instead) may not be functional for a while. (Insultingly enough, Union Station across the street, recently converted and remodeled into a convention and meeting center, was fine. The Union Pacific finally won a battle with Seattle. Too bad for them that they sold the building to a private concern just a few years ago.)

The streets in Pioneer Square, which were recovering from a bad case of Mardi Gras excess just the night before in which one man died, are still alitter with fallen bricks and shattered glass.

And this less than a month after the gorgeous iron and glass pergola in that district was flattened by a truck.

Ironically, the one building everyone hoped would finally give up the ghost and lie down, didn't. Up at the University of Washington, the horrible Seig Hall (I always wonder what possessed them to allow that name), in spite of raining mortar and plaster on people for years, stayed up. "Better luck next quake" was the message someone sent to the Chairman of Computer Science and Engineering, housed in that building.

Seattle is a weird place, though, and we have a history of rising to an emergency with brilliant avarice. Back in the old days, all we had was trees and mud, so we cut down the trees and skidded them on the mud to the shore and sold them to rebuild San Francisco. When our own town burned down, we rebuilt in brick and stone and raised the streets to install a proper sewer. When there were more men around who wanted wives than there we single girls to be had, a fellow named Asa Mercer started a subscription drive and recruited Civil War widows and orphans to come here and be married.

In the wreckage of the buildings and the 7,000 miles of road and the 3,300 bridges to be inspected, someone will find a goldmine, to be sure. This time it may take a while, but, as the owner of the club said: "the Fenix will rise again" and so will Seattle. (Though it looks like we'll all be giving up on something for Lent, whether we normally participate or not.)

Let's face it: any town that is perverse enough to install an heroic statue of Lenin in the patio of a taco stand won't stay lying down for long.

And on that note... I have to apologize for not getting the final segment of Suit and Tie up in a timely fashion. The visiting relative and this little contretemps with Nature did put a bit of a crimp in my style, but that's insufficeint excuse. I'm scrambling as quickly as I can to finish the story and get it ready for you guys. Please bear with me. Things are a bit... odd here at the moment.

And the [POD](../PODSuba.html) anim is moving to the gallery, as soon as I can redesign that page.

## Color-change

\

Well, I think I'm going to have to stop calling myself a blonde.... From now on, I shall be a calico, I think.

As I've gotten older, my hair has not been able to make up its mind what it wants out of life. (Rather like the rest of me....) First it was a pale blonde, then it got darker, then it turned yellower, then lighter again.... now it's gone a funny combination of colors ranging from slate to silver, brown to pale gold. (No, I don't color it, it just does this stuff, itself.)

Sometimes I have the urge to bleach it all platinum and then color the tips something *outré*, like purple or green.

I expect the official change from Blonde to Calico Kat to be beneficial, over all. One of my friends declares a certain distaste for "blondes", not the hair color, the attitude, and has decided that I can't possibly be blonde, because I'm not air-headed or venal enough.

Is hair color an attitude? Can one be a brunette in blonde hair? Are brunettes really smarter than blondes? Are people with black hair more dangerous? Are platinum blondes, as of Hollywood's golden age, sexier? Do red-heads have fiery tempers? Realistically, no. I know people who do fit these stereotypes, sure, but just as many who don't. But do we, inadvertently, fit ourselves and our friends into little boxes because of it?

I always liked being a blonde, though the blonde jokes get old really fast.... I've always thought it a particularly nice color: bright, sunny (camouflage, you know). I never considered myself silly or air-headed or mercenary. I doubt anyone else ever has, either, but it was kind of strange to think that, despite the fact that my hair is undeniably blonde, at least in streaks at the present time, I had been ruled a non-blonde by my friend. For attitude reasons, of course.

Is one defined by what one is not? Sounds terrible, doesn't it? I'm not tall, by most standards, nor am I short. Not fat, but not thin. Not excruciatingly blonde, but not really a brunette. I am "not" many things, but I am also decidedly some things. Most of these are things I either can't or won't change. Many are things I fought to become or acquire.

I'm an amalgam of parts: writer, wife, ex-journalist, sailor, nut-case.... Not a deep well of knowledge, but a broad and spreading puddle, a patchwork of ideas and stolen bits of who and what. I hold together pretty well for all that I seem to be made of parts.

A calico. Yeah, I guess I'm a calico....

## Copy, copy, copyright

\

This business with Napster is really pissing me off, still. Well, the more I think about this, the more annoyed I become and so I am about to shoot myself (figuratively) in the foot (possibly in the figurative head...).

Now, as someone whose livlihood is based on copyright issues and exercising and protecting those, some of this may sound a bit hypocritical, but just bear with me.

The noisiest opponents of Napster want us all to believe that artists are being irreparably damaged in their copyright protection by file swapping. Yep, a few people with very recent copyrights are not getting their due and so are a few people with really old copyrights that are not being exercised. But I suspect that, over all, the recording industry is in debt to Napster more than they would like to admit and that their real fear is not loss of revenue, but loss of control.

I don't know about you guys, but If it weren't for Napster there are about a dozen albums I would not have purchased this year. Napster downloads made it possible for me, who has no TV and only intermittent radio reception, to listen to music I would not have been exposed to otherwise. In most cases, I was referred to music and bands I'd never heard of or had dismissed. I went to Napster, downloaded a few, listened and liked them. So, I wrote down the artist, the albums, and went and bought them (many at full price, not used or discounted) because I knew I liked at least three or more cuts from the recording. So, I'm now the very happy owner of a bunch of music I really like, thanks to those evil file swappers.

Frankly, it's not always convenient for me to listen to music on my computer and I don't own a freestanding MP3 player of any kind. Besides, the quality and predictability of downloads from Napster is spotty at best. Who wants to deal with second-rate stuff when you can spend a little bit of cash and have a nice copy all of your very own? I doubt that I am in some kind of weirdo minority in this. Everyone I've asked has said that they've done the same thing. Some may not have done it to the same extent, but they are, most likely, buying more recordings than they used to and enjoying them more, since they know what they are going to get before they shell out the \$12 to \$17 per CD.

How many times have you bought a recording because it had a piece on it you loved, only to discover it was the only piece on the whole disc which you could stand? I have way too many CDs lying about to which I never listen, because I only like one or two songs on the whole thing. Once you've slit the seal on the CD, you can't take it back and say "I hate this". Most shops won't let you. Heck, you'd think that if they'll let you return a pair of pants which have been all snuggly-intimate with your body they'd let you bring back a compact disc, which is clearly the same one that left the store. Are they afraid you've been licking it, or something? Or worse: copying it? Horrors!

Additionally, I've seen a lot of stuff on Napster which is so rare and hard to find that you might as well forget trying to get a copy in decent condition for a decent price. One of my friends is a big fan of a certain radio show from the 30s. You can't get many of the recordings at all. They simply aren't available in a standard format. But he now owns several years' worth of the show, because he found a fellow fan on Napster who managed to get copies off the original recordings. This isn't a matter of "robbing" an artist who is making millions, or even paltry thousands, with a major recording contract. These recordings don't represent a big enough return for any major distribution company to bother pressing them. My friend would be glad to pay for better-quality re-mastered recordings, if they could be had, but they can't. Technically, my friend and his fellow fans are committing a crime. The copyright is not in the public domain, just yet.

It would be great if some kind of payment could be made to these artists (the gods know that these forgotten artists deserve it more than most), but there is no one to pass the payment through. These folks ought to be getting the money they are due, but, short of that, at least these things aren't dropping into oblivion. Maybe some new fans will crop up, referred by other fans to Napster.

Oh, but no. That won't happen, will it? Napster is supposed to block all copyrighted material. Well, so long as the recording and distributing company sends a list of the artist, album, titles and all possible versions of the title. Totally toothless, which is fine by me, since the whole ruling represents the court kissing the collective ass of the music distribution industry (note that I don't say "the music industry"), but it's going to seriously screw folks like me and my friend, who use Napster as a resource, not just a place to rip someone off. And I think that's probably true of more of the users than it is not.

The folks screaming the loudest about Napster's potential for "theft" turn out to be, not the artists, but the distributors. Funny, isn't it? Especially when you look at the profit margins. Believe me, it's not the artists or the record stores who are potentially losing money.

The real issue seems to me to be the potential loss of control by the distribution companies. They don't like the idea that someone can help themselves and not fork over the lion's share of the profits to leeches like them.

This is pretty much the same thing that is going on with publishing, too. Except that most of the traditional publishing industry has chosen to bury it's head and try to pretend that the internet doesn't pose the greatest opportunity for low-cost, low-interference distribution in the history of publishing.

As a struggling writer, I find the issue both intriguing and frightening. The 'net offers someone like me far more opportunities to get published without kissing butts and "doing time". But it's a lot harder to get paid for it. It's harder to get people to look at your stuff when it is not represented by a major publisher and placed in bookstores and reviewed by the New York Times.

From my point of view, as an owner of intellectual property, I would much rather have control of my stuff, myself, but I also want to make money on it. Usually, this requires a compromise, wherein I sign a contract with a publisher to print, distribute and publicize my work in exchange for a piece of the profit. But the thing that's always struck me is that I am the one who put, in some cases, years of effort into creating the work, but it's the publisher, the printer and the distributor who get the lion's share of the money.

And they are also the people who, ultimately, end up controlling my success, not me, because a big publishing company can decide to sink me and my book by not pushing it, by burying it, by marketing it poorly. They may not even do this on purpose, but simply because they have thousands of other books to worry about, too. Something like Napster would at least give my work some kind of wide-market access. Then I just have to figure out how to get paid for it....

And there's the real problem: not that some people are losing a few pennies per transfer to file swappers, but that the 'net as it currently exists, does not provide a good way for artists of any kind to be assured of getting the recognition and pay they deserve for their work. Eventually, someone is going to solve this problem, but then a whole new battle will begin as the entities who currently hold control of distribution, and therefore of public access and, ultimately, money, attempt to maintain control of these things. They won't be able to do it, but it's going to be an ugly battle. In a way, the Napster case is the most visible of the front-line skirmishes which will continue for a while as the larger issue comes to a head and, eventually, get's resolved.

The chaotic nature of the internet, the true free-market that it represents, does not hand over monopolies and exclusives. Ability and quality rise to the top, not mediocrity and intimidation. (Yeah, yeah... "what about AOL and Microsoft?" They're doomed if they remain the stupid, slow-moving, ever-chewing maw that they have become, recently. Evolve or die, folks. You know it's true.)

So, in the end, something like Napster is a benefit to artists and to the people who want to enjoy what they have produced. If the noisy, greedy pigs of the distribution net would just shut up a moment and look at the benefit they, too, could be reaping, we'd all be a lot happier. It's not possible to stop all forms of piracy absolutely. However, like cheating on an exam, when it's more work than it's worth, most people will play by the rules. When it is easy to pay an artist, directly, for his work, the price will drop to the end user while the percentage that goes to the artist rises. Who wouldn't like that? (Well, the middlemen, of course, but they will never really disappear....)

So I have to say: Shut up, get off your butts and let's fix the real problem, instead of attacking a service which ends up benefitting you more than it hurts you.

I know my attitude on this may be my undoing, but, oh, well.... I've never been good at keeping my mouth shut, have I?

## Boing, Boeing..., Splat

\

Well, it appears that the state of Washington has decided to screw itself into the ground. Or at least the western half of the state.

A few months ago I thought our governor's decision to sell power to California at a protected rate and raise the rates to Washingtonians was a bit ill-advised, especially in light of the fact that we've had an unusually dry winter and most of the state's electricity is generated at hydroelectric stations. So, we're looking at a potential power shortage here in the summer, as well as a water shortage in what is usually one of the wettest areas of the US. If electricity is so scarce on the southern half of the western grid, then why in Hell is Las Vegas lit up bright as ever 24/7? Let the leeches suck from someone else, damn it. We cannot afford this and it's the ordinary folks who get it in the backside without benefit of lubrication, so far as I can see. (And I really want to know who signed this stinking contract which apparently swapped our weather and seismic patterns as well. Damn it all, I moved *out* of California.)

And now the largest employer in the western half of the state is leaving. Yep: Boeing Aircraft has finally had enough of being taxed and regulated into the red. So, with various excuses in tow, they are heading out and taking the economic stability of the local area with them. This is not exactly unexpected by some, but it is horribly bad timing, since the second largest area employer, Microsoft, is currently experiencing a bit of a rough patch, as well.

At the moment, we are still feeling the pinch from this little shake up we had last month. I mean the literal shaking of the earth which damaged roads and buildings as well as the busiest airport in the state. (Got to pay for that somehow, and I doubt tourism from our new cruise ship dock is going to do it.)

And the City of Seattle has also taken their lash at Boeing as the local cash cow to be milked. They have offered nothing in return while encouraging the waste of resources our governor has proposed. We happily head for an economic cliff like a giant lemming under the leadership of a mayor so closely tied to commercial development that he hasn't had time to do anything but approve so-called civic improvements which the people are paying for but get no direct benefit from, like a football stadium we pay to build, while the owner of the team gets to keep the profits. Now, where is the supposed "partnership" in that which we were told we were entering into? Sounds like we are silent partners--because we've been bound to a chair and gagged. (I'd like to gag over this, but I hate football, anyhow, so I always gag on that. Who can tell the difference?)

Our mayor, Paul Schell, has been busy putting his foot in it and has the gall to think we're going to re-elect him. First we have a little riot back a little over a year ago from which there is still fallout and the lawsuits that go with same. Then, the night before our earthquake, we have another riot, once again poorly handled by the mayor and new chief of police (who replaced the one who mishandled the WTO debacle.) Meantime, the City has decided to spend tax money on new football and baseball stadiums while public transit and road maintenance goes to hell in a handbasket and the monorail the people voted for is sabotaged while the light rail we voted against goes forward at ridiculous cost and not a single foot of track or tunnel.

So, here we sit, watching our two most influential politicians drive the state to the poor house. Without Boeing and with Microsoft's necessary downsizing and consolidation (let's face it, no company whose product is, essentially, intellectual, can expand at that rate forever) we are facing a rough ride. The City of Seattle, the largest and most important city in the state (sorry, Olympia, Spokane and Tacoma, but it's true) is about to see big unemployment in it's two formerly-largest fields: aerospace and software development. These workers are already panicking and so are a lot of others.

Just in the few days since Boeing announced its plans to move corporate offices out of state and much of its design and manufacturing, people are talking about moving out of state, putting their homes up for sale or not buying new ones. I already hear people saying they won't buy things because they fear they won't have a job in six months. They may be overreacting, but the fear is still there. Perception is the killer.

And it is starting to show. We went out to look at boats today and when we came home I described the odyssey to some of our neighbors on the dock.

"We just haven't seen a boat in our price range that I really feel comfortable with and my husband says we can't expect perfection at this price. All I want is something I like."

"Oh, just wait: you'll find it. Think of all the folks who are about to decide they can't afford a boat anymore because they think they may lose their jobs in the next two years. Hell, it's happening already."

"Ahhh... yes, they'll bail out of the so-called luxuries like rats off a sinking ship," I said.

"Yeah, the good ship Seattle, captained by Paul Hell."

"And the first name is spelled P-A-L-L."

They laughed and walked off.

And it started to rain. The first good, hard rain since January.

Ahhh.. rain ya bastard. Rain like merry Hell. Might as well.

posted: March 28, 2001

\

Things have taken a confusing and strange turn for me, lately. So, I'm going to be taking a little time away from here, I think. Well, at the very least. Who knows, though? Could change my so-called mind in any of a dozen ways in an instant.

Life is a really strange place, no matter where you live it: in the "real" world or in a virtual one. And even more so when you live your life in your head, for the most part.

My life goes on in my mind and, often, this "real" world of math and physics and universal laws seems to be only a place to survive, not a place to *live*. That's upside down and backwards, really, but that's the way my head works.

So, I'm going to go sit in my mental attic for a while and try to sort out the junk. Just think of me in my tatty blue jeans, eating cheese sandwiches and making piles of what to keep and what to throw away. I'll come downstairs, occasionally, to pick up the mail and answer the phone, so, if you want to talk to me, drop me e-mail.

I'm going to go, now.

Be seeing you.
