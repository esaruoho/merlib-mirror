## The Piano

\

I have come to the point of having to sell my piano. This makes me quite unhappy. Although I have not played it in some time and it has not been in my own home (since I live on a boat) in five years, I am still very attached to it.

This is a rather strange piano by modern standards: full upright grand, heavily carved and sporting a now-deteriorated finish which was originally a hand-painted burl pattern. It's striking, to say the least. It was built in 1908 in Boston by the Hallet, Davis piano company. My great-grandfather ordered it as a wedding gift for my great-grandmother.

We're not sure why, but instead of being put on a train and carted across the country, it was put on a steamship and sailed around the tip of South America, through the Straits of Magellan and up the West Coast to Portland, Oregon, arriving nearly a year after the order was taken.

In Portland, the piano was unloaded and put onto a wagon which hauled it to Salem, where my great-grandparents lived on a small farm. The family story is that grandpa, being a country boy, had bought the piano for grandma, a city girl, to assure her that she wouldn't be leaving everything sophisticated and decent behind by marrying him and moving out the the sticks.

The piano hadn't been with them long before it became plain that farming just wasn't Great-Grandmother's thing. Apparently it wasn't such a terrific thing for Great-Grandfather, either, as they sold the farm and moved to Los Angeles. I'm not actually sure what Great-Grandfather did while they lived in LA, but, eventually, he joined the Army and Great-Grandmother decided to become a nurse, just in time for World War I. My grandmother has a rather silly picture of my great-grandmother and her friend Irene outside the Los Angeles Area Nurses' Training Facility in their white dresses and huge picture hats on graduation-day (I'm not sure of the date but it looks like about 1915), mugging for the camera and standing in the fountain.

I suspect that my great-grandmother was quite a cut-up.

All through my childhood, one thing you could depend on was Sunday dinner once a month at Great-Grandmother Olson's house, which she shared with her nursing-chum Irene (Great-Grandfather having died before I was born). We called her "Aunt Irene" though she was, in fact, no relation at all. Aunt Irene both intimidated and thrilled me. She was incredibly kind and easy-going with a rather strange child like me, but she was six-foot-tall in her stocking-feet and had a bun the size of Gibraltar perpetually perched on the top of her head. I did once catch her with her hair down on the one and only occasion I was allowed the incredible privilege of sleeping overnight at Great-Grandmother's house. Irene's bun, I discovered, unwound into a gray-white curtain which reached her knees.

My great-grandmother was remarkably tolerant of the disruptive and destructive nature of children and she let me play with her china dolls and, very carefully, with the imposing piano, which I loved. I didn't merely want to make noise on it; I wanted to look inside it and see how it worked. When it was finally living in my house--not my parent's house or my mother's house or some storage unit or garage as it did, variously--I would sometimes remove the face panels on the top and the bottom and look at the mechanism for the pedals and the hammers. I loved the way the hammers moved through their complicated linkages of leather straps and bent wire and wood to make the felt-covered mallets hit the strings. I enjoyed watching the damper pedal move the felt dampers away from the strings and back again and locking the practice pedal in place so I could play nearly silently, with only the soft "pah, pah" of the muffled strings.

When my great-grandmother was 97, she became extremely ill, suffered a stroke and was committed to a nursing home. My mother and I would go to visit her, occasionally, but these visits made me so sad and horrified (what was this horrible thing which was happening to my dear great-grandmother?) that I finally requested not to go. I hope she didn't realize that I had stopped coming, but I fear she did.

At 98, she died. I was still in Middle School. It didn't seem odd to me that she had left the piano to me and my sister, though she had designated us "the girls" apparently, and this caused no end of confusion, later, as my mother determined that she was one of "the girls" in question, and eventually took the piano away from my house. This always confused and upset me as I was the only person since Great-Grandmother, herself, who played piano at all. My mother hates and fears playing due to a demonic piano teacher who used to smack her across the knuckles with a ruler for every wrong note and my sister never cared for the instrument, but preferred the guitar. It was my piano if usage counted for anything. But the piano went to my mom's house and I got to visit it, occasionally until I moved in with her for 5 years at the end of High School. My mother kept the piano when I went to college and took it with her from house to house after that.

Many years later, "my piano" finally came back to me. It was in poor shape, externally, with a finish badly cracked and flaking, one broken bass hammer and a broken pedal linkage. But it still sounded magnificent, even slightly out of tune, with its clean treble and resonant, rich bass. I loved it and I was pleased to have it.

When we moved onto a boat, there was no question in my mind that Great-Grandmother's piano would be kept, somehow. It moved into the house of friends and became an admired piece of furniture which no one played, upon which photos and plants were displayed for visitors to goggle at. It was placed near an unused door and ignored, except for the few times I was able to go and visit it.

And there it was for five years. Now, my friends are selling the house, the new tenant takes possession in a month, and there is no place left to store the piano. I have no money for a proper storage unit and the boat really won't hold it. No one else I know has room.

But, remembering my mother's proprietary attitude about it, I called her, partially in hopes that she might take the piano in. But, alas, after much discussion between her and my grandmother, it was decided that they had no room, either. They threw the ball back into my court.

"Well," my mom told me, "I guess it really is *your* piano, anyway. So, I guess it's all right for you to do whatever you want with it."

Finally, almost 25 years after her death, Great-Grandmother's piano is out of family probate. It's finally settled and transferred only to be imminently homeless after all these years in Limbo.

Now, when it is, at last, admittedly, mine, I have to give it up, because there is no room for it and no money to care for it. I hate giving up Great-Grandmother's piano. It's like she's finally, really gone and Great-Grandfather's gift is being tossed out like an old toaster.

But, I would rather sell it and believe that my great-grandmother's beautiful piano is being played and enjoyed by someone, somewhere, than leave it to moulder away into wood rot and mouse-droppings in a garage.

Anybody have a home for a homeless piano?

## Bump in the Night

\

Conking yourself on the skull is not what it's made out to be in the movies. It really doesn't hurt much at the time, but it's a major pain later. Y'see, this past Saturday night--well, OK, it was early Sunday morning, actually--I had an intimate little tête à tête with a stretch of tarmac. I don't usually snuggle up to bits of parking lot, but there were *Circumstances*, you understand: I was sick as the much-maligned and proverbial dog (and I cannot help but wonder if this was the same dog which took to hanging out in mangers, which would make it proverbial, but not particularly maligned--unless, of course, it's all just an ugly rumor--and it might explain a lot about the viciousness of food...).

Anyhow. There was this lovely party....

Our immediate marina-neighbors got married at eleven o'clock that morning and the reception started in the parking lot at Noon. It was quite a shindig. It rolled on continuously until about 2130 (9:30 p.m. to you normal folk) when it finally petered out in a drunken shuffle as the youngest of the party-crowd wandered off to find more beer at an Irish pub nearby (the keg having given up its last in a wheezy, carbonated gasp and sputter about thirty minutes earlier) and the normal humans staggered off to individual boats and homes. The bride and groom had eeled off just before to catch a little bed-time before bedtime. I, being the compulsively helpful person I am, stayed to clean up the tremendous mess along with another couple. I think we did this because we were the only people now sober enough to manage to pick up something other than a glass. The last bag of trash was stuffed into the dumpster at 2200, missing keys found and returned to lost tug-boat captains, chairs stacked and wine cases stashed under lock and key, and I was snuggled down in my boat with a nice cup of tea by 2230.

About 0230, the feast had its revenge. I suspect the pork roast. Or it might have been the pasta salad. Maybe the whipped-cream cake.... But, regardless, as I made my way from the boat to the restroom (a distance equivalent to about one city block) ambulation and I parted company and that happy state which is known as "being awake" took a flyer, abandoning me completely to the tender mercies of thin air and wet asphalt.

Some space of time later, I began to think that the breeze felt rather nice, considering how hot and sweaty I felt, otherwise, and that it was really not at all nice of those lads to have wandered off and left me, but at least they hadn't put any of those hot, heavy covers over me. And I was thinking that the bed was remarkably firm and not really too uncomfortable, in spite of the grit, and maybe I'd just open my eyes, now, and see if it was still dark out and decide if I wanted to get up or not. And I opened my eye, the lower one being somewhat cranky about the idea, and thought "I don't remember putting that thing there. What is it? Oh, it's a door. It's sideways, though. How is anyone supposed to go through that?"

Which is about when Reality stepped back in, somewhat tardily and with apologies, and informed me that I was lying on the ground about five yards outside the restroom door. I did consider just staying where I was, except that certain things seemed rather insistent on being attended to, so I dragged my sorry self up to the restroom.

I think I scrubbed the grit out of the contusion on my forehead and the scrapes on my hands before I started back out to the boat, but I don't remember it. I didn't particularly notice the state of my face until the next day. I do recall my husband coming along and helping me around and flashing a pocket light in my eyes to see if I had a concussion.

If he had asked me, I'd have been able to tell him I was perfectly all right in that regard, since I remember what a concussion feels like. This isn't the first time I've smacked myself in the skull, y'know. The last time I managed such a feat was slipping in the bath and smashing the other side of my forehead against the tub spigot. That, strangely enough, didn't knock the "awake" out of me, but it did knock the sense straight down the drain. I just sat under the shower until the water got cold enough to be dangerously uncomfortable, at which point some clever survival instinct reminded me to turn it off. I got a really large lump on my head, which left a small, permanent bump of bone under the skin, and a technicolor bruise, blurry vision, a constant headache and a general feeling of nausea which lasted about a week. This time I just had a really nasty bump and a scrape or two. Nausea was entirely the fault of the rampaging botulinus. (Hah! See if I ever trust a pork roast again!)

At the moment, I have a bruise around my eye which is green, gold and magenta and it looks as if someone popped me one. It actually looks worse than it did at the time and people comment on it. Alas, I suspect they ascribe it to being loopy-drunk and simply falling over. Nothing so much fun.

Really, if you must knock yourself silly, I recommend doing it someplace more salutary than a parking lot and for reasons better than trying to make the early-morning sermon at the Porcelain Temple.
