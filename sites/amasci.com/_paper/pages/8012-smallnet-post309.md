The foamboard warpage mentioned by JW is indeed a common occurrence. I usually buy mine at a craft store that has it stacked flat 20-30 pieces high. If you pick one out of the center it usually is flatter and more resistant to warping. The foamboard that is colored on one side is particularly bad about warping.

With careful planning you can use the bend to your advantage, and get either a slight amount of dihedral for a wing or some beneficial wing warp. Usually though, the air here in Colorado is dry enough that I don't get more than 1/8"-1/4" of warp from end to end on a sheet of foamboard. Some sources that I like:

I have really enjoyed building planes out of this stuff. It goes together quickly and always gets attention at the field when one of these "flat" planes fly.

Sam Brauer \< 

 \> wrote:

Jim Braunam reported having trouble with a House of Balsa AT-6 after having powered the airplane with a Saito 30. About 10 years ago, I was flying this airplane with an HB .25, so I have some experience with it.

However, I never had any trouble with the airplane spinning in the way Jim's described. I suspect what's going on is that the motor is too heavy and that once the mass of the motor has been set in motion by a spin, that the controls do not have sufficient authority to overcome this inertia.

I have a couple of suggestions to anyone contemplating this airplane. First off, a Sportster 20 should be more than enough power. The HB .25 (non-schnuerle) had way too much power for the airplane, which then exhibited some skittish ground handling. Furthermore, the upper half of the power range only made more noise. Given the frontal area of the airplane, it didn't fly any faster, it just made more racket.

I've rebuilt mine, and one of these days I may drop a .15 in it. The airplane actually flew quite well at low power settings, although I though it was a little heavy. I would go for a smaller power plant and build it light.

The plans also called for a lot of washout in the wings. DON'T forget this washout! I snapped mine in on takeoff several times because I didn't have enough. I suspect a lack of washout may cause the airplane to behave like it's tail heavy.

I wanted to find out if it's only me, or do other people think that engines have been getting heavier over the years? A couple of months back an old flying buddy and I hauled out his Butterfly, since his son is now old enough to take an interest. The airplane originally had an old OS 20 (the engine is now 25 plus years old; pre-FP days) that I was never very fond of, so I gave him a K&B Sportster 20.

One of the problems with this Butterfly is that it always had some wing flutter since he used a soft iron-on fabric covering on it. In a dive if you weren't careful, you'd hear the wings really begin shaking -- and that was 10 years ago.

Anyhow, the problem didn't improve with age. Furthermore, he had to move the battery pack back a fair amount to get the plane to balance, another giveaway that this new engine is heavier. The Sportster is certainly easier to handle than the OS, more powerful, but unfortunately heavier. The wonderful floating glide of the Butter- fly is gone due to the increased wing loading. I think he may pull the OS out of hiding and try repairing it because it was a pretty good match with this airplane.

If anybody is contemplating a Butterfly, use something smaller and lighter than the K&B. By the way, this airplane is one of the best trainers I know of -- not that hard to build, and it's easy enough to control so that you can teach someone to fly in an afternoon.

Sam Brauer Norwalk, Connecticut

George Hostler \< 

 \> asserts:

I've got to hand it to modellers, their willingness to use their noggins to come up with a useful solution by experimentation. They are true engineers. I'd never think of using Son-of-a-Gun or Armor-All, but it makes sense. If it can preserve and provide that slipperiness to the interior of cars and preserve the rubber on tires, it ought to work on rubber motors. I remember the days of mixing glycerin and green soap.

BTW, the best Guillow flying models were their "el cheapo" series that included the DeHavilland Chipmunk, T-28D Trojan, Cessna Bird Dog, Hawker Tempest, and a few others. I've built several of the T-28D's and one Chipmunk. Reason, their airframes were built light. With around a 16" - 17" span, I think they are around the 100 square inch wing area realm.

The favorites in the Comet series, although they didn't fly as well, were about the same size in 16" or so span except for the biplanes.

Reason, they were so easy to build, but appealed as stick models. That series included the Curtiss Robin, Ryan ST, Bellanca Jr., Fokker D-VII, Spad. Secret was reducing weight by careful sanding of the 5/8" x 1/8" wing trailing edge to nearly a knife edge, and well rounding of the leading edge, and covering with Japanese tissue and lightly spraying clear dope over it. Then replace the small 3-1/2" Comet propeller with a cut-down Japanese or Guillow prop.

I like the Comet simplified looks of the Bellanca Jr., have built several. One of these days, I plan to scale it up 3X, giving it a wingspan of 48", just right for an .074 to .10 motor.

George Hostler, Gallup, New Mexico, USA

...George's comments remind me of a little-known fact: rubber power performance suffers very little from high-altitude effects. George flies at something like 7500 foot altitude, and has mentioned needing bigger power plants to fly his engine-powered craft than we sea-level flyers require. But the rubber models do just as well at altitude on the same size props and rubber motors as they do down low. \[JW\]

Ed Stevens at Norvel wrote: "I'm not exactly sure why those with rubber bladder tanks may not have plug contamination problems."

I have a feeling that the difference may be as simple as the chemical composition of the rubber compounds in the two devices. The Latex used in balloons is of a quite high quality. (I make balloon animals for a part of my living, and use HIGH quality Qualitex balloons.) It's similar to the formulas used in Condoms and Surgical Gloves.

The fueling bulbs are a different matter! The ones I have seen are of a compound that appears to have been made in about 1930 and stored in brine. I wouldn't use a rubber bulb for ANYTHING!

If you want a really great small capacity fueler, check the local pharmacy. I have very often picked up a syringe and large gage needle. You can get 60 cc or larger sometimes. Also, a veterinarian supply can provide you with syringes that are sturdy and reusable and of awesome proportions! These are the needles we had nightmares about as small children! But I digress!

If you use a Dremel tool and cutoff wheel to take the point off the needle, you can have a pretty good small capacity fueler. Very precise too, and the price is usually unbelievably cheap. As to objections from the pharmacist; well, I tell them what I am going to do with the syringe and have never had a problem. This may be a different story in other places, but so far no difficulty. Mark

<div class="Joe">

...Maybe so, Gene. However, I'm reminded of the off-water flying demonstrations at the fabulous hobby shows held at the Shrine Auditorium in Los Angeles from 1949 until sometime in the 1960's.

Inside, in the center of the main floor, was a 42-foot-square enclosure, fenced off with something akin to "chicken wire". Demon- strations of control-line flying took place several times a day there, and the shows lasted about 9 days (a week plus a weekend).

The famous F.A.S.T. club flew their amazing scale control-line models of Schneider trophy racing seaplanes. These were powered by K&B Greenhead .09's, modified with clapper-type throttles taken from Jim Walker "Firecracker".065's. The line length was about 16 feet, and the types included Supermarine S-5's and S-6's, Sopwith "Tabloid", Macchi-Castoldis, Curtiss biplane racers, and even a Deperduissin.

These airplanes took off from, and landed on, a semi-circular tank filled with water about 5 inches deep. The engines were glow, of course, and as far as I could tell (and I attended MANY of these demonstrations, acting as a helper sometimes too) there were no adverse effects from the water.

And these models inhaled a LOT of water sometimes too! The planes were flown three or four at a time, taking off in rapid succession. The prop blast from one model on the water being held ready for takeoff would spray a spume backwards for two feet or more -- right into the nose of the next model being readied for flight.

No starters were used at any time; yet I don't remember any special balkiness. Yes, sometimes it took a little more trouble than usual to get one of these K&B .09's perking. But I attributed that to the rather Rube-Goldbergish throttling system, which used a small rubber diaphragm in the nose to move the throttle clapper -- the actuating pressure being supplied by a small rubber bulb in the pilot's left hand, through a long, thin vinyl plastic tube...! It worked -- sort of -- but that setup surely wouldn't satisfy any model flyer today!

But there's more to this "water ingestion" story. A couple of years after the seaplane racers were first flown at the Shrine Hobby Show, the F.A.S.T. club added another crowd-pleasing feature. They built a simulated aircraft carrier, and flew profile Grumman Wildcats off the deck. They made arrested landings, too -- but the real hit of the "carrier show" was when a profile Zero was hand-launched into the flying circle, and "combat" took place.

After a dozen or more wildly-maneuvering circles, the Zero would get "hit"; wobble drunkenly in the air -- then splash full throttle into the seaplane takeoff tank!

This show was repeated probably eight or more times per day. That means that the Zero crashed into solid water with its engine running more than 50 times a week. Yet all that was necessary to put the model back into flying status was to shake the water out of its nose and squirt glow fuel into the engine to drive out its inhaled water.

I held that Zero several times while its engine was started -- and can't remember ANY difficulties. (The Zero's engine had no speed control; so far as I remember, it was just a stock Firecracker .065 without its throttle -- the same kind of engine supplied with the "Firebaby" R-T-F control-line airplane.)

All this experience indicates to me that there's no particular problem flying seaplanes with glow engines. \[JW\]
