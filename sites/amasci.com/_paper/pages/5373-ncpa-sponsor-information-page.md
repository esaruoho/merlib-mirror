<h3 id="what-makes-paintball-a-great-sponsorship-opportunity-for-out-of-industry-sponsors-over-other-extreme-sports-and-sports-in-general">What makes paintball a great sponsorship opportunity for out-of-industry sponsors over other extreme sports and sports in general?</h3>
<p>Paintball has several advantages for sponsorship over sponsoring other extreme sports and sports in general.</p>
<ul>
<li>Paintball is a team sport.</li>
<li>It is easy and fun to play paintball the first time.</li>
<li>Paintball has a broad base of appeal.</li>
<li>BUT paintball especially appeals to 14-25 year old males.</li>
<li>Paintball is played everywhere.</li>
<li>Paintball is a non-contact sport.</li>
<li>Paintball is more exciting than golf.</li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li>Paintball is a team sport. Snowboarding, skateboarding, free-style bike riding, etc, are all individual sports. The audience likes to see more than just one person performing stunts; they like to see what happens when teams of people interact. Extreme sports like dirt bike racing move in this direction, but paintball is the only team sport with team vs. team interaction, which parallels the vast majority of professional sports the public gives their attention to today.</li>
<li>It is easy and fun to play paintball for the first time. Baseball, soccer, volleyball and to a lesser extent, football and basketball are popular because it is easy for anyone to participate in the game with minimal preparation. Anyone can swing a bat and throw or kick a ball. Extreme sports suffer from a high barrier to entry - It takes virtually everyone three days of a sore posterior to learn to snowboard even mildly proficiently, the amount of effort required to be even moderately consistent with even the easiest tricks in skateboarding and free-style biking is significant. Paintball much more closely parallels backyard sports: Once a player gets to a field, the only thing separating them from proficiently playing the game is a 5-minute safety briefing. In addition, anyplace a player may play paintball can, at a minimal cost, provide the player with all of the equipment necessary for them to do so. There is no lengthy sizing process, no need to invest hundreds of dollars in the appropriate equipment, one must merely show up, rent, and go. The benefit is that a spectator watching paintball can easily also participate in the sport. Spectators have a much closer association with sports that they play themselves, and paintball provides players with a sport that they will like immediately and are therefore much more likely to participate in again.</li>
<li>Paintball has a broad base of appeal. One of paintball's strongest advantages is that success in the game is virtually independent of physical stature. In virtually every athletic competition, there is a strong advantage associated with size, making it difficult for people of differing sizes and ages to compete on the same level. By removing the effect of physical strength on the game, paintball allows younger brothers to play with older brothers and fathers to play with daughters on an equal competitive level. No extreme sport, and very few other sports in general, offer this level of family interaction. Paintball provides an athletic sponsorship activity with a FAMILY based appeal, as well as a sport where anyone, regardless of size, has an equal opportunity to excel. Paintball is a sport available to many people who are less likely to excel at more traditional sports.</li>
<li>Paintball especially appeals to 14-25 year old males. The most basic lesson in demographics presents 14-25 year old males as an excellent target demographic: Disposable incomes and still in a stage to develop product loyalty. What do 14-25 year old males find especially appealing? Paintball.</li>
<li>Paintball is played everywhere. Paintball can be played year-round. It can be played in the woods, on a speedball field, or a portable inflated field - indoors or outdoors. This makes a paintball field a fairly easy thing to find, you don't need snow or an elaborate race course or skate park setup to play; in a pinch, a large backyard will do the trick. This makes it easy for viewers to be and get involved personally with the sport.</li>
<li>Paintball is a no-contact sport. While it may be common for paintball players to receive small welts, paintball is 1000 times safer than contact sports such as soccer and football. There is no tackling, no kicking, no physical contact between players that causes the vast majority of injuries in other team sports. There is also no trick jumping, 720's, rapid velocity movement, and other extreme physical endeavors that cause the vast majority of injury in most extreme sports.</li>
<li>Paintball is more exciting than golf. Millions of people watch golf. Its boring to watch. The only reason people WATCH golf is because people PLAY golf. Paintball is cheaper to participate in, exponentially easier to learn, and immediately more gratifying to play. The only difference is golf has been around longer. Getting into paintball early gives sponsors the ability to reap the huge benefits down the road as paintball continues to grow at the phenomenal rate it has been.</li>
</ul>

<h3 id="what-makes-college-paintball-a-better-sponsorship-opportunity-than-professional-or-amateur-paintball">What makes college paintball a better sponsorship opportunity than professional or amateur paintball?</h3>
<ul>
<li>College paintball has the only definitive national title in paintball.</li>
<li>College paintball has team names familiar to the general public.</li>
<li>College teams get their players by creating large recreational clubs and bringing new players into the game.</li>
<li>College players are 17-22 year old college students.</li>
<li>College paintball teams have college girls.</li>
<li>College teams have a much more sportsmanly on-field attitude than professional and amateur teams.</li>
<li>College teams provide an opportunity for younger and inexperienced players to get into tournament paintball.</li>
<li>College tournaments are much less expensive for the participants.</li>
<li>College tournaments offer unannounced donated prize packages.</li>
<li>The NCPA is not out to make any money and pays no salaries.</li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li>College paintball provides something that has to date been missing from paintball as a sport: A defined, all encompassing national league with a defined membership and a singular national championship. Football has the NFL and the Superbowl, baseball has the Major League and the World Series, basketball has the NBA and the NBA Finals, and each has their NCAA sanctioned college league with their own separate championships. Paintball has a hodge-podge of professional teams and tournaments arranged in various manners with little to no formal relationships between the teams and the leagues. Paintball teams don't belong to any league, they choose tournament by tournament where they want to play. This makes it very hard to establish any sort of consistent recognition in the sport to sport aficionados themselves, much less the general public outside the sport. College paintball may not solve the professional problem, but does bring the sport the equivalent of an NCAA sponsored national league. The importance of this from a sponsorship perspective is significant: All college teams are in the league, and there are no teams in the league that are not college teams. This means that you will see the same teams playing each other in the same regionals year after year trying to advance to the national finals, and at the end of the year you have one definitive national champion. Sponsors and fans alike know in advance which teams they can expect to see at a tournament, know which teams are the "powerhouses", and know which teams have rivalries.</li>
<li>College paintball teams have familiar names. Sponsorship of paintball can be daunting to out-of-industry sponsors confronted with team names like "Adrenaline", "Avalanche", and "Ground Zero". Who are these guys? How is the public going to relate to these teams? Professional paintball teams barely even have home cities, let alone a presumed fan base. College paintball has the advantage of being able to trade in on an existing college fan base. Members of the public find it much easier to associate with names like "Michigan State Spartans" and "University of Illinois Illini" since they are already familiar with those names. Thus it is much easier for someone watching a paintball event where familiar college teams are competing to form an emotional involvement in that competition. If a member of the public turns on the TV and sees Avalanche and Ground Zero squaring off, are they likely to care? Probably not. The channel will be changed. But what if they see the Michigan State Spartans playing the Ohio State Buckeyes? There are many, many more people who know those names, from the general public to college area residents to graduates of those colleges themselves, who have an instant means of involving themselves in the competition. It isn't necessary for the viewer to already be a paintball fan to feel interested in the game, as it would be if professional paintball teams were competing, the viewer can utilize their existing associations with the universities represented instead. Sponsoring college paintball allows sponsors to associate themselves with recognizable university team names instead of obscure paintball team names.</li>
<li>Growth. This is especially important for paintball industry sponsors. The vast majority of college paintball players are recruited through college recreational clubs. Turning this around, college teams have a vested interest in maintaining large recreational paintball programs to provide them with new tournament players to replace graduates. This is a huge difference over professional teams - in addition to the players that college teams get involved in tournaments directly, there are thousands of players who play paintball for the first time each year with college recreational clubs. By supporting college paintball, you're not only encouraging the formation of more college competitive teams, but indirectly encouraging the formation of college recreational clubs that provide the teams with players they can turn into tournament players. Those players that play with recreational college clubs will most likely have never played paintball otherwise, will eventually be college graduates and have correspondingly higher disposable incomes, are young and have a lifetime of paintball in their future, and will eventually have children they will turn around and get involved in the sport. Unlike professional and amateur teams, who have primarily self serving interests, college teams are a very significant impetus for growth of the industry as a whole as a result of their continued efforts to bring new players both into the game in general as well as help them step up their game to the tournament level.</li>
<li>Demographics again. Everyone chases the 14-25 year old male demographic. Young, impressionable, disposable incomes, advertisers like these guys. Who especially likes paintball? 14-25 year old males. Who PLAYS college paintball? 17-22 year olds. Who plays professional paintball? Well, lets just say the age range doesn't cut off at 22. Not only are college paintball players closer in age to the targeted viewer, they are also much more likely to be attractive, educated and articulate. There aren't any college paintball scholarships (yet), so players who play on college teams are also people who were able to get into college. This is a very large improvement over the "anyone who can use the marker and pay the entry fee" qualification of amateur and professional teams.</li>
<li>Girls. We realize it's a superficial world, so we might as well trade in on it: College paintball teams have girls. College girls. Go back to the demographics thing; we shouldn't have to explain this one.</li>
<li>Attitude. 80% of college paintball team members first played tournament paintball for their college team, most of them attracted from college recball clubs and college trained from the start. The result is the chances that you see a college player do such unsportsmanlike things as yell at the refs, throw his equipment, scream at other players, and display other such typically professional unsportsmanlike behavior is virtually nonexistent. Since the college league is well defined, the same players see each other on the same teams over and over again, and players know that their actions well permanently affect their team's reputation. The likelihood you see a college player treat the players on other teams as friends, check themselves, and call themselves out when hit is correspondingly much larger. The short version is that college paintball players never had professional paintball players as examples to learn from. They learn to play paintball playing with their friends from school, and that carries over to the tournaments as well. College team captains are also very aware of the benefits of this situation and make the effort to catch the rare problems early and make sure the team understands what sort of behavior is appropriate. Professional players have the luxury of a bad temperament: If they play like assholes and get kicked off the team, they just go find another professional team where their skills are more important than their attitudes (or, regrettably, where poor sportsmanship is even encouraged.) In college paintball, such a luxury doesn't exist. In addition, problematic players regulate themselves out of college paintball; they simply don't find the college environment a hospitable environment to play in. Unsportsmanlike behavior is frowned upon much more than winning is rewarded; a problematic player will simply find it easier to find a professional team to play for than to try and fit in the college environment and will choose to pursue professional/amateur paintball over college paintball.</li>
<li>Opportunity. College paintball gives young and inexperienced players an expectation of being able to play tournament paintball at some point. Getting on an amateur or professional team is difficult at best, creating one of your own even harder. When young players watch college paintball, they see an easy opportunity for them to become competitively involved with the sport - all they need to do is go find the paintball club at the college they are or will be going to. They don't need to be experienced players to get on the team. College teams coach the recreational player into a tournament player, which is rare amongst established professional and amateur teams. Players who feel they have this opportunity available to them are much more likely to stay involved in paintball, bringing more money into the sport as a whole.</li>
<li>Price. College tournaments don't buy prizes and don't try to make any money, which keeps event costs and thus entry fees to a minimum. The added advantage to this scenario also translates over to attitude: Teams aren't paying hundreds of dollars to enter a tournament in the hopes of winning thousands of dollars in prizes. College teams never have to invest more than the cost to play, thus aside from travel expenditures, a college tournament isn't significantly more expensive than any other Saturday or Sunday at the local paintball field. The motivations for playing move from trying to win prizes and pay off the entry fee to playing paintball. Our philosophy is to have everyone pay a little for trophies to fight over than everyone pay a hundreds of dollars to get a huge prize package to fight over in an attempt to break even. Professional teams to win large prize packages, college teams play for fun and their egos.</li>
<li>Prizes. In addition to not buying any prizes, we also don't announce the prize package in advance. College teams come to the tournament not expecting to win anything, so if a team doesn't manage to place and doesn't win anything, they've got nothing to be upset about. Put another way, no team ever loses anything. Every team pays a little money to play, and they all get to play. This serves to reinforce the positive on and off field attitude.</li>
<li>Profits. We don't make any. We'll give you as much return on your sponsorship investment as we can, we'll work with you to make a sponsorship package that fits the specific needs of your company. All we ask in return is your help in making the cost of playing in the tournaments for the players as low as possible. We're not here trying to get your sponsorship so we can make more money. We want our players to play cheaper and you as the sponsor to get as much out of helping us as possible. This is a large departure from the amateur and professional event organizer who seeks sponsorship chiefly as a means to maximizing the profit margin.</li>
</ul>

<h3 id="what-does-the-ncpa-offer-to-sponsors">What does the NCPA offer to sponsors?</h3>
<ul>
<li>All sponsors for any event are listed permanently on that event's web page and are included in any distribution or display of the results of that event afterwards. People may not care about the results of Skyball 3 years ago (and especially not last year ;)), but people do check to see who won the previous years' regional and national college tournaments.</li>
<li>All sponsors are included in any publicity associated with the event, including magazine publications, web page ads, and newsgroup postings.</li>
<li>All sponsors are eligible to set up booth at the event as well as sell their product at the event.</li>
<li>Sponsors contributing to reduced paint, entry fee and airfare for the participating teams (subject to a minimum level of contribution) receive prime banner placement at both regional and national tournaments, including around the field, on bunkers, and as the backdrop for the photos of the top four teams.</li>
<li>Naming rights. A sponsor willing to cover a significant amount of airfare or paint costs will be given the right to name the tournament the "Corporation X National Collegiate Paintball Association Championships", and the event will always be referred to and publicized as such. (And we promise its cheaper than the Fiesta Bowl or the United Center.)</li>
<li>Anything else that you want that we can give you. Talk to us, we'll work something out.</li>
</ul>

<h3 id="what-sort-of-sponsorship-needs-does-the-ncpa-have">What sort of sponsorship needs does the NCPA have?</h3>
<p>The NCPA is all about making the cost to participate for teams as low as possible. The lower the cost, the more participation, the more attention, and the greater reward for the sponsors. The more you give, the more you receive. In rough order of priority, the NCPA needs:</p>
<ul>
<li>Help in reducing travel costs for attending teams. We would primarily like to be able to offer teams winning regionals a $100 per person credit towards airfare, with additional credits to other teams who attended regional events to make it easier to get them to nationals.</li>
<li>Help in reducing paint costs for the tournament. We would primarily like to give each team winning a regional event 5-8 free cases of paint at nationals, and additionally reduce the per-case cost of paint for all teams at both regional and national events.</li>
<li>Help in reducing the entry fees for the tournament. This mainly amounts to the $1400 - $2000 cost of paying qualified referees.</li>
<li>Donation of prizes. While the NCPA has never prioritized giving away prizes, we're more than happy to offer sponsors the ability to advertise themselves by donating prizes to the prize package for regional and national events. If you would like to donate prizes, contact the appropriate representative for the tournament you're interested in on the contacts page.</li>
</ul></td>
