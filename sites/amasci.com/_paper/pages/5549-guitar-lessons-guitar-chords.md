<td width="100%"><h1 id="guitar-chords">Guitar Chords</h1>
<p>Knowing how to play a chord is not just important for beginners. Even skilled players should brush up on their chord theory every so often. This page has chord guides and lessons on chords.</p>

</tr>
<tr>
<td data-valign="top"><p><strong><a href="open.html"><img src="images/aopen.gif" data-border="0" width="49" height="70" alt="Open Chords" /></a></strong></p></td>

<td><p><a href="open.html"><strong>Open Chord Chart</strong></a><br />
These are the most important open chord forms for all guitar players. There are five different forms (C, A, G, E, D) and three different types (major, seventh, minor.)</p></td>
</tr>
<tr>

<td><p><a href="barre.html"><strong>Barre Chord Chart</strong></a><br />
All barre chords are based on the first five chord forms (C, A, G, E, D). Use your index finger to barre and let your 2nd, 3rd and 4th fingers to make the open chord positions.</p></td>

<h2 id="more-on-guitar-chords">More on Guitar Chords</h2>
<p><a href="../../maximum/20020504.html"><strong>How Chord Progressions Work</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By Darrin Koltow</span><br />
Here's a common chord progression used in many popular songs. You'll hear it in songs like "What a Wonderful World," and "Cupid," by Sam Cooke, as well as some more modern tunes. I highly recommend singing a song you like as you play these changes. That makes the song come alive. Let's discuss why this song sounds as good as it does.</p>
<p><a href="theory.html"><strong>Chord Theory</strong></a> This introduction to chord theory is an excellent starting point for someone who is interested in learning more than tab but doesn't know where to start.</p>
<p><strong><a href="beginner.html">"Easy Chords" for Beginners</a></strong> These are the "easy" chords all beginners should learn inside and out. With them you can play almost any song. There are also two chord shapes that will help you find any major or minor chord, including sharps and flats.</p>
<p><strong><a href="../../columns/1999/19991122.html">Theory Without Tears</a></strong> The very word theory conjures up images of geometry class, or worse, physics lab. Scary stuff. Scarier, when you put the word music in front. Music is supposed to be fun, carefree, an expression of our feelings. Music is not science. Is it?</p>
<p><a href="diminished.html"><strong>Diminished Chords</strong></a> Diminished chords are very cool, and easy to understand. Diminished chords serve a cadential function just like a dominant 7. Just like all bar chords these are universal shapes and can be moved anywhere on the fretboard and you will come out with a diminished chord as long as the shape is retained.</p>
<p><a href="formation.html"><strong>Guide to Chord Formation</strong></a> This lesson gives you the information you need to be able to understand which notes make up a certain chord. After studying this lesson you will be able to work out the notes you need for any chord and work out what name should be given to any selection of notes.</p>
<p><strong><a href="../../help/faqs/answers2.html">Guitar Chord Help</a></strong> These topics cover some basics as well as some pretty involved theoretical questions. The questions range from beginner to experienced.</p>
<p><strong><a href="../../columns/2000/20000117.html">Unearthing The Structure</a></strong> In this series of articles on ear training we look at common and often used chord progressions. Whether you use this knowledge to write your own songs or figure out someone else's is entirely up to you.</p>
