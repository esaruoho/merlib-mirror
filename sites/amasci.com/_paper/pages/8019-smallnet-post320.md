Steve Morris \< <mlbco@sirius.com> \> contributed:

I recently finished an R/C conversion of a Guillow BF-109 kit (3/4" scale) and pictures of it can be seen on my web site at:

\< <http://www.sirius.com/~mlbco/BF1.JPG> \>\
\< <http://www.sirius.com/~mlbco/BF2.JPG> \>\
\< <http://www.sirius.com/~mlbco/BF3.JPG> \>\

This model follows in the form of several rubber-to-R/C conversions I've made in the last year; and the formula for success is:

1.  Use a PAW 0.03 diesel for power. It is quiet enough for park flying, will run 15 minutes on 3/4 ounce of fuel, loves a scale size propeller (7x3), throttles beautifully, can be safely electric started with a 1/2A-size starter, has the power of a reed valve .049. If you want to fly small with gas power and little noise you have to try this engine!!! I make my own fuel tanks from K&S thin brass shim stock and they are lighter than any commercially available tank.
2.  Use HS-50 servos glued to the structure. Make control horns from 1/16" ply. Hinge with strips of Sig Easy Hinge material. Use a 110 mAh 4-cell battery pack.
3.  Reinforce the dihedral braces with ply, fuselage nose with 1/16" sheet, and use 3/32" sheet for tail surfaces. Do not over-do the reinforcing or it will be too heavy to fly! Lighter is better than stronger!
4.  Shoot for a 10-12 ounce RTF weight for 24" biplanes and 28" WW2 scale models.
5.  Cover with Japanese tissue, seal with 2 coats of Nitrate (thinned 50/50), paint model with Acrylic color paints typically used on plastic scale models (uses water to thin and clean!), seal with matte finish spray polyurethane (from hardware store).
6.  Rudder/elevator control is OK on WW1 biplanes, but aileron/elevator is best for the WW2 models.

My BF 109 flies beautifully and is fully aerobatic when power is applied. When throttled back I can fly low and slow around the park in a very realistic style. These models look great in the air and the kit price is dirt cheap (about \$23 for the BF109).

Steve Morris

...Steve's contribution urges me to write about a paradoxical relationship between weight and load-carrying capacity of structural members such as wing spars. Heavier does NOT necessarily mean stronger!

Consider a wing spar, say 3/16" X 3/4". Would you believe that by reducing its weight by 50%, you can DOUBLE its load-carrying ability?

It's true. The rectangular spar, being of equal thickness from one end to the other, transmits all bending load to its "anchored end". But if the spar has half its material cut away, so that it tapers from a knife edge at the "free end" to full depth at the "anchored end", it will flex when loaded. That distributes the load equally along its length, reducing the stress at the anchored end to HALF what it is for a constant-thickness spar.

Because of the convenience of using stock balsa strips for wing and tail spars and leading edges, we've all acquired the habit of using these constant cross-section structural members in our models. They work, of course -- but a LOT of weight could be saved if we took the trouble to taper these parts.

As for plywood dihedral braces, I stopped using those 40 years ago. That was because I suddenly realized that half the wood in the usual plywood dihedral brace has its grain aligned the wrong way.

I used birch tongue depressors for dihedral braces for a while. But then I asked myself, "Why does a wing spar need to be far stronger on the inboard side of rib \#2 than on the outboard side?" I remembered the wing spars Joe Konefes used on his Buzzard Bombshell: not a dihedral brace in sight!

After that, I've never again used anything but balsa for "dihedral braces" -- and most of the time I just pre-assemble the wing spars in Buzzard Bombshell style. No problems -- and doing that guaran- tees that dihedral in the completed wing will be as designed. \[JW\]

A few random jottings:

SNIP!

\> "Electric flight running costs are zero" -- I don't think so, if that means a cost comparison of fuel versus battery power. I'm not only referring to the negligible cost of recharging batteries; rather that batteries need replacing at some time and the cost of a pack can be several times the price of a gallon of fuel, depending on where you live. \<

At SMALL sizes, this has to be true. A few years ago, a member of the British Electric Flight Association (BEFA) did a cost analysis of three years' worth of average club-style flying, using a typical sports glow model, a smaller gaspetrol burner and a decent sized electric (probably now of a type only found in museums -- a lot has happened to e-flight recently). The analysis was a realistic and even-handed effort; and the final outcome was a Cost Per Flight.

The gaspetrol and electric models came out near as durn equal dollarpounds per flight, while glow was three times the CPF of the other two. I think he used a .40 or .46 glow -- the outcome would have been much different had the Norvel BigMig 061 R/C been the most popular sized engine in club R/C flying :-).

I remember being horrified at the cost of glow fuel in the US, when I arrived in '93. Being of a tight nature, in Britain I'd go to Old Warden early in the year and buy a load of 5% or straight for the year. The last O.W. would be a load-up trip for the Winter!

Fuel there was cheap, castor oil coloured and worked. In the US, it was pink or green, expensive and no mention of contents other than the more nitro it contained, the more the label screamed the nitro content at you! At SMALL in '96, I well nigh cleaned Fox's out of 5% and 10% castor fuel, and still have some - went into electric power by late '96, and had about quit glow by end of '97,

One model was a geared tailless by Dana Gantt, a sort of Simitar that has evolved into an electric taildragger. This has a hot brushless running a high reduction ratio 'box and sounds great. The noise in flight is close to a radial engine heard at a distance, but is mostly gear and prop noise combined. Comparatively raucous alongside a straight drive Speed 400 model!

The only covering that ever gave me grief RF-wise was Micafilm. The "Stringer" wing-warping plane I built for MAN (August 1995) had a white Micafilm-covered fuselage. Radio was a one-channel Cox "Failsafe." (27 MHz)

Allan Leppanen \< <alepp@sympatico.ca> \> wrote:

For quite a few years I used a home-made 12 V starter for small engines from .099 to .19 that worked very nicely and cost peanuts.

It was made up of a scrap 12V car heater fan motor which happened to have a 1/4" diameter shaft which fit into a 1/4" drive socket that suited the prop nut of the engine in question.

I'd double nut the prop just to be sure that I wouldn't spin it off with the starter. The socket was secured with a number of tightly applied turns of black electrical tape. I fastened a small switch to the side of the motor and added leads with clips to use on a 12 V motorcycle battery or just connected to the battery in the car if that was handy.

Worked great; easier to use than the rubber cone on my Sullivan; never loosened a prop nut; never loosened a taped-on socket; and started the small motors quickly.

Al Leppanen, Cambridge, Ontario, Canada

Andrew Donatelli \< <andrew@donatelli.net> \> offers:

More Norvel stuff! Don Bailey wrote that he had idle problems with his BigMig .061. It sounds like he may have the throttle arm upside down. The idle screw should only stop the throttle from closing all the way. The way that the Norvel carb is designed, it will lean the mixture when reducing throttle; if the throttle arm is upside down it will richen the mixture at idle, causing the engine to load up.

Also try the Norvel fuel, it does run better than Omega.
