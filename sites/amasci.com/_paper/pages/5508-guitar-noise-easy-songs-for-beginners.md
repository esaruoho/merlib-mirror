<td width="100%"><h1 id="easy-songs-for-beginners">EASY SONGS FOR BEGINNERS</h1>
<p>If you already have a grasp of guitar basics (holding your guitar, tuning it) then the next step is to start building a repetoire of songs. This series of lessons will teach you a bit of music theory and technique while you learn to play some pretty easy and well known songs. For best results you really should begin with the first lesson and work your way up.</p>

<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
Let's combine some strumming techniques with our on-going lessons on developing bass lines. There's a lot of fun to be had with this one as we work through straight strumming, a little bass-and-chord work and even toss in some arpeggio playing.<br />

<p><a href="20000822.html"><strong>Lesson 1: Horse With No Name (The Simplest Song)</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
This week we take a break from chord theory and get into some song analysis, cleverly disguised as “Songs For Beginners.” We’ll take the easiest song I know and learn to play it. Not only is it a song most of you know – it’s also probably a song most of you would rather forget that you know.</p>
<p><a href="20000829.html"><strong>Lesson 2: Horse With No Name (Adding Some Personal Touches)</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
Last time out, I showed you an incredibly easy to play song, Dewey Bunnell's <em>Horse With No Name</em>, along with some basic strumming patterns. Today we're going to work on adding a bit of accessories to the basic model. Feel free to use any of the ideas, riffs or leads we develop here. Or you can use some of the things we go over as a base with which to come up with your own stuff.</p>
<p><a href="20001025.html"><strong>Lesson 3: For What It's Worth</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
This week we learn the old Buffalo Springfield classic. Since it's only two chords, we also get to learn a new strumming technique - how to get that cool acoustic percussive sound into our rhythmic patterns.</p>
<p><a href="20001101.html"><strong>Lesson 4: Feelin' Alright</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
Who says a two chord song has to be boring? Like anything, it all comes down to what kind of effort you decide to put into it. Creativity is whatever you bring to the table. It is what you do with something and not just something you do. This time out, we've got yet another two chord song to work on. It's called <em>Feelin' Alright</em> and it was written by Dave Mason back when he was with the group Traffic.</p>
<p><img src="../images/includesmp3.gif" data-align="right" width="134" height="26" alt="Includes MP3s" /><a href="20001222.html"><strong>Lesson 5: Three Marlenas</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
Usually the biggest challenge for any beginner is to be able to play "at speed." This does not mean to play something fast; it means to play something in a steady prescribed tempo. Fingering and playing a chord may come quite easily to some, but the chances are that sense of ease disappears pretty quickly when faced with more and more chord changes within a song.</p>
<p><a href="20010119.html"><strong>Lesson 6: Before You Accuse Me</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
Today we'll be branching out into three chord songs in a big way. We'll do this by learning a standard blues song, <em>Before You Accuse Me</em>, written by E. McDaniel, known to the world as Bo Diddley. But we're also going to be examining the theory behind what is known as "Twelve Bar Blues" so, in essence, you will be able to play almost any blues song (in any key) when we're finished with these next few lessons.</p>
<p><a href="20010205.html"><strong>Lesson 7: Roll Over Beethoven</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
Today we will look at the 12 bar blues pattern and typical blues shuffle rhythm. When we are done you will be able to play an amazing number of straight ahead rock and roll songs. All sorts, from Chuck Berry to the Rolling Stones to Led Zeppelin to Bruce Springsteen and more performers than I could possibly list in a simple introductory paragraph.</p>
<p><a href="20010424.html"><strong>Lesson 8: (Going To) Kansas City</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
Let’s wrap up our (all too) brief introduction into the (very basic) blues. Today we’re going to look at three things: the “turn around,” the (again, very basic) blues lead scale and what I call the “grand finale.” I have chosen to use the old blues standard <em>(Going To) Kansas City</em> for this lesson because I want to demonstrate these techniques on a song that you probably have heard but are not overly familiar with.</p>
<p><a href="20010508.html"><strong>Lesson 9: Margaritaville</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
Today we’re going to learn <em>Margaritaville</em> by Jimmy Buffet. In and of itself, this will not take very long. We will only need three chords (okay, four, but one (A7) is just a simple variation of another).</p>
<p><a href="20010513.html"><strong>Lesson 10: House Of The Rising Sun</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
In this lesson we’re going to learn two different fingerpicking patterns for <em>House of the Rising Sun</em>, a song performed by the Animals way back in the sixties. The first will be very straightforward, simply arpeggiating the chords in a very easy finger style. Then we will go on and doctor that version up a bit, throwing in slightly more complex picking which will include a bit of a moving bassline as well as using a standard alternating bassline in a few spots.</p>
<p><a href="20010720.html"><strong>Lesson 11: Bookends</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
Last time out we learned some basic fingerpicking with <em>House of the Rising Sun</em>. Today we’re going to do something that is both a little easier and also a little harder with an old Paul Simon song called <em>Bookends</em>. It’s a very simple song to learn and it will help us to develop some more coordination in our fingerstyle play. Specifically, it will serve to teach us to use two fingers on two different strings at the same time. And just to keep things even more interesting we’ll get to look at time signature changes within songs as well as what I call “planning ahead” in regards to finger placement.</p>
<p><a href="20010813.html"><strong>Lesson 12: I Shot The Sheriff</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
Today’s song is Bob Marley’s <em>I Shot The Sheriff</em>, which will not only serve as a delightful introduction to reggae rhythms, but I’ve also managed to get very sneaky and throw in some transposing as well.</p>
<p><a href="20011023.html"><strong>Lesson 13: If Not For You</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
In and of itself, Bob Dylan’s <em>If Not For You</em> is a very easy song. You won’t find any tricky chords and if you already know the song you’ll be able to breeze through this on the first take and then wonder why I’d make a lesson of it at all.</p>
<p><a href="20011205.html"><strong>Lesson 14: Riders on the Storm</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
Believe it or not, this is a blues song. At least in terms of song structure. It consists of four verses and each one is twelve measures in length. </p>
<p><a href="20011220.html"><strong>Lesson 15: Happy Christmas</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
In addition to being topical (both in terms of the season as well as in regard to the "topic of the month), John Lennon's <em>Happy Christmas</em> is a good way to follow up on some of the lessons we learned last time out.</p>
<p><img src="../images/includesmp3.gif" data-align="right" width="134" height="26" alt="Includes MP3s" /><a href="20020329.html"><strong>Lesson 16: Love The One You're With</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
In today's lesson, yet another (very) old chestnut from the early seventies, we'll be seeing practical applications of the chord shapes learned there. For good measure, we'll toss in a few (very) easy fills and then also look at how we'd play it in Drop D tuning.</p>
<p><a href="20020901.html"><strong>Lesson 17: Hey Hey My My</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
You may not know it, but Neil Young is a true hero to the fledgling guitarist. His songs are relatively easy enough to learn quickly and still complicated enough to give you things to work on in order to hone your guitar skills. And, as bonuses, they appeal to both the electric and acoustic guitarist.</p>
<p><a href="20021005.html"><strong>Lesson 18: Happy</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
We'll look at an arrangement of this classic for the single acoustic guitar, made possible by the use of open G tuning and a cool driving rhythm. After this lesson you'll have no trouble at all playing rhythm like Keith Richards. Of course we've included MIDI files to help you.</p>
<p><a href="20021124.html"><strong>Lesson 19: Fire</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
Sometimes the simplest of songs present the biggest of challenges - how do we take a basic song of four or five chords and make it memorable? Well, one way is to use all the dynamic power built into your guitar, working in bass lines along with the chords and playing with the dynamics of a song.</p>
<p><img src="../images/includesmp3.gif" data-align="right" width="134" height="26" alt="Includes MP3s" /><a href="20030126.html"><strong>Lesson 20: Tangled Up In Blue</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By David Hodge</span><br />
Let's take advantage of the MP3 files and do a lesson focusing on strumming. Here's the opening song from Dylan's classic album, Blood on the Tracks. Have fun!</p>
<p>After working your way through these lessons you may be ready to graduate to the next level: <a href="../intermediate/index.html">Songs for Intermediates</a>.</p></td>
