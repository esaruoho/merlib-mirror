Our friend [Roger](images/roger.jpg) inspired us to find some land to look after. Roger is seriously into the land stewardship business, having bought his first piece years ago, and expanded it over time to some 450 acres. OK, so we were not really looking for land on that scale, but we were interested in anything larger than typical exurban subdivisions. We looked up and down the eastern slopes of the Cascades from Ellensberg to the Canadian border, which was particularly inspiring to Arvia, since as an artist she liked the light and the open spaces of that region. We also really wanted to be away from utilities, since wherever there is piped power, people usually wind up expecting city values and services. Prices and taxes then rise to follow, and we'd be priced out, and have to move on.

<img src="images/ponderosa-bark.jpg" data-border="2" data-align="left" data-naturalsizeflag="0" width="288" height="384" />Our first area of interest was the Methow valley. Really neat place: beautiful, closeby wilderness hikes, moderate continental climate. Unfortunately, the Methow was discovered by Seattleites some 20 years ago, and now prices (and taxes) aren't that much different from urban areas in western Washington.

On to plan B. During one of our scouting trips, we happened across the town of Tonasket (named after an Indian chief). First thing that struck us about that place was the health-food Co-op - a very unlikely thing in rural eastern Washington. So we mentally filed this away. Some months later, I was researching some ads in the paper, and called a guy in Bellevue who had some land that sounded interesting in the Tonasket area. He put me in touch with a buddy of his who lived in town, and eventually, on a rather dreary February morning in 1998, I took a drive out to visit.

Derek however, wanted me to see some other land than the one I'd been thinking about (which looked on the map like a large-scale suburban plot). He and his wife own a ranch just above Tonasket in the foothills of the Okanogan Highlands. This area had originally been part of the John Fancher Ranch in the early part of the century when it was 33,000 acres or so. Over the years, it was split up and part of it became home to a "back to the land" bunch of the hippie generation in the late 60s and early 70s. They took their families and homeschooled their kids up in the hills, off the grid - it was some of them who started the Co-op in town. Derek wanted to sell some of this land, so he could move his cows onto better land further north.

Well, our station wagon wouldn't have made it up that jeep track, not in winter with deep ruts from the semi-ploughed job that the locals leave after a snowfall. But, it certainly was remote - around 5 hours from Seattle, almost as far as one can get from Puget Sound and still be in the state. I dragged poor Derek around in a couple of feet of snow, looking over the piece. The first view of it looked really interesting - a place that we both found attractive, and something we could actually buy.

### [The Original Parcel](images/homestead-way.jpg)

We spent another weekend in the spring doing our final hunting, and in best scientific fashion created a spreadsheet with the 5 top properties we'd seen, listing all kinds of factors such as proximity to things we wanted to do, neighbors, closeness or remoteness of the place, future suburbanisation of the area, as well as things like price. Then we did logistic regressions on the binary variable "Buy or not buy". Well not quite, but you get the idea.

After a year of looking, we made an offer on Derek's piece, haggled quite a bit over the price, the easements, and various other things. We finally closed in June of 1998 on the original land.

### [Middle 40](images/site-sunset.jpg)

No sooner had we done that, when Derek came back and asked us if we wanted to buy an adjoining 40 acre piece to the south. Well, we wound up buying that too in September of 1998, which we now call the Middle 40. This turned out to be a good decision, since we later found it has the best [site](images/siting.jpg) for a cabin, as well as some of the best places for pines to grow. There are 1.5m diameter stumps around, so unfortunately, we're not there to see them as trees, but they'll be back in our descendants lifetimes.

### [The Southern 20](images/shack.jpg)

Then that fall, while talking with our neighbor [Mark](images/mark.jpg), we learned that the owner of the 40 acres to the south of our most recent purchase was interested in selling: land fever takes over! This new land was legally divided in two 20 acre parcels, the southern one with a large cabin we call [The Battleship](images/battleship.jpg), and the northern 20 acres also with added destinations: a vintage 1970 shack with a corral, and a new-age wooden pyramid. (Sadly, these burned in the great fire of 2000) In addition, there are two dead Toyotas in the middle of a nice glade we named after Robin Hood. More usefully, there is an old drilled well which might still be workable; the theory is that it stopped producing water at a good enough rate, but for us, even half a gallon a minute could be usable. Well, I'm not sure we weren't taken advantage of by Clarence (a very nice old man), but finally, in December 1999 we had a deal to purchase what we call the Southern 20.

### [The Ravine](images/ravine.jpg)

Now, we were looking for a way to exclude [cows](images/cows.jpg) from the place. Due to the topography, if we could build a fence from the SE corner of Clarence's land to the ravine, this would have the effect of keeping them out from the whole of the eastern perimeter, since the ravine is so steep I think cows would topple over if they tried to go down there. The implication of this is that we needed to buy the parcel to the east of the Southern 20, that the ranch has been trying to sell for years. It's roughly a third woodland, a third flat meadow and a third steep ravine descending down to Antoine Creek. This piece has a completely separate eco-system down in the ravine - a place where the sun barely visits in the summer and is wholly absent during the winter; it has some typical west-side vegetation like Oregon Grape, as well as some really sunny slopes where the flowers come out around a month before the rest of the area and there's some of the only species of cactus native to Washington. Not exactly low bank riverfront though - there's a 200m drop down to Antoine Creek!

### The Future

We haven't given up the idea of buying land completely - neighbors to the north west might sell some day, and Clarence has been trying to sell his remaining 20 acre parcel for a while now...
