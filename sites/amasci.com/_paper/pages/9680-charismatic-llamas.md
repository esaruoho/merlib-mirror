<p><strong><span id="Eat"></span>Question: Do People Eat Llamas?</strong></p>
<p><strong>This was a question that came up on a Llama group list today, and Kelly had a prompt replied which I have reprinted with her permission:</strong></p>
<p><strong>Answer: If I had a dollar for each time someone has asked "Can you eat them?", I'd have a lot of dollars! At each fair, educational exhibit through 4-H, parade, relatives, etc. the question is always asked. I choose to say this: Yes you could eat them, but you could eat your dog, cat, or anything else you choose to stick in your mouth, that doesn't mean it's the thing to do or that you should choose to do that. I don't eat my llamas and I never will, period. I know some people say yes you could eat them, they do it in S. America. I think that is what keeps this topic alive, instead of just saying no you don't eat them. I also know people who have eaten them, it was the one they had to put down because of illness but it's all the same to me. If I have to put my horse or dog down I'm not going to eat them! Geeze.... Kelly, Wind Dancer Llama &amp; Herb Farm, Bow, Washington e-mail</strong></p>

<td colspan="2"><p><strong>My Personal Opinion Llamas are highly intelligent creatures, they provide service such as packing, their wool for fiber, their joy as PR llamas visiting children and people in rest homes, they can pull carts, walk in parades, appear on TV, and they make people feel relaxed and peaceful. Llamas have the ability to learn many things. Llama's are also used to guard flocks of sheep, and they have been used to rehabilitate mentally ill that have been unable to communicate. In prisons they have been used to help inmates communicate and deal with mental problems, they are gentle and loving with the blind and handicapped. This is not a creature I would want to eat, this is a friend who brings beauty into a lot of lives.</strong></p>
<p><strong>However, I can understand eating a chicken, they have no intelligence, you can't go back packing in the wilderness with a chicken, chickens don't do PR work and visit rest homes, chicken's are not used to enrich the life of emotionally or mentally ill people, you don't use chickens feathers to make a scarf or coat...well, what else do you do with a chicken? AND Cows, would I take a cow to a rest home to visit...I don't think so.</strong></p>
<p><strong><span id="horses"></span>Question:Your site is interesting for me as I have never heard of or seen a llama!! Are they a bit like horses but with shorter legs?</strong></p>
<p><strong>Answer: Llamas are nothing like horses, they are exceptionally intelligent and curious for one thing. For example, if a coyote, deer, or human they don't know, strange dog etc., comes into the pasture...they all come running towards what ever the object is. It's not because they are going to attack, but it is because they are curious and want to make sure the object is not a threat. The reaction from most strange animals is that they run away as fast as possible:^)).</strong></p>
<p><strong>Friends that have visited with their dog have gone in the pasture (with us), the llamas run up to the dog and then just watch, sniff and study him. Once the dog realizes they won't hurt him he starts relaxing and moving around.</strong></p>
<p><strong>They learn quickly, after repeating a task usually in 5 consecutive tries, they understand what you want and comply. Llama's work well with handicapped people and go visit rest homes, they have no problem walking down halls, by wheel chairs, in-between beds...they don't knock things over as they are very graceful and aware of what they are doing. Also a full grown Llama rarely weights more than 450 pounds, a horse is triple+, that size.</strong></p>

<p><strong><span id="spit"></span>Question: Do Llamas spit?</strong></p>
<p><strong>Answer: Yup Spit Happens, and I have been spit on, but it has never been directed at me, I just happened to be in the way of one Llama telling another Llama that he was boss! The spitting is something that they do with each other to establish a pecking order. For example when I put out fresh hay the oldest llama's attitude is that he is "first", if a junior llama comes along and he doesn't want to share the threatens to spit. If the younger doesn't get the message and back off...Well, spit happens.</strong></p>

<p><strong><span id="halters"></span>Question: I have finally bred for woolly llama babies, but now I can't get halters on them without getting their hair tangled in the buckles. Since they rarely stand still for their first lessons, do you have any suggestions for easy halter fastening? All thumbs</strong></p>
<p><strong>Answer: The easiest thing I have found is to take one of those thumbs and an opposing finger (usually on the left hand) and put it around the buckle itself. With your finger(s) between the back of the buckle and the young llama's neck and head wool, the fingers will flatten the hair down before it gets wrapped around the buckle. A side benefit of keeping a finger or two there is that you will not get the halter too tight. Also make sure you can fit a finger or two inside the nose piece after the halter is fastened. Adjustable halters make getting a good fit on a growing llama a lot easier. Remember, your little ones grow very fast and the halter that was fine last week may fit like an iron mask today. My halter trainees take major exception to halters that fit too tight -- a too tight halter can set my catching and haltering training back weeks. Especially with your young llamas, NEVER leave them unattended with their new halters on. Your first time halter wearer will usually try to rub this new strange growth off his face and can get himself hung up on something. Or, what can be equally as dangerous, he may rub the nose band down onto the soft tip of his nose restricting his breathing and frightening him. If you positively cannot get the hang of buckles, there are halters with snap closures. However, most of these are not recommended for training as the snap closures are not as secure as buckles.</strong></p>
<p><strong>If you are training in a catch pen or paddock, having the halter come off would only be a nuisance not a potential tragedy. Which ever style of halter you choose -- fixed, x-style, or adjustable nose band and buckle or snap closures -- make sure it fits your llama's face properly. Quickly on fit: the nose band should be well back on the bridge of the nose so it cannot slip down on and mash the soft cartilage in the nostril area; the nose and under chin straps should be loose enough so that the llama can eat, chew, drink, and yawn; and the back strap should fit behind the head just below the ears not draped down the neck.</strong> Question &amp; Answer supplied by: Nancy Larson &amp; Dale Schultz, North Umpqua Farms, Glide, OR</p>

<p><strong><span id="eating"></span>Question: Since I've put my young males on a diet this summer, they have gotten very pushy about eating out of the grain bucket as I am walking to their feeder. Do you have some suggestions to break this habit? Walking Target</strong></p>
<p><strong>Answer: Yes, if you do not want to be the meat in a llama sandwich, you need to break this habit. Not only is a full body hug by an adult male enough to take your breath away, he is loosing his respect for you by pushing you around.</strong></p>
<p><strong>First, llamas are very good with lids, so unless you are carrying something with a lid as tight fitting as Tupperware, forget placing a lid over your bucket. What I have found works well is to place an empty bucket inside the one with the grain in it. The llamas cannot get around the empty bucket to get to the grain, and they see that there is nothing in the bucket for them to fight over. After a couple of times of checking the bucket for grain and finding it empty, my males are happy to trot along behind me and wait for me to perform the magic of making the grain appear. You can feed them out of your hand if you like, and you still retain control. But by letting them dive into the feed bucket while you are still carrying it, gives them the control as well as endangering you physically.</strong> Question &amp; Answer supplied by: Nancy Larson &amp; Dale Schultz, North Umpqua Farms, Glide, OR</p>

<p><strong><span id="pre-potent"></span>Question: What does it mean when a male llama is "pre-potent?" Does it mean he is not yet potent (fertile)? Inquiring Mind</strong></p>
<p><strong>Answer: "Pre-potency" is not a fertility disaster nor is it an advertising gimmick to sell an ugly male llama. Usually the prefix "pre" refers to something coming before something else. In this case, it points to the llama's parents and grand-parents, etc. When a male llama (it can also apply to a female, but she is rarely advertised as much as the sire) is said to be "pre-potent," it means that he sires babies that look better than he looks with a range of females. This can be in the case of a single trait or color to the entire animal's package. Since the male is made up of a selection of genes from his parents and grand-parents, it is really their genes that are at work. So although the genes may not express themselves well in the male, they are available to be passed to his progeny.</strong></p>
<p><strong>If you are considering parting with some of your money for this male based upon claims of "pre-potency," examine his parentage well. Do they have the traits that you are hoping for in your babies. Also check the dams of these excellent offspring. If he is always bred to the females of your dreams, he may not be adding anything worthwhile to the baby. Finally, does he have enough babies on the ground to establish a definite trend?</strong> Question &amp; Answer supplied by: Nancy Larson &amp; Dale Schultz, North Umpqua Farms, Glide, OR</p>

<p><strong><span id="alfafa"></span>Question: What do you feed a llama when it's exceptionally cold that will help to warm them up?</strong></p>
<p><strong><strong>Answer:</strong> Woolly Acres is located in Rhinebeck, New York. A long way from Seattle, Washington. Our winters are windy, cold and snow covered. During one period of January of 1994, our temperatures read -30 degrees F. with wind chills of -40 F. The weanling and yearling female llamas kushed in 3 - 4 feet of snow, with nary a glance towards the warmth of the barn. I, their self-appointed human mama, was worried that her 'children' might be frozen in place. "Perhaps they're too cold to move!" Mama thought in a panic. On went layers of clothing, mittens, hat, boots and jacket. Mama looked like the Michelin Tire Man. This story is factual. What is does enlighten people about is the hardiness of our llamas, our concern over their well being and my own undying llove.</strong></p>
<p><strong>On her way out to the pasture, Mama grabbed the bag of alfalfa cubes a well meaning friend had given to the llamas as their Christmas present. "I'll break them up into tiny pieces and feed them to the ladies carefully. Alfalfa will warm them quickly." Mama with the 50 pound sack strode out into the weather. "Mama's llamas want a treat?" Mama took off her gloves and tried breaking the alfalfa cubes into proper llama sized edible portions. The cubes wouldn't budge apart. The llamas waited patiently, stretched and then kushed surrounding Mama. With chunk in hand, Mama put the alfalfa cube into her mouth and bit down HARD! The cube broke into pieces and with bits of alfalfa left in her mouth, Mama gave each one the wonderful warming morsels. Mama kept breaking up the chunks and more was left in Mama's mouth. Llamas were munching and everyone was content.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Mama forgot about the wind, cold and time. Llove doesn't wear a watch. The llamas, filled with alfalfa, snuggled back into the snow. Suddenly Papa was out, standing next to Mama. "Are you alright? I just realized it's been two hours!" he screamed his concern over the howling wind. Mama, chewing, looked up at Papa and serenely smiled. He gasped, "What are you eating? Your mouth is GREEN!" "Ah...um...alfalfa dear. I guess I'm chewing cud." "Honey", Papa gently said," Come on into the house now. You've been out here WAY too long." Mama, still quietly ruminating, gave each llady a hug and went trudging off with Papa towards the house.</strong> Question and Answer provided by Sharon N. Miller</p>

<p><strong><span id="footed"></span>Question: How useful are Llamas for packing and hiking in the Anirondacks?</strong></p>

&#10;<p><strong><span id="hum"></span>Question: Why do Llama's hum?</strong></p>
<p><strong>Answer: one question 90% of the people who visit our farm ask is: "Why do llamas hum?" Bob and I look at each other and one of us says "Because they don't know the words!" It's old as the hills among llama people, but the people look at each other and at us and then start laughing too. I love it when the moms hum to their new babies and the babies hum back - and watching the moms move their babies by pushing them gently with their chests in the direction they want to go!!</strong></p>

<p><strong><span id="wash"></span> Question: Wow, these are amazing creatures! Do you need to wash them though?</strong></p>
<p><strong>Answer: Washing them isn't the problem, it's blow dry them that takes so long.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Llamas are pretty clean animals, most of the time you only give them a bath if you are going to be showing them or if you are getting ready to harvest the fiber. They have their own process for keeping clean, it's called a dust bath. They create areas that are free of debrie and dry, then they role around in the dirt. This fluffs up their wool and loosens debrie and dirt, which falls out of the wool. Sometimes when the weather is really nasty you can hardly tell what their original color was, after a couple of dry days and a few rolls in the dust, they are back to looking clean and normal.</strong></p>
<p><strong>What I notice in the little boys, those around 6-8 months, is that haven't quite gotten down when to and when not to take a dust bath. You should see how funny they look when they decide to take a dust bath but it turns out to be a "mud bath". They make a total mess of themselves, like little kids making mud pies and end up wearing them. The older boys are more patient, they wait for some dry spots before they are willing to take their next dust bath.</strong></p>

<p><strong><span id="harvest"></span>Question: What do you mean by harvesting the wool/fiber?</strong></p>
<p><strong>Answer: I can only give a very basic answer here as I don't really know much about this area of raising Llamas, however soon there will be a new site that will have much more information.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Harvesting wool is like shearing sheep, but more like grooming a poodle. Not that I have seen very much of either, but from what I have observed so far, sheep look really funny and all blotchy after they have been sheared. Llamas on the other hand, look like highly styled pieces of art work. You remove the wool for one of two, or both reasons. First to use the wool for spinning, second is when the weather it very hot, to help the animal stay keep cool and reduce the risk of heat stress. Usually a skilled person, (llama groomer???) is hired for this task.</strong></p>
<p><strong>There are also people like me that do it themselves. Last Spring Jerry and I were sitting out on the patio, enjoying the beautiful warm evening and watching our llamas playing in the field. After a couple glasses of wine I decided to go catch my oldest llama which was the only one we have never been able to get a halter on. Guess what, I actually got him in the barn and haltered. Then I didn't know what to do with him, I didn't dare try to take him out of the barn because he would have tossed me around like a rag doll. I wanted to spend time with him and have he get used to being touched and having a halter on. So I got a brain storm.</strong></p>
<p><strong>I ran to the house and got my sewing scissors, tied him to a post in the barn and started shearing him. I found out three things, once you start it's hard to figure out where to stop. Second, I should have been wearing my glasses. Third, you've heard the warning "don't drink and drive"...need I say more. End result, he didn't look like either a sheep or a well groomed llama, he looked like he had been attacked by a mad woman on a bad LSD trip!</strong></p>
