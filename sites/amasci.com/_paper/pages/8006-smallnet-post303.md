...Gang, the reason for the long delay since the previous Posting is low input. That's been good for me, though. I've been able to do a lot more modeling in the last week than I have for quite a while.

Could it be that SMALLsters have run out of things to contribute? Lately it seems that the only topic that much material comes in about is engines -- mostly about new ones on the market.

Why is that? Personally, I've been messing with model engines sufficiently long that I no longer get excited about ANY new motor on the market. Those only begin to interest me when they've been around a while, and have developed a reputation for reliability.

But SMALL encompasses a much larger territory than just engine- powered models! For example, here are a bunch of CO2 fans amongst us -- but nobody's been sending in any CO2-related submissions.

(One item that we need to know more about is bulk liquid CO2 tanks, say from 200 gram capacity on up to a couple of pounds. Yes, Peck sells a 2 1/2 pound tank plus a "charging tip" adapter for it. But the set costs over \$130, and supplied empty at that...!)

I've recently been investigating the possibility of using a modified paint-ball gun tank. Wal-Mart sells those for under \$40. However, they don't refill them -- and so far I haven't been able to find any source who does. (One of my sons \[who lives in Tupelo, Mississippi\] is a paint-ball gun fan. I've asked him about CO2 tank refill sources, and the closest one he's been able to find so far is in Jacksonville, Florida. Not one of the local gun shops here in southeastern Alabama could help me on this, either...)

...What about compressed air power? The Air Hogs "package" is mighty inexpensive, and separating the power unit from the molded foam airplane isn't difficult. The tank and motor are ingeniously assembled in such a way that they still fit onto the supplied air pump even with the fuselage of the airplane removed.

I haven't given any thought to HOW to do this yet, but I believe that a quick-release sort of mount could be devised for the Air Hogs motor/tank, that would allow it to be easily attached to and removed from a model. (I'm thinking about enlarging one of Bill Hannan's cutie-pie flyers for Air Hogs power.) (Yes, FREE FLIGHT !)

...What about rubber power? How about gliders? Jetex is back too !

C'mon, gang -- I know there are lots of you out there who are having fun with NON-engine-powered airplanes; and with NON-R/C types too. How about sharing your experiences and expertise? \[JW\]

Andy Woitowicz \< <ajw@ntl.sympatico.ca> \> wrote:

Regarding the VA .049 MK I with the split crankcase, this is the best throttling engine I've ever seen in my thirty-five years of modelling. I was lucky enough to get an article published in Flying Models on my adventures in throttling this engine. See the May 1997 issue. My VA's were rigged up with both the G-Mark and the Cox .05 R/C throttle. Both worked very well. My best numbers were 23K on a 5.5 X 2 APC and 25% Sidewinder fuel. I used the VA on a 14 ounce, 34" span Ugly Stik as well as a profile Sukhoi. As Dave Campbell suggests, the VA likes the smaller props. My aircraft flew well on 6 X 3 black Tornados, but they really came alive with the APC.

As Joe advises, the optimum prop disk area is proportional to the wing area. My Stik had some 220 squares and did well on the smaller prop. I've flown VA-assisted gliders in the 600 square inch area and the 6 X 3 did better there.

With all due respect to everyone, I think it is very important to consider type of aircraft, expected speed envelope, overall drag and the load imposed on an engine when determining the optimum prop size to use. Not to mention the engine's timing and whether we're referring to glow or diesel.

When I discovered the original VA's perfect throttling, there weren't a lot of throttled .049s around other than the Cox R/C .05, and its throttling was inferior to the VA. I set about to improve the throttling of my collection of TD .049s. All will be revealed in a forthcoming article recently accepted by Flying Models. The same article describes my efforts to improve the throttling of the Norvels as well.

Also described is an exhaust throttle complete with conventional muffler that throttles the TD, Babe Bee and the Cox Texaco to perfection. All will idle for 10 minutes without faltering and acceleration is instant with no smoke or spitting fuel. Because the Texaco was turning a 7 X 3.5 at a very low rpm and because it had a muffler, it was so quiet, you could hear the reed vibrating.

As far as engines in the .07 range, there is yet another -- but as far as I know, I've got the only one. This is an MP Jets .06 into which I've stuffed the piston and liner out of the AP Hornet .09. This actually gives a displacement of .078. At no increase in weight, I get an engine that turns a 6 X 3 black Tornado in the 21,000 to 22,000 rpm range.

I utilized the Jets' own two needle throttle; and despite the two needles, an air bleed hole was required to get very good throttling from 5000 to 21,500 average. This engine has glow button adapters to accept Glow-Bee, Nelson and conventional plugs -- and the experiment is still currently in progress. Bigger props on glow will be tried and a diesel head is in the works.

All in fun, Andy Woitowicz

George Aldrich \< <amadist8@satx.rr.com> \> also comments on small modern glow engines:

The recent comments about the VA Mk I and Mk II are very well said. There is a suction venturi available for either engine however.

For those of you that want to swing big props on an .049 engine, you all should try the Fox .049, now marketed by a man in Kentucky. It will swing a 6 x 3 at an honest 19K, on most decent nitro fuels in the 25% to 35% range.

When this engine was first released back in the 1960's, Dale Kirn found it 1000 revs better than the Cox TD. He told LeRoy Cox this, and Cox merely said, "Don't tell anybody". Dale didn't, and it went away !

There has been much gnashing of teeth recently over the Nostalgia F.F. rules, and modifications to the Cox Medallion. With the Fox available to all, the TD has no advantage, so the worry about any other is unimportant really !

The fact remains, that if you turn a ball socket piston/rod set up over 22,500 for very long, IT WILL BREAK !

If any of you really want to know how to prop a certain engine, make a degree wheel, so you know what the exhaust timing is. You can't expect an engine with much over 155 degrees of exhaust timing to swing larger props ! The Fox has something like 112 degrees ONLY ! Lots of power stroke there for bigger props !

GMA

George Penniket \< <penniket@netidea.com> \> informs us,

A couple more observations on the Tiger Moth, which incidentally was the aircraft in which I learned to fly in 1942. (Gosh, that's 58 years ago this July, believe it or not!)

De Haviland didn't make up new jigs to build the Tiger's wings, apparently, except to shorten the rear spars and change the angle of the root ribs. Close examination of the wings will reveal that the ribs are all at right angles to the spars, NOT in line with the airflow!!

Also, all Gypsy engines, (made by De Haviland), as far as I know, rotated anti-clockwise when viewed from the rear, like an automobile engine, so a modeler entering a Tiger Moth, or any other Gypsy-engined aircraft model in a scale competition should put a left-handed prop on it to get full static points.

Regards, George.

...That's about it for the e-mail contributions. To add a little more "bulk" to this Posting, here's a "shop technique" I've used from time to time; most recently last week.

I had bought a box of Sanyo nicads to replace outdated ones in my R/C equipment. I prefer the type with solder tabs -- but was surprised when I first saw these new cells, because the "tabs" are really fairly long straps, and have no "wire holes".

The straps are fine for making up multiple-cell packs; but for attaching wires, I prefer having a hole to hook the wire into. The Sanyo nicad straps are soft copper; too thin & yielding to even think of drilling. So I re-activated my "drill press punch" for making neat holes in thin sheet metal. (It works well in plastic and 1/32" & thinner plywood too.)

I clamp a small block of metal firmly in my drill press vise, with its flat top surface level with the table. Then I drill the size hole I want -- usually 1/16" or 3/32" diameter -- through the block. Then without moving the vise, I remove the drill bit from the press chuck, and replace it with a piece of the same diameter music wire.

The music wire has its lower end carefully ground flat, with the edges left sharp. This now becomes a "punch", and the drilled hole in the metal block is a "die". Using the drill press's operating lever to provide the force, it's an easy matter to punch all the holes I need in items such as nicad terminal straps. \[JW\]
