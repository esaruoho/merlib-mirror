# BP - Beyond Petroleum or Beyond Probity?

*Source page: `~rarnold/driessen.htm`*

**<span style="font-size: 16.0pt; font-family: Arial; color: #333399">BP: Beyond Petroleum or Beyond Probity?</span>**

## <span style="color:#333399">As ideology, activism, marketing, politics and financial gain converge, </span>

## <span style="color:#333399">                                                        one company stands out          </span>

 

 

UK-based British Petroleum became the worlds second largest hydrocarbons producer in 1998, when it finalized a \$55 billion merger with Amoco Corporation and changed its name to BP Amoco. After a \$36 billion merger with Arco the following year, the company adopted the simpler moniker BP. Now, according to its latest marketing campaign, BP no longer stands for British Petroleum, but for *Beyond* Petroleum.<a href="#_edn1" id="_ednref1"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[1]</span></span></a>

The company has installed expensive solar panels on 200 of its 17,000 service stations and launched a barrage of news releases and newspaper, television and wall ads asserting its basic corporate message: We protect the environment, vigorously support the Kyoto Protocol, devote vast sums to wind and solar energy and by the way, still produce a little petroleum. (But we produce it more responsibly than our competitors.) Televised interviews with people on the street expand on the same themes.

One Great Beyondo ad announces that BP was the first oil company to publicly recognize the risks of global climate change and set a target to reduce its own greenhouse gas emissions. Another claims the company voluntarily introduced cleaner burning low-sulfur fuels. Yet another says, Were one of the largest producers of natural gas and are investing in the new energy sources of the future hydrogen and wind. Its a start.

A building-sized wall ad along Washington, DCs New York Avenue boldly proclaims: Solar, natural gas, hydrogen, wind. And oh yes, oil. Its a start. Another tries to get beyond the guffaw test with the line: We believe in alternative energy. Like solar cappuccino.

The ads have not gone unchallenged. Conservatives, environmentalists and journalists alike have voiced bemusement, skepticism and even outrage. Indeed, the credibility of the Beyond Petroleum message is neatly summed up by the fact that the enormous (30 x 60 foot) wall ads are made of vinyl as in plastic, as in petroleum.

Certainly, the ads stretch the truth, gloss over inconvenient facts, and rely more on spin than on science, economics or reality. *Fortune* magazine reacted thus: Well, please: If the worlds second largest oil company is beyond petroleum, FORTUNE is beyond words.<a href="#_edn2" id="_ednref2"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[2]</span></span></a> One wag even suggested that BP might want to address its identity crisis yet again, file for one more name change and just call the company BS.

A green activist group in the Netherlands decried BPs hypocrisy, suggested that BP actually stands for Boiling the Planet, and noted that the company is still increasing oil production and moving to exploit some of the worlds most sensitive ecological areas.<a href="#_edn3" id="_ednref3"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[3]</span></span></a> Others deride the entire campaign as slick advertising and blatant greenwashing, saying BP is hardly beyond petroleum; its all about petroleum.<a href="#_edn4" id="_ednref4"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[4]</span></span></a>

Clearly, BPs effort to reinvent itself as a born-again savior of the environment raises some very interesting questions especially against the backdrop of recent history, where the glowing claims of Enron, Global Crossing, WorldCom and other companies were ultimately revealed as nothing more that hot air, clever manipulation of facts and figures, and criminal fraud.

One can legitimately ask, Has BP really gone Beyond Petroleum or just Beyond Probity?

 

But the basic question does not apply only to British Petroleum. It is symptomatic of a much larger problem. It is a highly visible example of the propensity of many corporations (for- and not-for-profit alike) to stretch the truth reinvent reality substitute hype, spin and brilliant advertising for honesty and play fast and loose with ethics, the law and the numbers to promote their products and agendas, attract investors (and donors), and convince pliant journalists, politicians and bureaucrats to turn the corporate agendas into coercive public policies.

 

One would think that all the condemnations, investigations, recriminations and legislation of the past year would foster a new era of honesty and protection of consumers and investors. Most companies and other organizations have always been honest. Most others have certainly gotten religion. But not all.

Some have merely become more ingenious, more arrogant in their stolid belief that their way is the True Way, or simply more fully protected by politicians and activists who share their agenda and arrogance.

In many cases, they voice newfound commitments to pious goals like sustainable development, Corporate Social Responsibility, saving Mother Earth, environmental justice and renewable energy. Often, they behave as though the mere utterance of these terms ends the debate and puts skeptics on the side of evil. Just as often, they arrogantly assume they are immune from any attempt to hold them responsible for their actions, because they are too vital to their local (or even the world) community to worry about accountability

What is happening is a profound and disturbing convergence of ideology, activism, marketing, politics and financial gain, all in the service of ultra-liberal political agendas.

This is occurring not just on the part of huge multinational corporations like BP. It is also the *modus operandi* for the huge multinational ethical investment groups, foundations and NGOs (nonprofit Non-Governmental Organizations) that increasingly dominate the global political scene. These groups often work hand-in-glove with many companies condemning and shaking them down one day, then accepting secretive contributions and devising joint legislative, legal, regulatory and public relations strategies with them the next.

It is crucial that they be exposed to the harsh light of day and that they be compelled to operate in accord with the same rules that govern the rest of our society.

**<span style="font-size: 16.0pt; font-family: Arial; color: #333399">Renewable Energy</span>**

<span style="font-size: 10.0pt"> </span>

Has BP really gone Beyond Petroleum? Where does the hype over BPs investments in fossil fuels and renewable energy end and the reality begin? When will the romantic promise of near-term renewable energy reflect the reality of costs, energy output and environmental impacts?

<span style="font-size: 10.0pt"> </span>

Over the past six years, BP invested more than \$200 million in solar power. It plans to expand this into a billion-dollar business by 2007. Worldwide, it generates a combined total of 77 megawatts (MW) of wind and solar power.<a href="#_edn5" id="_ednref5"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[5]</span></span></a> These numbers and the companys ad campaign make its commitment to renewable energy sound quite impressive. However:

- During 2001 alone, BP spent \$8.5 billion exploring for and producing oil and gas. So its average annual investment of \$33.3 million in solar amounts to an almost imperceptible 0.4 percent of its 2001 petroleum expenditures.<a href="#_edn6" id="_ednref6"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[6]</span></span></a>
- Its total 6-year solar investment is a mere 1.3 percent of what it plans to spend drilling for oil and gas in the Gulf of Mexico alone, over the next 15 years. Its proposed billion-dollar business five years out is just 0.6 percent of the companys current worth (\$174 billion).<a href="#_edn7" id="_ednref7"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[7]</span></span></a>
- Its 77 MW of solar and wind output is barely enough to keep Boise, Idahos lights lit for a year whereas its oil and natural gas production (3.4 billion barrels of oil equivalent, or BOE) could satisfy all of Americas oil needs for six months.<a href="#_edn8" id="_ednref8"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[8]</span></span></a>
- BPs beyond petroleum advertising campaign is expected to cost the company \$100 million a year three times what its actually spending annually on solar power.<a href="#_edn9" id="_ednref9"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[9]</span></span></a> If BP executives were completely honest about it, says the *Washington Times*, theyd have to admit the company spends far more in a single year burnishing its environmental image than it has invested in solar power in the last six years.<a href="#_edn10" id="_ednref10"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[10]</span></span></a> And shareholder activist group SANE BP says the company spent more on its new eco-friendly helios logo than it did on renewable energy during all of 2000.<a href="#_edn11" id="_ednref11"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[11]</span></span></a>
- BP became the worlds largest producer of solar energy primarily by spending \$45 million to buy the Solarex Corporation<a href="#_edn12" id="_ednref12"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[12]</span></span></a> 0.1 percent of what it spent to buy Arco or 0.9 percent of what will spend between 2002 and 2006 on oil exploration in Alaska.
- Wind and solar currently provide only 0.1 percent of global primary energy production.<a href="#_edn13" id="_ednref13"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[13]</span></span></a>
- Environmentalists excoriate ExxonMobil as an eco-Darth Vader. Yet it spent over \$500 million on renewable energy R&D two decades ago (over \$1 billion in 2002 dollars), before abandoning the effort as too fraught with scientific and economic uncertainty, and refocusing on fossil fuels.<a href="#_edn14" id="_ednref14"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[14]</span></span></a> However, the company still invests heavily in fuel cell technology, energy efficiency, and reducing carbon dioxide and other GHG emissions.
- ChevronTexacos renewable energy division focuses on fuel cells, improved batteries (via a joint venture with Ovonics), hydrogen, wind and solar. But the company gets about as much green ink and acclaim for these environmental investments as does ExxonMobil.
- Shell Oil Company also operates a fledgling wind-power business and, having purchased Siemen's solar photovoltaic operations, is now the fourth-largest solar-energy business in the world. The company also emphasizes biomass burning wood in power plants to generate electricity. It is planning to invest \$500 million to \$1 billion in renewables over the next five years (and a like amount in oil shale), and believes there is a chance that renewable energy could become a profitable business over the next several decades.<a href="#_edn15" id="_ednref15"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[15]</span></span></a>

Even BP regional president Bob Malone admits the companys press releases, wall ads and investments will not take it beyond petroleum for at least a couple decades.<a href="#_edn16" id="_ednref16"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[16]</span></span></a>

But that inconvenient fact has apparently not gotten in the way of its advertising hyperbole, or efforts to tout its bottom line and share prices. Neither have the environmental, economic and practical downsides of politically correct renewable energy. Renewables will eventually play a growing role in our energy mix. The electricity they generate is more expensive than fossil, hydro or nuclear versions, but its still a welcome addition to power grids. However, decisions and ad campaigns need to reflect more hard reality, and less romanticism, hype and deception.

**<span style="font-size: 14.0pt; font-family: Arial; color: #333399">Wind energy</span>**<span style="font-family:
Arial;color:#333399"> </span>

A gas-fired power plant capable of generating 50 megawatts of electricity typically requires 3-5 acres of land. A 50 MW wind facility, by contrast, needs over 4,000 acres.

The American Wind Energy Association claims the United States could generate 20 percent of its electricity by dedicating less than 1 percent of our landmass to wind farms. (We now get about 20 percent of our electricity from nuclear power plants that utilize roughly 73,000 acres.)<a href="#_edn17" id="_ednref17"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[17]</span></span></a>

1 percent of the U.S. is 23,000,000 acres the entire state of Virginia (or the entire island of Cuba). Covering this much land with tall, noisy wind turbines is hardly an earth-friendly or sustainable alternative to fossil fuels. Moreover, these Cuisinarts of the air kill thousands of raptors and other birds annually a fact that would likely bring any proposal for a fossil fuel power plant to a screeching stop.<a href="#_edn18" id="_ednref18"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[18]</span></span></a>

Wind turbines work only when the wind is blowing at the right speed. Their actual output hovers at 35 percent of their rated capacity, and can go much lower. Thus, gas-fired power plants would still be needed, standing idle much of the time but ready to provide substitute or supplemental electricity to keep businesses, schools, factories, homes and essential services like police and fire departments, traffic lights, water treatment plants and hospitals operating.

A 555-MW gas-fired generating plant inaugurated in July 2001 by California Governor Gray Davis produced more electricity in its first year at a mere 72 percent of capacity (3.5 billion kilowatt-hours) than *all* the states 13,000 wind turbines produced in 1999.<a href="#_edn19" id="_ednref19"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[19]</span></span></a>

Britains planned Welsh wind farm of 39 turbines (Europes largest) will have a total rated capacity of just 60 megawatts. At a more realistic 18 MW output, 4,500 of these 400-foot-tall behemoths would be needed to replace a single 2,000 MW coal- or gas-fired power plant.<a href="#_edn20" id="_ednref20"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[20]</span></span></a>

Many ideological environmentalists support renewable energy only as long as it is a theoretical substitute for detested fossil fuels. Once a theoretical choice becomes an actual proposal, support evaporates. In Massachusetts, for example, pro-renewable energy sentiments were transformed almost overnight into gale-force indignation, when Cape Wind Associates proposed a wind farm off the Cape Cod coast. The Not-On-My-Beachfront lobbyists were aghast at the thought of enormous, ugly, scenery-destroying wind turbines off their scenic headlands.<a href="#_edn21" id="_ednref21"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[21]</span></span></a>

Much the same reaction occurs when someone proposes to build a geothermal energy plant, since most of the good U.S. geothermal sites are near scenic areas like Yellowstone, Lassen or Hawaii Volcanoes National Park.

**<span style="font-size: 14.0pt; font-family: Arial; color: #333399">Solar power</span>**<span style="font-family:Arial;color:#333399"> </span>

Producing electricity from solar photovoltaic cells is also land intensive. A 50 MW operation means blanketing well over 1,000 acres with solar panels and access roads to maintain and clean them (assuming an optimistic 10 W/m2 or 5 percent peak efficiency). Using the sun to meet Californias electricity needs would mean paving over hundreds of thousands of acres of desert habitat along with their resident plant and animal life.<a href="#_edn22" id="_ednref22"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[22]</span></span></a> Solar power also requires expensive gas-fired backup electrical generators, for nighttime and cloudy days.

**<span style="font-size: 14.0pt; font-family: Arial; color: #333399">Hydrogen</span>**<span style="font-family:Arial;color:#333399"> </span>

Hydrogen may be abundant, but separating it from other molecules is expensive, and takes more energy than is generated by running pure hydrogen through fuel cells. Per unit of heat generated, more CO2 is produced by making H2 \[hydrogen\] from fossil fuel than by burning the fossil fuel directly.<a href="#_edn23" id="_ednref23"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[23]</span></span></a> Ironically, some have proposed that nuclear power plants be built to generate electricity by day and produce hydrogen molecules by night.<a href="#_edn24" id="_ednref24"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[24]</span></span></a>

Its a chicken-and-egg problem. We cannot have large numbers of fuel-cell vehicles without adequate fuel available to support them, but we will not be able to create the required infrastructure unless there are significant numbers of fuel-cell vehicles on the roadways.<a href="#_edn25" id="_ednref25"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[25]</span></span></a>

America has a trillion-dollar infrastructure in place for its gasoline-powered automotive fleet. Replacing it with an effective system for distributing and serving up hydrogen is a daunting economic challenge.<a href="#_edn26" id="_ednref26"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[26]</span></span></a> Fuel cells based on gasoline would use our existing infrastructure and enable cars to increase their mileage and reduce pollution significantly.

Getting hydrogen into cars is also a challenge. A single tank of gasoline contains 2 million Btus of energy. A five-gallon propane tank full of hydrogen at 150 psi holds only 7,000 Btu meaning 285 barbecue tanks would be needed to equal a tank of gasoline.<a href="#_edn27" id="_ednref27"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[27]</span></span></a> A daunting task, even for a huge SUV. Heavier high-pressure containers of liquefied hydrogen could reduce the space requirement to about the volume of an average car trunk, but this would wipe out cargo space and spark fears of Hindenburgian conflagrations, if a container ruptured in a collision.<a href="#_edn28" id="_ednref28"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[28]</span></span></a>

**<span style="font-size: 14.0pt; font-family: Arial; color: #333399">Biomass</span>**<span style="font-family:Arial;color:#333399"> </span>

Shell Oil plans to grow trees on sustainably managed plantations, solely to burn them to generate electricity. It claims replanting the trees will absorb the carbon dioxide liberated during combustion, thereby preventing global warming. (Will BP follow suit?)

Lewis Carroll and Jonathon Swift would have delighted at exploring how reverting to wood fires represents progress in an age of nuclear power or ecological sanity in an era when radical environmentalists oppose cutting even diseased or burned trees (except perhaps if solar-powered chain saws alone were used, and if no one made a profit off the logging).<a href="#_edn29" id="_ednref29"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[29]</span></span></a>

**<span style="font-size: 14.0pt; font-family: Arial; color: #333399">Too much energy?</span>**<span style="font-family:Arial;color:#333399"> </span>

Even if all the environmental problems associated with fossil and nuclear power could be solved, one suspects that radical environmentalists would still be unhappy. Their real gripe appears to be that nuclear and fossil fuels actually do what they were designed to do: provide reliable, affordable, abundant energy.

- Itd be a little short of disastrous for us to discover a source of clean, cheap, abundant energy because of what we would do with it. We ought to be looking for energy sources that are adequate for our needs, but won't give us the excesses of concentrated energy with which we could do mischief to the Earth or to each other.<a href="#_edn30" id="_ednref30"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[30]</span></span></a>
- Giving society cheap, abundant energy would be the equivalent of giving an idiot child a machine gun.<a href="#_edn31" id="_ednref31"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[31]</span></span></a>

But others recognize the vital, fundamental role that energy plays in modern society.

-  A reliable and affordable supply of energy is absolutely critical to maintaining and expanding economic prosperity where such prosperity already exists and to creating it where it does not.<a href="#_edn32" id="_ednref32"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[32]</span></span></a>

<span style="font-family: Symbol"><span style="font:7.0pt &quot;Times New Roman&quot;">        </span></span>Reliable and affordable access to modern energy services is an indicator of sustainable development, for without it basic needs cannot be satisfied.<a href="#_edn33" id="_ednref33"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[33]</span></span></a>

The triple bottom line here is really quite simple.

First, renewable energy is not yet ready for prime time. Not only is it highly misleading to tell investors, consumers and policy makers that it is ready now, or just around the corner. It also results in terrible tax, economic and public policy decisions.

Second, the environmental case for renewables erroneously assumes that: wind and solar facilities are sustainable and ecologically benign; burning fossil fuels causes global warming; and fossil fuels are rapidly being depleted. In fact, as just discussed, the environmental impacts of wind and solar energy are numerous and significant. As noted elsewhere in this paper, the frightening tales about catastrophic global climate change are based on computer models and activist scare-mongering and have no basis in scientific evidence.

As to exhausting the worlds fossil fuel supplies, that day may eventually come (though some geologists believe the earth is continually creating new supplies of oil and natural gas). But that day is much farther in the future than green activists care to admit.

<span style="font-family: Symbol; color: windowtext"><span style="font:7.0pt &quot;Times New Roman&quot;">        </span></span>In 1947, the worlds proven reserves of crude oil totaled only 68 billion barrels. Over the next 50 years, we consumed 783 billion barrels and at the end of 1998 still had proven reserves of 1,050 billion barrels! Back in 1966, the world had proven natural gas reserves of just 1,040 trillion cubic feet (tcf). By the end of 1998, we had used up 1,880 tcf and still had untapped reserves of 5,145 tcf!

<span style="font-family: Symbol; color: windowtext"><span style="font:7.0pt &quot;Times New Roman&quot;">        </span></span>At 1998 consumption rates, todays proven reserves are equal to 43 years of oil and 62 years of natural gas and crews are still discovering new deposits all over the world.<a href="#_edn34" id="_ednref34"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[34]</span></span></a>

Third and most important, for radical NGOs and government policy makers to tell Third World nations that they must rely on wind and solar and forego hydroelectric or fossil fuel projects is to deprive them of reliable and affordable electricity for their citizens. It throws new obstacles in the path of their economic development and social, health and environmental improvement. Worst, it condemns billions of people in these countries to perpetual poverty and misery.

And it does so for no valid reason but only to promote the hard-baked ideologies of vocal activists, who refuse to let science, economics or opposing viewpoints stand in their way, and whose indifference to the suffering and aspirations of poor people the world over will eventually be their undoing.

### <span style="font-size: 16.0pt; color: #333399">Corporate Social Responsibility</span>

 

What is Corporate Social Responsibility? Who developed the concept and definition? What is the source of their moral and legal authority for determining what is ethical or socially responsible or in accordance with societys expectations? Why have other stakeholders had only a limited role or voice in this process and why are *their* interests not reflected in the CSR definition or application? Why have certain companies and foundations collaborated so closely with NGO and government activists in promoting the concept?

 

Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) purports to assign to businesses new roles and purposes, above their duties to shareholders and customers, and beyond their traditional practice of supporting families and institutions in their communities and nations. Now they are told they must embrace a radical new vision of corporate citizenship and run their affairs in close conjunction with an array of self-appointed stakeholders so as to promote the precautionary principle and sustainable development.

- According to the former, precautionary measures must be taken, whenever an activity raises threats of harm to human health or the environment, even if some cause and effect relationships are not yet fully established scientifically. This is just common sense.<a href="#_edn35" id="_ednref35"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[35]</span></span></a>
- Development is sustainable only if it meets the needs and aspirations of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.<a href="#_edn36" id="_ednref36"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[36]</span></span></a>

Adherence to these concepts supposedly generates a triple bottom line (social, economic and environmental) that companies should meet in judging true profitability and citizenship. Only by measuring their costs, benefits and profits against all three standards can businesses meet societys expectations, earn their license to operate, and give capitalism a human face.<a href="#_edn37" id="_ednref37"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[37]</span></span></a>

Caution is clearly essential whenever any activity is contemplated or undertaken, and measures should always be taken to protect health and environmental values, reduce waste and pollution, increase energy efficiency, and safeguard future needs while meeting todays. However, these common-sense practices are not what is meant by this highly politicized terminology.

Inherent in these precepts is a conviction that corporate activities especially those of large multinational companies result in resource depletion, environmental degradation, impaired human and societal health, social harm and imminent planetary disaster. Also inherent is the belief that the doctrines of sustainable development, precautionary decision-making and Corporate Social Responsibility are well understood, enjoy nearly global recognition and acceptance, and alone can prevent the disastrous future foreseen by eco catastrophists.

- There is certainly a superficial allure to CSR reinforced by frequent stories and reports promoting the concepts and denigrating critics. However, neither the terminology nor its constant repetition represents a groundswell of actual public support; makes specific actions or policies truly ethical, responsible, cautious or sustainable; or obviates fundamental problems with these precepts.

<span style="font-size: 11.5pt; font-family: Symbol"><span style="font:7.0pt &quot;Times New Roman&quot;">         </span></span><span style="font-size: 11.5pt">While they are altruistic sounding, no one can explain what they really mean, or </span>what they imply in practice for corporate behavior.

<span style="font-size: 11.5pt; font-family: Symbol"><span style="font:7.0pt &quot;Times New Roman&quot;">         </span></span>The concepts primarily reflect the aesthetic and governance preferences of a small cadre of bureaucrats, multinational NGOs and wealthy foundations in developed countries.

- These CSR advocates have little understanding of (and often harbor a deep distaste for) capitalism, market economies, global trade or the role of profits. Yet, it is they who proclaim and implement the criteria by which businesses are judged, decide which of societys goals are important, and determine whether those goals are being met while largely ignoring the needs, views and concerns of poor people. They also employ pressure tactics to harass and intimidate companies, and bring them in line with their thinking.

<span style="font-size: 11.5pt; font-family: Symbol"><span style="font:7.0pt &quot;Times New Roman&quot;">         </span></span><span style="font-size: 11.5pt">Actually implementing the doctrines requires significant centralized control of land and energy use, economic production and consumption, </span>corporate innovation and initiative,<span style="font-size: 11.5pt"> markets, transportation, labor, trade, housing, policy making processes and peoples daily lives. Under the activists agenda, control would be monitored and enforced through the United Nations and global NGOs. This is the antithesis of the private property rights, capitalism, and freedom of nations, communities, companies and individuals to make their own decisions and thereby generate prosperity, human health and environmental quality.<a href="#_edn38" id="_ednref38"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 11.5pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[38]</span></span></a> </span>

<span style="font-size: 11.5pt; font-family: Symbol"><span style="font:7.0pt &quot;Times New Roman&quot;">         </span></span><span style="font-size: 11.5pt">Corporate Social Responsibility thus stands in direct opposition to the systems that have brought the greatest wealth, opportunities, technological advancements, and health and environmental improvements. </span>Its real effect is to reduce competition, innovation, trade, investment and economic vitality while impairing economic development and thus social and environmental improvement in communities worldwide.

<span style="font-family: Symbol"><span style="font:7.0pt &quot;Times New Roman&quot;">        </span></span>Many companies pledge allegiance to the concepts in bids to improve their image, gain greater public acceptance, blunt or forestall activist attacks, or gain a competitive edge.

<span style="font-family: Symbol"><span style="font:7.0pt &quot;Times New Roman&quot;">        </span></span>Countries often utilize them as legally or politically acceptable methods (hidden tariffs) for keeping foreign goods out and protecting domestic businesses and interests. 

- The supposedly equal emphasis on all three components of the so-called triple bottom line is often skewed so that environmental considerations trump all others, even in the case where peoples lives are put at risk (as in the case of DDT bans and malaria).
- CSR, SD and the PP are rooted too much in conjectural problems and theoretical needs of future generations and too little in real, immediate, life-and-death needs of present generations, <span style="font-size: 11.5pt"> especially billions of poor rural people in developing countries</span>. Even members of elite political classes in developing countries collaborate closely with NGOs and the UN, and pay little attention to the basic needs of their own rural poor.
- The environmental activists notion of sustainable development focuses too little on fostering economic development, and too much on *restricting* development typically in the name of protecting the environment. It also reflects their erroneous doctrine that we are rapidly depleting our natural resources and destroying the planet. The putative welfare of ecosystems again trumps even the most obvious welfare of people.
- Their precautionary principle is typically applied in only one direction. It promotes agendas set by activists in developed countries, while ignoring countervailing interests and needs of developing nations: *eg*, creating economic opportunity, alleviating poverty and disease, ensuring adequate and reliable supplies of affordable energy, and giving people the right to protect their families against killer diseases. It gives the CSR, SD and PP precepts credit for any potential public health and environmental risks they might reduce, but imposes no discredit for risks, injuries or deaths that they might generate.<span class="MsoEndnoteReference"> <a href="#_edn39" id="_ednref39"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black"> [39]</span></a></span> 

<span style="font-size: 11.5pt; font-family: Symbol"><span style="font:7.0pt &quot;Times New Roman&quot;">         </span></span><span style="font-size: 11.5pt">Under these principles, science is replaced by rhetoric, and vital questions go unasked: Is the risk real? Do the benefits outweigh the risks? Will the radical policy proposals improve poor peoples lives or result in more poverty, </span>misery, disease and death<span style="font-size: 11.5pt">? </span>

- CSR thus ignores the legitimate aspirations and needs of people who have not yet shared the dreams and successes of the developed world. To suggest that this doctrine represents progress, environmental justice or ethical, moral, responsible behavior is absurd. 

## <span style="font-size: 14.0pt; color: #333399">Energy sustainable, abundant and affordable  </span>

I would promote solar and wind for power, not damming more rivers, intones television actor Ed Begley, Jr. Weve dammed most of the rivers in the nation. The two most abundant forms of power on earth are solar and wind, and they're getting cheaper and cheaper. Its much cheaper for everybody \[in Africa\] to have electricity where they need it, on their huts.<a href="#_edn40" id="_ednref40"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[40]</span></span></a> His views, however sincere, strike many as paternalistic and worse.

Environmentalism, couched in difficult-to-combat superficial imagery, has taken a sinister turn. Now highly politicized, it has a gross disregard for the \[positive\] impact that the energy industry has on the world economy. Using moralistic, yet blatantly dishonest slogans and pseudo-science, the environmental movement has digressed dangerously and has replaced some of the most radical movements for social experimentation of the century.<a href="#_edn41" id="_ednref41"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[41]</span></span></a>

Electricity and energy are power economic and political power. The Third Worlds poor increasingly want to trade their huts for real homes, choose their own destinies, modernize and improve their lives, enjoy some of the other benefits taken for granted by westerners (and by intellectual and government elites in their own countries), and see their children live into adulthood and enjoy even better lives.

They resent having their choices dictated by First World environmental activists, under the guise of sustainable development and Corporate Social Responsibility. As one Gujarati Indian woman told a television news crew, We dont want to be encased like a museum,<a href="#_edn42" id="_ednref42"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[42]</span></span></a> in primitive lifestyles so romanticized by Hollywood and radical greens and so rife with desperate poverty, disease, malnutrition and premature death. Perhaps most of all, they bristle at the suggestion that they must never expect to enjoy the lifestyle of the average American or Westerner.<a href="#_edn43" id="_ednref43"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[43]</span></span></a> 

Barun Mitra, president of Liberty Institute of Delhi, India, presented the matter forcefully and succinctly in a recent commentary.

European governments, third-world bureaucrats, businesses such as The Body Shop and the European Wind Energy Association, and NGOs such as Greenpeace, have decided that renewable energy and clean development are the future for third world countries. But while the coalition focuses on preventing the hypothetical, long-run risk of climate change, \[it has\] conspicuously ignored the real risks that poor people face today. Indias economy needs reliable, cheap energy of any kind to fuel economic growth and improved quality of life.

The human health, economic, and environmental impact of burning these renewable fuels is immense. Young children and women spend hours each day in the drudgery of collecting firewood or \[squatting in mud laced with animal feces and urine, to collect, dry and store\] manure for use in cooking, heat, or light rather than attending school or engaging in more satisfying or productive economic activity. The refrigerators, televisions and computers that environmentalists take for granted are not to be seen here.

While environmentalists fret about the effects of air pollution caused by the burning of fossil fuels, the risk that poor people face is indoor air pollution. The World Health Organization says that indoor air pollution is linked to 4.3 million childhood deaths worldwide each year primarily from respiratory illnesses such as pneumonia. Burning biomass also contributes to asthma and lung cancer, particularly in women.

Likewise, the unsustainable cutting of firewood on marginal lands leads to erosion and environmental degradation. The burning of wood in urban areas contributes to air pollution. Reduced economic productivity, increased human suffering and loss of life, and negative environmental consequences all result from current reliance on renewable energy. For Indias poor rural people, efficient, reliable energy remains a dream rather than a reality.<a href="#_edn44" id="_ednref44"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[44]</span></span></a> 

Following a march against sustainable poverty at the Earth Summit in Johannesburg, Mitra observed that environmental activists romanticize poverty then fly to these eco summits in jet airplanes and stay in five-star hotels, talking about poverty and not giving options to the people who are actually poor to come out of poverty.<a href="#_edn45" id="_ednref45"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[45]</span></span></a>

In Indias Gujarat Province, the Narmada dam project ground to a halt, because Friends of the Earth and other eco-activists pressured international lending agencies to withdraw their financial support. In Uganda, where only 3 percent of the citizenry has access to electricity, work on the Bujagali dam has been halted, due to environmentalist pressure. What this really means, says Gordon Mwesigye, a senior official of Kampala, is that people will cut down our trees, because they dont have electricity, and the country will lose its wildlife habitats and the health and economic benefits that come from having abundant electricity. Air quality will also deteriorate, and the regions ecological balance will be increasingly jeopardized.

People will not just cut down trees. They will also die. The Narmada project alone would have provided electricity for 5,000 villages, renewable power for industry and water purification plants, irrigation water for crops, and safe drinking water for 35 million people. In a land where thousands of infants and children die every year from diarrhea, it is not surprising that local people have reacted angrily and some even view opposition to the dam as a crime against humanity.<a href="#_edn46" id="_ednref46"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[46]</span></span></a>

However, at the recent World Summit on Sustainable Development in Johannesburg, companies, environmental NGOs and government bureaucrats continued to promote small-scale wind and solar projects in developing countries, oppose hydroelectric and fossil fuel projects, and call for passage of the Kyoto Treaty on global climate change. And for this, they were deemed to be responsible, concerned about the poor, moral and passionate about the environment.

## <span style="font-size: 14.0pt; color: #333399">Malaria and DDT </span>

In 2000 alone, say World Health Organization and other studies, malaria infected over 300 million people. It killed nearly 2,000,000 most of them in sub-Saharan Africa, and over half of them children. Since 1972, over 50 million people have died from this dread disease.<a href="#_edn47" id="_ednref47"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[47]</span></span></a>

In addition to thousands of needless deaths, malaria also saps economies and health care resources. It keeps millions home from work and school every day, and chronic anemia can sap peoples strength for years, while cerebral malaria can cause lifelong learning and memory problems. The disease drains the Indian economy of up to \$345,000,000 every year, in lost wages due to deaths and absence from work, reduced productivity due to fatigue, and money spent on insecticides, medicines and malaria research.<a href="#_edn48" id="_ednref48"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[48]</span></span></a>

Africas gross national product would be \$400 billion a year instead of its current \$300 billion annually if malaria had been wiped out in 1965, when it was eliminated in most of the developed world. Malaria control costs Africa \$12 billion annually, depleting budgets for other health, environmental, economic and social programs. It particularly afflicts poor families, who have to use up to 25% or more of their income on prevention and treatment.<a href="#_edn49" id="_ednref49"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[49]</span></span></a>

These are real deaths and real impacts not just theoretical deaths, based on extrapolations from rodent studies (as in the case of Alar<a href="#_edn50" id="_ednref50"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[50]</span></span></a>), or hypothetical impacts (like flood and drought scenarios generated by certain climate change computer models).

They are due in large part to the near-global ban on the production, export and use of DDT. Originally imposed in the United States by EPA Administrator William Ruckelshaus in 1972,<a href="#_edn51" id="_ednref51"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[51]</span></span></a> the DDT ban has been expanded and enforced by NGO pressure, coercive treaties, and threats of economic sanctions by foundations, nations and international aid agencies.

Where DDT is used, malaria deaths plummet. Where it is not used, they skyrocket. In South Africa, for example, the incidence of malaria had been kept very low (below 10,000 cases annually) by the careful use of DDT. But in 1996 environmentalist pressure forced the program to cease using DDT, and one of the worst epidemics in the countrys history ensued, with almost 62,000 cases in 2000.  Shortly after this peak, DDT was reintroduced and in one year malaria cases were reduced by 80 percent; in two years they were almost back to the 10,000 cases per annum level. Similar experiences have been recorded in Zambia, other African countries, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh and elsewhere.<a href="#_edn52" id="_ednref52"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[52]</span></span></a>

No other chemical comes close to DDT as an affordable, effective way to *repel* mosquitoes from homes, *exterminate* any that land on walls, and *disorient* them, largely eliminating their urge to bite in homes that have been treated once or twice a year with tiny amounts of this miracle insecticide. (And affordability is a critical consideration in these impoverished countries.)

DDT is not a panacea, nor a super weapon that can replace all others; nor is it suitable in all situations. However, it is a vital weapon often the best available technology in a war that must be fought against a number of mosquito species (vectors) and malaria parasites, in different terrains and cultures, and under a wide variety of housing and other conditions. Like any army, healthcare workers need to have access to every available weapon. To saddle them with one-size-fits-all solutions (swords and pistols, bed nets and drug therapies) is unconscionable.

The chemical is no longer used in agriculture (which accounted for 99 percent of its use at the time Rachel Carson wrote *Silent Spring*). Today it is used almost entirely, and very selectively, in malaria control, via spraying in tiny quantities on the insides of houses.

It is not carcinogenic or harmful to humans; used in accord with these modern practices, it is safe for the environment; and malaria-carrying mosquitoes are far less likely to build immunities to DDT than to other pesticides that environmentalists and US, EU and UN agencies tolerate only as a last resort. In fact, notes Richard Tren, president of Africa Fighting Malaria, in the 60 years since DDT was first introduced, not a single scientific paper has been able to replicate even one case of actual human harm from its use.<a href="#_edn53" id="_ednref53"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[53]</span></span></a>

Nevertheless, the United Nations Environmental Programme (UNEP), Greenpeace, the Pesticide Action Network and World Wildlife Fund, Physicians for Social Responsibility and other groups remain adamantly opposed to the use of DDT a stance that angers many who must live with the consequences of malaria every day. The UNEP advocates using insecticide-treated mosquito nets, which do help at night, expensive drug therapies, and fish that eat mosquito larvae (a haphazard approach, at best) but not pesticides, and certainly not DDT.

A principal argument against DDT is that its use is not sustainable. This claim has frequently been made by Gro Harlem Brundtland, who was instrumental in promoting the sustainable development concept when she was Norways prime minister and has headed the World Health Organization since 1997, when it adopted its current anti-pesticide stance. However, without DDT, the lives of millions in developing countries are certainly not sustainable. 

<span style="font-weight:normal">Dr. Donald Roberts, Professor of Tropical Public Health at the Uniformed Services University of Health Sciences, is outraged at the high pressure tactics that have forced many countries to abandon public-health uses of DDT and see their disease and death tolls soar. He is not alone. </span>

If we dont use DDT, the results will be measured in loss of life, David Nabarro, director of Roll Back Malaria, says bluntly. The cost of the alternatives tend to run six times that of DDT.<a href="#_edn54" id="_ednref54"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[54]</span></span></a> That fact, however, appears to be irrelevant to many activist groups and aid agencies.

The U.S. Agency for International Development will not fund any indoor residual spraying and neither will most of the other donors, Tren says. This means that most African countries have to use whatever \[these donors\] are willing to fund (bed nets), which may not be the most appropriate tool. Belize and Bolivia have both admitted that they stopped using DDT in the face of USAID pressure, and many other developing countries refrain from using DDT because they don't want to damage their chances of exporting agricultural produce to the North.<a href="#_edn55" id="_ednref55"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[55]</span></span></a> 

Indias Department of Trade and Industry worries that the countrys agricultural produce will be turned away from Europe if any traces of DDT are found, Tren notes. And in a truly bizarre example of misplaced priorities and concerns, Zimbabwes department of health was forced to stop using DDT because tobacco growers were worried that their (carcinogenic) tobacco would get rejected by the EU if any DDT were found on it.

Ugandan Health Minister Brig Jim Muhwezi summed the matter up succinctly, when he recently announced that his country would begin using DDT to control mosquitoes. Uganda did so despite warnings by environmentalists and the European Union that it risks having its fish and agricultural exports banned in EU and other foreign markets, if it goes forward with its plan.

In Muhwezis view, the cost of treating malaria and the burden it has placed on the country outweighed any environmental repercussions. He cited the successful use of DDT in Mauritius and South Africa to slash malaria disease and death rates and said, Our peoples lives are of primary importance. The West is concerned about the environment because we share it with them. But it is not concerned about malaria because it is not a problem there.<span class="MsoEndnoteReference"> <a href="#_edn56" id="_ednref56"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[56]</span></a></span>

A prime example is 34-year-old designer and businesswoman Fiona Fifi Kobusingye, a friend of Cyril Boynes, Jr., director of international affairs for the Congress of Racial Equality (CORE). She saw her doctor in early November, because she felt fatigued and discovered she has malaria. Her year-old niece shivers and cries all night, and is suffering from kidney failure, because of malaria. Her sister is critically ill and hospitalized with malaria, and her mother came to Kampala to help tend everyone but ended up in the hospital herself with malaria.

The United States and Europe successfully used DDT to all but eradicate malaria. For them to downplay the impact of this disease on developing nations while worrying incessantly about largely theoretical health and mortality problems from trace amounts of chemicals in food and drinking water strikes Nabarro, Tren and Muhwezi as hypocritical, paternalistic and callous.

New insecticides and drugs are clearly needed. However their development and use are hampered by insufficient funding, excessive reliance on the precautionary principle (particularly in Europe) and the ever-present threat of multi-billion-dollar liability judgments (especially in the United States). Even if they might someday be a reliable substitute for DDT, tens of millions are likely to die in the meantime. The false alternatives are little more than wishful thinking in its deadliest form promoted by people who have staked out an ideological position against DDT anywhere, anytime and under any circumstances, and cling to their position like limpets to a rock.<a href="#_edn57" id="_ednref57"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[57]</span></span></a>

The DDT ban and intense pressure on countries not to use it are a lethal form of eco-colonialism, imposed by nations that eradicated malaria, dengue fever and typhus decades ago, against nations that continue to be devastated by these deadly diseases. They are also a grotesque abuse of CSR and the precautionary principle akin to telling terminally ill cancer patients they may not use morphine to ease their pain, because you wouldnt use addictive drugs when youre healthy.

And yet, for their intense opposition to DDT use and their blatant lack of concern for people companies, NGOs, Hollywood celebrities, foundations and government bureaucrats are deemed to be responsible, concerned about the poor, moral and passionate about the environment.

## <span style="font-size: 14.0pt; color: #333399">Starvation and biotechnology </span>

<span lang="EN-GB">Green groups and European bureaucrats may not be conspiring to starve millions of sub-Saharan Africans, but they may as well be, suggested Andrew Natsios, director of the U.S. Agency for International Development. He accused environmental groups of endangering the lives of 14 million people who face starvation in southern Africa, which is enduring its worst drought in a decade, by encouraging their governments to reject genetically modified U.S. food aid. </span>

<span lang="EN-GB">They can play these games with Europeans, who have full stomachs, but it is revolting and despicable to see them do so when the lives of Africans are at stake, he said. <a href="#_edn58" id="_ednref58"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"> <span lang="EN-GB" style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[58]</span></span></a> </span>

<span lang="EN-GB">Nearly 2.5 million are on the verge of starvation in Zambia alone, where President Levy Mwanawasa has caved in to NGO and EU pressure, and refused to accept food aid from the United States. </span>

<span lang="EN-GB">The U.S. has shipped 17,000 tons of corn to Zambia, where many are down to one small meal per day, only to have the grain sit in storage. Parroting the EU/Greenpeace line, Mwanawasa has decreed it is unsafe for consumption, because some of the corn has been genetically modified, to make it more resistant to drought, salt and insect pests, reduce the need for pesticides, and save wildlife habitat by enabling farmers to grow more food on less land. <span lang="EN-GB">The fact that Americans have been consuming this corn for years has not altered their intense opposition nor have repeated scientific studies concluding that biotech foods are safe to eat nor demands by the starving Africans, who have attempted to break into the warehouses. <span lang="EN-GB">The green brigade, which likes to buttress its political opposition to GM foods with junk science, is cheering Zambias intransigence. And the willingness of Greenpeace, Friends of the Earth and the like to let Africans starve in the name of someone elses ideology is remarkable enough.</span>

<span lang="EN-GB">But the Europeans are also blameworthy. Zambia is just as worried about upsetting trade relations with Europe, its biggest export market. The European Union bans most GM crops lest they upset Europe's heavily subsidized farm system and Mr. Mwanawasa's concern is that the U.S. corn will cross-pollinate with non-GM varieties and taint future yields \[thus triggering EU bans on crops from Zambia and neighboring countries\].</span>

The eco-lobby has targeted the Third World with a five-year, \$175 million campaign against GM foods. The Sierra Club is calling for a moratorium on the planting of all genetically engineered crops. Greenpeace says it opposes all releases of genetically engineered organisms into the environment, an act it calls genetic pollution.<a href="#_edn60" id="_ednref60"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[60]</span></span></a>

<span lang="EN-GB">\$175 million would feed millions of starving people for months. But of course, President Mwanawasa and his cronies are not going hungry. Nor are Mr. Mugabe and his cohorts in Zimbabwe. Meanwhile, the desperate masses continue to starve. </span>

<span lang="EN-GB">Indeed, even where malnourished Africans seek to plant disease- and drought-resistant GM crops only to feed themselves, these precautionary radicals still cry genetic pollution. <span lang="EN-GB">Ugandans eat 500 pounds of bananas per person every year more than any other people on earth. But their banana crops are being devastated by an airborne fungus that prevents photosynthesis and nematodes that destroy the roots. Scientists have already developed a bio-engineered solution for the fungus, and are working on the nematode problem. However, implementation of these crop-saving technologies remains snarled in a bureaucratic nightmare that insists Uganda must first construct a legal framework (acceptable to GM-phobic Europeans) to prove beyond any doubt that the non-traditional plant breeding techniques will pose no environmental risks. </span>

<span lang="EN-GB">If I want to eat a biotech banana here, the U.S. shouldnt care, and Europe shouldnt care, says C.F. Mugoya, associate executive secretary of Ugandas National Council for Science and Technology. If science offers us a solution, we should go for it if we want. </span>

<span lang="EN-GB">Unfortunately, if you say biotech here, all hell breaks loose, bemoans John Aluma, deputy director general of the countrys National Agricultural Research Organization. Adds W.K. But we Africans dont.<a href="#_edn61" id="_ednref61"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span lang="EN-GB" style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[61]</span></span></a> </span>

<span lang="EN-GB">But these harsh realities likewise have had no effect on anti-GMO radicals or on the politicians and companies that are too timid or uncaring to speak out for more thoughtful approaches to these life-and-death concerns. Indeed, for their intense opposition to GMO seeds and crops and their callous disregard for Africas starving multitudes companies, NGOs, European bureaucrats and Hollywood celebrities are frequently lionized as responsible, compassionate, morally superior and passionate about the environment.<a href="#_edn64" id="_ednref64"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span lang="EN-GB" style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[64]</span></span></a> </span>

## <span style="font-size: 14.0pt; color: #333399">International trade </span>

Corporate Social Responsibility tenets also demand that uniform rules apply across international borders for the transport of industrial materials and the wages paid by multinational companies (MNCs) in their domestic and overseas factories. Rarely is any consideration given to the perverse consequences that these policies have on ordinary people in developing nations.

This notion of a level playing field, however, is often little more than a clever subterfuge by developed countries to protect their own industries, by imposing hidden tariffs on imported goods, under the guise of safeguarding environmental quality, workers health and human rights, preventing sweatshop conditions in overseas plants, or demanding living wages. The result is the elimination of economic opportunities for the Third Worlds poor, and developing countries single most important competitive advantage: low cost labor.<span class="MsoEndnoteReference"> <a href="#_edn65" id="_ednref65"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[65]</span></a></span>

In many cases, cries for US or EU standards often harm those whom the self-proclaimed champions of the worlds poor purport to represent. It may well be that wages and working conditions in foreign countries are far below what would be tolerated in America or Europe. However, they are frequently better than the alternatives: no jobs, worse jobs, malnutrition and, for young girls, prostitution (if thats what it takes to put food on the family table). In many cases, they are also higher than wages paid to teachers, and even doctors, in those countries.

The machinations of special-interest groups in this country may work well to protect domestic job security. But they also prevent workers in developing countries from finding better opportunities to raise their living standards. If MNCs are forced to pay US wages in countries where job skills, productivity, and local economic and labor-market conditions cannot justify such wages, there would be no production cost savings and hence no purpose in relocating to those countries in the first place. Their poverty-stricken people would then lose the jobs and other benefits of direct foreign investment, including improved local economic conditions, a chance to develop their technical skills, and better roads, schools, hospitals and sanitary facilities near the new factories (many of them built by the MNCs).<a href="#_edn66" id="_ednref66"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[66]</span></span></a>

CSR and the precautionary principle also have perverse unintended consequences in the area of waste recycling. The low value of labor in developing nations justifies the long hours spent extracting material and recycling metals and chemicals. Free trade in waste would allow the comparative advantage of societies to make waste processing economically viable and efficient. However, recently enacted treaties and rules thwart this process.<a href="#_edn67" id="_ednref67"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[67]</span></span></a>

The Basel Convention on the Transboundary Movement of Hazardous Wastes prompted European companies to stop exporting chemicals, lead and zinc and India to stop importing them. Indias low consumption of automotive batteries and other products has thus meant that its own industrial and consumer markets are no longer able to support recycling plants in the absence of imported raw materials. Plants have closed, jobs have disappeared, and Indias ability to remove chemical and metallic contaminants from its environment has been drastically curtailed.

Activist demands for comparable wages and working conditions are now threatening another major Indian industry: the dismantling of huge ocean-going vessels to recycle their steel and other metals. Trade creates wealth, and if free trade, including trade in waste, is allowed greater productivity and more advanced technology will ensure \[greater recycling rates,\] reasonably priced disposal of waste that cannot be traded, and better standards of living for millions of people in Third World nations.<a href="#_edn68" id="_ednref68"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[68]</span></span></a>

Restrictions on free trade mean that fundamental human rights are glossed over or ignored. Those basic freedoms include the right to jobs, adequate food, better health and economic conditions, and priorities set by sovereign nations and communities, without being second-guessed or dictated to by bureaucrats and NGOs from developed countries.

## <span style="font-size: 14.0pt; color: #333399">Shareholder activists and stakeholders </span>

CSR proponents often speak of stakeholders most of them well-funded activists and NGOs that piously assert a right to participate in corporate and government decision-making. In some cases, they are politicians, bureaucrats and other elites in developing countries. They may also be shareholder activists, who may own substantial shares in a company or just enough to qualify them to introduce resolutions at annual meetings, demanding that the company adopt their positions and agendas on Corporate Social Responsibility, sustainable development, global warming, the precautionary principle or human rights.

The final 2000 report of the World Business Council for Sustainable Development (WBCSD) asserts that the essence of Corporate Social Responsibility is to recognize the value of external stakeholder dialogue.<a href="#_edn69" id="_ednref69"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[69]</span></span></a>

But who elected them stakeholders? Who made their narrow definitions and agendas the arbiter of what is moral or in the broader public interest? Who gave them the authority to exclude other stakeholders from debates and decision-making processes entire nations and billions of mostly destitute people, who are being denied the benefits of global trade, economic development, centralized energy projects, and wise use of resources, pesticides and biotechnology?

Pension fund investors and other ethical investor groups, we are told, want to retire into a clean, civil and safe world.<a href="#_edn70" id="_ednref70"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[70]</span></span></a> They therefore pressure companies to appease activist NGOs and meet acceptable standards of sustainable development and social responsibility.

Their commitment to Corporate Social Responsibility and its tenets may bring about a cleaner, safer, more civil world *for them*. But what about for the poorest citizens of Africa, Asia and Latin America? Or even the poorest citizens of the United States, Europe and Australia?

A moral vacuum surrounds this arrogation of power. Businesses and elected government officials ought to take a leadership position on these issues or at least question the underlying premises of CSR, as currently defined, and contest the demands of anti-business activists. Instead, many pursue a strategy of appeasement and accommodation, and cede moral authority to unelected NGOs, bureaucrats, ethical investors and other activists. Some have actually endorsed the activists demands and collaborated closely with them.

But for this they are praised as moral, responsible and concerned about the worlds poor.

As Charles Schwab has noted, trust in business will be restored only when companies accept three fundamental principles: transparency, disclosure and accountability.<a href="#_edn71" id="_ednref71"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[71]</span></span></a> But what about these unelected power brokers?

They often oppose economic development and opportunity, and the NGOs are exempt from false advertising and other laws (including the Lanham Act) that govern for-profit corporations. They likewise are not held to basic standards for disclosure and financial transparency, give little consideration to needs and desires that may be in conflict with their own, and are not made accountable for the adverse consequences of agendas they promote or impose.

Perhaps it is time that NGOs, activist stakeholders, and government bureaucrats themselves do what they demand of business: demonstrate that they can behave ethically and responsibly in return for the freedoms and opportunities that society bestows upon them.<a href="#_edn72" id="_ednref72"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[72]</span></span></a> 

## <span style="font-size: 14.0pt; color: #333399">Global climate change </span>

The very definition of CSR to say nothing of its actual implementation clearly raises an entire new genre of far-reaching concerns, not just for businesses, but for families, communities and nations. The worlds poorest citizens are especially vulnerable.

How corporations respond to the challenge will play a critical role in determining whether our future will be shaped by science, technology, freedom and hope or driven by fallacies, deception, despotism and fear. Many of the omens are not overly optimistic. 

In May 1997, British Petroleum CEO John Browne endorsed the catastrophic global warming hypothesis and said tough measures should be taken quickly, even without scientific proof. Many interpreted BPs support for the precautionary principle as evidence that the company was convinced about the science and had decided to become a responsible citizen.<a href="#_edn73" id="_ednref73"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[73]</span></span></a> Indeed, Sir John later said, We accepted that the risks were serious and that precautionary action was justified. We were the first company in our industry to do so, and the first to say that, if we were asking other people to take precautionary action, we had to show what was possible and to set an example.<a href="#_edn74" id="_ednref74"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[74]</span></span></a>

Under the leadership of chairman Sir Mark Moody-Stuart, Royal Dutch Shell likewise played a lead role in promoting CSR, sustainable development, the precautionary principle and the Kyoto Protocol. The company issued a series of reports on people, planet and profits, offering guidance on the meaning and purpose of CSR, the nature of the corporate commitment it requires, and how companies should actually implement its doctrines.<a href="#_edn75" id="_ednref75"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[75]</span></span></a> 

Rarely, if ever, have these companies or any other members of the World Business Council for Sustainable Development so much as suggested that NGO opinions or CSR assumptions might be ill-informed, misleading, false or even open to question. Never have they opined that their narrow definition of CSR could actually be harmful to millions of people.

Indeed, BP, Shell and other WBC members provide significant financial and political support for activists, politicians and regulators, to further promote the CSR agenda. They are also strong proponents of the notion that CSR principles are widely accepted and irrefutable permitting no disagreement or deviation.

According to this line of thinking, neither companies, communities nor countries have a right to adopt their own definition of Corporate Social Responsibility, sustainable development or the precautionary principle to fit their own unique circumstances, or their own pressing needs and priorities. To permit this would tilt the playing field, give some an unfair advantage, and result in irresponsible or unethical practices.

However, in the realm of CSR and the race to appear green, some have suggested that BP is in a class by itself surpassing even Enron in seeking to profit from emission trading, a largely coercive switch from coal to natural gas in electrical power generation, and other policies designed to combat catastrophic global climate change.<a href="#_edn76" id="_ednref76"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[76]</span></span></a>

Columnist Robert Novak brought to light several internal Enron memos that outlined in detail how the company could parlay the Kyoto treaty into immensely profitable business endeavors. <span style="font-family: Times">A December 12, 1997 memo claimed Enron had excellent credentials with many radical environmental groups which, the memo claimed, referred to Enron in glowing terms. It asserted that the Kyoto treaty would do more to promote Enron's business than any other initiative and said the treatys emissions trading authority would be good for Enron stock!! </span>

An August 4, 1997 memo to Enron president Ken Lay sought to prepare him for a White House meeting on how to persuade the United States to embrace the climate treaty. Present at the meeting were President Clinton, Vice President Gore and BP CEO Sir John Brown. The memo described BP as Enrons international equivalent, presumably because of the UK giants global reach, similar policy objectives and equally stellar reputation among environmental groups.<a href="#_edn77" id="_ednref77"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[77]</span></span></a>

<span style="font-family: Symbol"><span style="font:7.0pt &quot;Times New Roman&quot;">        </span></span>The Kyoto climate treaty would compel signatory nations to slash fossil fuel use and greenhouse gas emissions. But Enron, BP and others believe they can create and run a complex system that allows companies and nations to trade (sell) credits for energy use foregone. Credits that now sell for a few cents or couple dollars per ton of emissions might go for \$10, \$20 or \$100 a ton under a mandatory Kyoto program generating tremendous returns on investment, and tremendous incentive for these socially responsible companies to promote vigorous action to combat catastrophic global warming.<a href="#_edn78" id="_ednref78"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[78]</span></span></a>

<span style="font-family: Symbol"><span style="font:7.0pt &quot;Times New Roman&quot;">        </span></span>The Kyoto accord would slash Americas fossil fuel use to some 40 percent below what it would otherwise be by 2012. The International Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) has suggested that, to stabilize greenhouse gases, global emissions must be reduced to at least 60 percent below *1990* levels, even as emissions rise in developing countries that are not subject to the treatys requirements.

<span style="font-family: Symbol"><span style="font:7.0pt &quot;Times New Roman&quot;">        </span></span>The U.S. Energy Information Administration has calculated that the treaty could drain over \$300 billion a year from a U.S. economy that drives one-third of the entire global economy further harming American citizens, and making it even more difficult for developing countries to climb out of poverty, disease and despair. The climate treatys impact on Europe could be similarly devastating, further harming developing nations.

<span style="font-family: Symbol"><span style="font:7.0pt &quot;Times New Roman&quot;">        </span></span>Even worse, the misery would bring few benefits. According to the National Center for Atmospheric Research, even full and perfect compliance with Kyoto would reduce average global temperatures in 2050 to only 0.13<span style="font-family: Symbol"></span>F below they would be in the treatys absence. Actually stabilizing greenhouse gases at current concentrations would require 19 Kyotos and send our use of fossil fuels tumbling to Depression Era levels.<a href="#_edn79" id="_ednref79"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[79]</span></span></a>

**<span style="font-size: 14.0pt">The profit motive.</span>** At least some of this supposedly principled, altruistic, moral concern about global warming on the part of BP, Shell and other companies is likely to be actually motivated by a crass desire to develop new revenue streams and maximize profits. This is apparent from the Enron and BP emission trading activities, and from statements made at a recent oil industry conference on global climate change.

In response to questions after his formal address, Dr. Stephen Pecala, a Princeton University researcher working under a \$20-million BP-funded grant, suggested that oil companies should embrace the Kyoto Protocol and global climate change theories, because they could raise their per-barrel prices through a self-imposed energy tax. The tax would promote greenhouse gas sequestration and would allow companies to reap the *full* benefits of oil development, he said. 

In other words, energy and other companies that play the climate change game according to the Kyoto rules could earn billions of dollars in profits via traditional steps of petroleum extraction, refining and sales and then earn billions more by disposing of the resultant carbon via emission trading and sequestration schemes.<a href="#_edn80" id="_ednref80"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[80]</span></span></a>

**<span style="font-size: 14.0pt">The power and influence motive.</span>** Another factor driving the climate treaty is Europes clear desire to protect its industries, preserve its massive welfare state, and continue its efforts to integrate the economic basket case it inherited when the Soviet Union collapsed and the Eastern Bloc states again sought to be aligned with Western Europe. It is also a matter of Europeans chafing under American wealth, power and influence that once were theirs, and that they believe should rightfully still be theirs. And it reflects a prevailing green and EU mindset that Americans are crass, greedy, wasteful and wedded to profligate resource consumption.

For nearly two decades, Europes growth rate has been about half that of the United States, while unemployment has been nearly twice the U.S. rate, and tax burdens in some EU countries is 40 percent or more of their Gross Domestic Product. Europeans seem to prefer high levels of job security, unemployment and welfare while Americans opt for lower levels, as a tradeoff for greater growth, innovation, living standards, and flexibility in changing jobs and cities. But now the Europeans want to impose their choices as the worlds norm.

The EUs self-interest is highly visible in its insistence that Ireland, the United States and other nations adopt a system of global tax equity or tax harmonization, under which the U.S. would raise its taxes to EU levels, to prevent disruption and eliminate unfair and harmful tax competition.<a href="#_edn81" id="_ednref81"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[81]</span></span></a> It is also apparent in EU support for the Basel Convention on Transboundary Movement of Hazardous Materials (to protect chemical companies and jobs) and its intense opposition to GMO foods (to protect its agricultural base).

One suspects that the Europeans and greens opposition to biotechnology might also be related to their insistence that catastrophic climate change is behind every weather oddity and rain, drought or storm cycle. Although droughts are a centuries-old problem in southern Africa, if the latest ones can be tied to global warming, that might deflect some of the blame they deserve for their unbending antipathy toward GM crops that grow well in saline soils and dry climates.

Several major companies and activist groups (including BP, Vivendi, Greenpeace, Friends of the Earth and the World Wildlife Fund) were members of the *official* European Commission delegation to the August 2002 Earth Summit in Johannesburg, South Africa! They and Europe in general see the Kyoto Protocol, not merely as an environmental program, but also as a trade issue a way to pressure the United States to reduce its energy use and economic productivity, and thereby bring itself down to the level of European nations that refuse to adjust their industrial, labor, tax and welfare policies.

EU Environment Commissioner Margot Wallstrom made precisely this point in response to President Bushs decision to abandon the Kyoto climate treaty. This is not a simple environmental issue, where you can say it is an issue where the scientists are not unanimous, she declaimed. This is about international relations, this is about economy, about trying to create a level playing field for big businesses throughout the world. You have to understand what is at stake and that is why it is serious.<a href="#_edn82" id="_ednref82"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[82]</span></span></a>

Her remarks were echoed by several EU officials, who suggested that Americas lower energy taxes constitute an unfair trade advantage that could justify trade sanctions against the U.S.

The EU countries recognize that they cannot compete with the U.S. or developing countries as long as Europes tax rates, unemployment levels, welfare benefits and work disincentives remain high, their farms remain mired in traditional or organic practices, and many of their companies are reluctant to become lean and mean the way their American counterparts did in the 1980s and 1990s. It is far easier politically for the EU to criticize its competitors, and demand equitable arrangements under compulsory international treaties, than to admit and correct its own shortcomings and anti-competitive policies.

<span style="font-family: Times">The EU, </span> environmental activists and United Nations also envision the Kyoto treatys restrictions on energy supplies, energy use and greenhouse gas emissions as an opportunity to expand the authority of international institutions. Indeed, French President Jacques Chirac has termed the Kyoto Protocol the first component of authentic global governance. It would be, moreover, a massive bureaucracy, devoid of checks and balances, ensconced primarily in the EU and UN, fed and nourished with billions in tax dollars, and holding unprecedented power to control decisions by nations, states, communities, businesses and individuals over energy, economic, housing, transportation and numerous other matters.<a href="#_edn83" id="_ednref83"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[83]</span></span></a>

How moral, ethical or socially responsible these corporations are, for promoting their business interests via a climate treaty with such far-reaching impacts, is certainly open to question. How altruistic and socially responsible the EU countries are, for protecting their political and economic interests through the treatys hidden tariffs, is also questionable.

It would clearly seem far more responsible for BP, Shell, other companies and European nations to take a principled stand in defense of true science, and human life and welfare by

- revealing all the ways they and the various activist groups stand to profit from ratification of the Kyoto climate treaty; and
- publicly challenging radical environmental agendas, while actively supporting less hasty action on climate change, approving the use of DDT and GMO foods in Africa and Asia, and engaging in a full re-examination of CSR doctrines.

## <span style="font-size: 14.0pt; color: #333399">In search of true Corporate Social Responsibility </span>

From its very beginning, the doctrines of Corporate Social Responsibility have too narrowly defined what is moral, ethical, sustainable, ecologically beneficial, compassionate or in the public interest. From its inception, companies, journalists and politicians alike have treated the views and demands of CSR activists and other hostile critics of business as more rationally based, more representative of societys expectations, more morally sound, than they really are.

But under what standards by whose definition is it moral, ethical or socially responsible for them to take the positions they have on energy development, DDT, biotech foods, trade and global warming? How is it that the assertions, accusations and agendas or these radical elements have become the arbiter of corporate and societal responsibility?

Free markets and free people generate innovation, opportunity, optimism, prosperity, health and environmental quality. But CSR activists demand one vision, one mode of thought, one kind of ethical, economic and environmental policy. They permit no marketplace of ideas no deviation from politically correct thinking no right of people in different companies, communities, cultures and countries to think for themselves, make their own decisions, set their own priorities, address their own most pressing problems, decide their own destinies.

At best, this blind adherence to current CSR doctrine puts BP, Shell, the EU and the WBCSD in league with some of the most radical elements of Western society. At worst, it makes them guilty of silent complicity (or even active collaboration) in the misery and death of millions.

When will they demonstrate real leadership and reexamine the tenets of Corporate Social Responsibility, and their corporate stance on this radical doctrine?

When will they demonstrate that they truly care about people and not just about profiting from policies sanctioned by CSR doctrine, or burnishing their reputations among Euro, green, Hollywood and media elites?

If BP and Shell want to demonstrate real social responsibility and lead the way in truly making the world a better place for its poorest inhabitants, they need to challenge Europe and the radical environmentalists on energy, malaria, biotech foods, climate change and trade and insist that CSR, sustainable development and the precautionary principle reflect the hard realities noted here and by many other commentators.

 

C.S. Lewis may have put it best, when he observed:

 

 

**Of all tyrannies, a tyranny exercised for the good of its victims may be the most oppressive. It may be better to live under robber barons than under omnipotent moral busybodies. The robber baron's cruelty may sometimes sleep, his cupidity may at some point be satiated; but those who torment us for our own good will torment us without end, for they do so with the approval of their own conscience.<a href="#_edn84" id="_ednref84"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[84]</span></span></a>**

                                                  

 

<span style="font-size: 14.0pt; font-family: Arial; color: #333399">\
 </span>

<span style="font-size: 16.0pt">Greenwashing</span>

 

Is BP really greener than its fossil fuel competitors? Or do its actions and expenditures belie its public pronouncements and advertising hype? How do other oil companies stack up to BP in terms of energy efficiency, renewable energy, pollution control, greenhouse gas reduction and wildlife conservation? Who else ranks high on the list of greenwashers?

** **

**Greenwashing**, says the Concise Oxford English Dictionary, is disinformation disseminated by an organization so as to present an environmentally responsible public image. It perfectly describes **BP**s Beyond Petroleum ads and other public pronouncements, proclaiming that the company is much greener than its competitors. 

Solar, natural gas, hydrogen, wind. And oh yes, oil. Its a start. A minuscule one, perhaps, but enough to get the masters of spin and advertising hyperbole rolling along in a \$100-million-a-year, self-congratulatory ad campaign.

- In the midst of spending \$91 billion for its merger with Amoco and Arco, BP managed to scrape together \$200 million for a 6-year solar program that included installing solar panels on 200 of its 17,000 service stations. That works out to 99.8 percent on oil, and 0.2 percent on solar (and solar panels on 1 percent of its stations).<a href="#_edn85" id="_ednref85"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[85]</span></span></a>

<!-- -->

- Its average annual \$33 million solar investment also equals a stunning 0.4 percent of its \$8.5 billion 2001 petroleum expenditure 0.02 percent of its current net worth or a third of what it is spending each year on its Beyond Petroleum campaign.<a href="#_edn86" id="_ednref86"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[86]</span></span></a>
- BPs combined global generation of 77 MW of electricity from wind and solar is barely enough to keep Boise, Idahos lights lit for a year let alone make a dent in Californias electricity demand.<a href="#_edn87" id="_ednref87"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[87]</span></span></a> The tally of birds chopped up by its wind turbine rotors is unknown.

In contrast to its omnipresent beyond ads, BP is far more reticent when the conversation turns to drilling in Alaskas Arctic National Wildlife Refuge (ANWR). Yet, it is the biggest operator on the North Slope, with huge lease holdings, and would certainly consider producing oil there if given the chance, admits John Mogford, BP vice president for renewable and alternative energy.<a href="#_edn88" id="_ednref88"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[88]</span></span></a>

How could the company responsibly take any other position? Government geologists estimate that ANWR could hold 6-16 billion barrels of recoverable oil. Thats 11-30 years of imports from Saudi Arabia. Turned into gasoline, it would power Californias cars and trucks for 18-50 years. Midrange estimates put ANWRs natural gas potential at 4 trillion cubic feet enough to fuel Californias electrical generating plants, homes, schools and businesses for several years.

All this from impacted areas totaling less than 2,000 acres. Thats one-twentieth of Washington, DC or 20 of the buildings Boeing uses to manufacture its 747 jets\!<a href="#_edn89" id="_ednref89"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[89]</span></span></a>

Its no wonder the Eskimos who live in ANWR support oil development by an 8:1 margin and are outraged that Gwichin Indians continue to take money from environmental groups for opposing oil drilling in the regions flat, desolate coastal plain. Moreover, while the Gwichins hypocritically denounce drilling in an area 300 miles from their villages, they have leased every single acre of their own land and formed an oilfield services company to drill and develop oil prospects and build a pipeline along a major caribou migratory route.<a href="#_edn90" id="_ednref90"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[90]</span></span></a>

But mentioning these facts (especially in the midst of its ad campaign) could tarnish BPs greener-than-our-competitors image. So the company has quietly pulled out of the ANWR debate and the pro-drilling trade association, Arctic Power while keeping its onshore Alaska drilling options open and building an artificial drilling island in the Beaufort Sea off the Prudhoe Bay coast.

Meanwhile, BP was handed a maximum criminal penalty of \$500,000 by a U.S. District Court in 2000, for failing to report the illegal disposal of hazardous waste on Alaskas North Slope. The company was also ordered to pay \$15 million over the next five years to establish a nationwide environmental management system designed to prevent future violations in addition to the \$6.5 million that it agreed to pay in civil penalties.<a href="#_edn91" id="_ednref91"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[91]</span></span></a>

In January 2001, BP was assessed a \$10 million penalty and agreed to spend \$500 million modernizing its pollution-control equipment, for violating federal clean-air laws at eight U.S. refineries.

On the other hand, BP is clearly at the forefront of efforts to secure ratification of the Kyoto Protocol on global climate change. It may be that Sir John Browne sincerely believes the global warming theorists and computer models are right even in the absence of any satellite or other empirical evidence to corroborate them. However, one can justifiably suspect other motives.

One could be BPs substantial and growing stake in natural gas, which many tout as a key substitute for coal and oil, since it emits less carbon dioxide when burned. Ratification of the Kyoto accords would send the value of BPs gas holdings skyward, significantly improving its bottom line (the traditional one, as well as the fabled triple bottom line.)

<span lang="EN-GB">A second motivator might be emissions trading. <span style="color:black">Before it went into Chapter 11, Enron hoped to cash in on Kyoto by masterminding a worldwide trading network in which major industries could buy and sell credits to emit carbon dioxide.<a href="#_edn92" id="_ednref92"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span lang="EN-GB" style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[92]</span></span></a> </span>BP may now be in a position to replace Enron as the primary power broker for any greenhouse gas emissions trading scheme that may emerge from the climate change debates, as nations seek to find ways to comply with its draconian provisions, without decimating their industries and economies.<a href="#_edn93" id="_ednref93"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span lang="EN-GB" style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[93]</span></span></a> </span>

# Market Manipulation and Investor Fraud

 

Are some investment advisory firms using misleading, perhaps even fraudulent, tactics to denigrate certain companies and attract investors to competitor companies favored by the advisors, using promises and projections about future earnings that reflect dubious energy and environmental claims? Do cozy relationships between foundations, corporations, activists, and socially responsible investor groups and stock analysts further this strategy?

 

Investors lost billions in 2002, when the deceitful practices of Enron, Global Crossing, WorldCom, Arthur Anderson and other companies became public and their financial house of cards came crashing down. 

*Fortune, Business Week,* the *Washington Post* and *New York Times*, activists, citizens and politicians alike made impassioned pleas for prosecutions, new laws and tougher penalties. These actions, they said, were needed to curb widespread patterns of cooking the books, shading the truth, misleading statements and other distortions of facts and accounting figures, breaches of trust, abuses of power, unfair enrichment and even outright fraud.

Arrests followed, corporate crooks are being prosecuted, and Congress enacted new rules. In the process, many legislators assumed that the nature and scope of the problem was worse than it actually was, and wound up criminalizing even inadvertent or minor accounting errors.<a href="#_edn103" id="_ednref103"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[103]</span></span></a>

However, some dubious practices escaped notice, and continue today. The old Roman proverb is particularly relevant to this brand of deception: *Caveat emptor*. Let the buyer be equally aware of what is being pedaled as sound or ethical investments by politically correct organizations.

The hard reality is that some companies, investment advisors, foundations and activist groups still have not gotten the message. Sometimes, its because they wear three hats at one time.

- They are corporations even huge multinationals some for-profit, others not-for-profit.
- They may serve as financial advisors, urging consumers and investors to put their money into companies that they assert are ethical, green or socially responsible, and pull their money out of companies they portray as environmental or human rights Darth Vaders.
- They may also be social activists, vigorously promoting certain energy, economic and environmental agendas that reflect their ideologies and help fill their own coffers.

Perhaps now is the time to ensure that our new and rediscovered principles and standards are applied across the board not just to companies, but also to activist NGOs, analysts, advisors and foundations that appear to be engaging in Enronesque practices, to further their political, ideological and financial goals. The advice offered so freely for corporate reform in 2002 would be a good starting point for further house cleaning, to inspire government oversight and better enforcement of ethical principles for nonprofit organizations. Here is a sampling of that advice.

Companies should be required to state profits in a way that is more meaningful and less subject to manipulation. Even if companies want to tout random, unaudited, watch-me-pull-a-rabbit-out-of-my-hat figures in their press releases, investors should immediately be able to compare these figures with full financial statements prepared in accordance with Generally Accepted Accounting Principles.<a href="#_edn104" id="_ednref104"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[104]</span></span></a>

Accountants and auditors should not be permitted to merely sign off on clients financial statements. They should also have to grade the quality of their earnings.<a href="#_edn105" id="_ednref105"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[105]</span></span></a> Because they are in a unique position to judge how dependent the financial statements are on assumptions that could prove faulty, accountants and auditors also need to exercise much more skepticism and not conduct merely cursory reviews.<a href="#_edn106" id="_ednref106"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[106]</span></span></a>

To ensure research with integrity and avoid conflicts of interest, analysts should not be allowed to participate in investment-banking deals, or make recommendations on buying stocks while simultaneously selling their advice to the companies whose stocks they cover. They must disclose any actual and apparent conflicts of interest.<a href="#_edn107" id="_ednref107"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[107]</span></span></a> They must go beyond putting out glowing recommendations that are long on concepts and potentials, but short on facts and figures.<a href="#_edn108" id="_ednref108"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[108]</span></span></a> 

Companies should require: codes of ethics for their boards of directors, annual (public) performance evaluations of directors, and directors who are truly outsiders independent from the company, with no ties to the CEO, and no dependence on the company for their livelihoods.<a href="#_edn109" id="_ednref109"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[109]</span></span></a>

Companies need to replace CEOs who fail at moral leadership, accountants who engage in borderline accounting, and directors who fail to direct, have vested interests in maintaining systems that are beneficial to them, are asleep at the wheel, or dont want to rock the boat.<a href="#_edn110" id="_ednref110"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[110]</span></span></a>

Corporations should also strive to create cultures that encourage and reward integrity as much as entrepreneurship hire executives who are not only exemplary managers but also the moral compass for the company and establish a system of checks and balances that insures against companies crossing the ethical divide, by providing the CEO with a roomful of skeptics and actively encouraging dissent.<a href="#_edn111" id="_ednref111"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[111]</span></span></a>

Companies must no longer employ the arrogant, closed-culture approach. They cannot presume that they know what is best not only for the organizations, but for affected constituents. Nor can they assume they are immune from responsibility for their actions \[because they are\] too large, or too powerful, or too vital to a community to worry about accountability.<a href="#_edn112" id="_ednref112"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[112]</span></span></a>

And finally, from shareholder activist Robert Monks: Our legal system and companies should empower institutional owners to hold corporate managers accountable for their actions and the consequences thereof (especially in the social, environmental and human rights arenas), to ensure that corporate interests reflect the goals of society as a whole.<span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><a href="#_edn113" id="_ednref113"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[113]</span></a></span>

Curing corporate malfeasance, accounting fraud and conflicted advice can also be summed up in three core principles. **Transparency** conducting business and making decisions in the light of day, with integrity, honesty and input from investors, consumers and the institutions that serve them. **Disclosure** giving investors and the general public a clear picture of the biases and conflicts built into the advice they receive, and providing easy access to that information, so that they can make informed choices. **Accountability** at the senior-most executive levels, for actions and their consequences, under codes of conduct that require the CEO and chief compliance officer to certify that the proper controls are in place.<a href="#_edn114" id="_ednref114"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman; color: black">[114]</span></span></a> 

But will these basic principles now be applied in other arenas, where passivity and a too-narrow focus have led to numerous abuses that continue to cry out for reform?

** **

America has long prided itself on having a single legal system that applies equally to all citizens, does not permit selected groups to live by different standards, and imposes the same duties and penalties on all. But this system of equal justice for all has lost its bearings.

**In this critically important era when for-profit and not-for-profit corporations, activist groups, investment advisory firms, institutional investor funds and major foundations all exert enormous influence on our economy and the performance of individual companies our system allows these selected groups to play by very different rules.**

Not only are these groups exempt from false advertising, transparency, financial disclosure and accountability rules. But recent legislative and regulatory reforms apply primarily or only to the for-profit corporate sector, despite widespread patterns of abuse within these exempt sectors.

Having spent much of 2002 addressing corporate fraud, our watchdog groups, journalists, legislators, regulators and courts must now devote their attention to correcting abuses that have become prevalent in these other sectors. The principles noted above would provide excellent guidelines for long overdue reforms.

#### <span style="font-size: 16.0pt; font-family: Arial; color: #333399"> Johannesburg: End of the road for eco-imperialism?</span>

 

Some 60,000 activists, bureaucrats and politicians flew gas-guzzling, greenhouse-gas-spewing jets into Johannesburg, South Africa, last August for a \$50-million Earth Summit. Many settled into comfy five-star hotels, intending to make the summit a high water mark for sustainable development, global warming and the precautionary principle.

Over the next two weeks, they denounced everything from electricity and flush toilets, to fossil fuels, biotechnology, capitalism and, of course, the United States. The World Wildlife Fund issued a jeremiad, wailing that mankind will need at least 1.2 more Planet Earths by 2050 to maintain our present standard of living, if we dont change our evil ways.

However, despite their Herculean efforts, the summit didnt turn out quite the way they expected.

The United States, Australia and Canada refused to go along with the orchestrated agenda and, instead, promoted free markets, free trade and economic development as the solution to global poverty and environmental degradation. Secretary of State Collin Powell spoke in support of these themes and the three nations increasing alliance with the developing countries against the forces of alarmism and pessimism. USAID Director Andrew Natsios spoke out against NGO and EU demands that starving people be denied GMO corn.

Writing in Londons *Telegraph*, the director of South Africas Free Market Foundation had earlier accused the NGOs and EU of insidious eco-imperialism.

<span style="font-weight:normal">These latter-day imperialists are neo-Luddites who place elitist environmental whims and nebulous fears of resource depletion above the needs of the world's destitute billions. They seek to impose first-world conceptions of environmentalism and human development on developing countries. They do not want poor countries to follow the path that made the prosperity of their own countries possible.<a href="#_edn133" id="_ednref133"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[133]</span></span></a> </span>

<span style="font-weight:normal">The charge resonated. Hundreds of poor African protesters marched in Johannesburg against what they called the politics and policies of Sustained Poverty. Their growing anger and resentment toward green despotism was obvious. Barun Mitra of India presented a special BS Award to the international NGOs for sustaining poverty. It was a plaque heaped high with two piles of animal excrement symbolizing both the quality of the environmental radicals arguments and the biomass fuel that many NGOs seem to want poverty-stricken Third World families to use for generations to come.   </span>

<span style="font-weight:normal">In the end, many of the greens key agenda items were tabled, voted down or relegated to indeterminate timetables, and they were left to cry angrily in their white wine. The Energy and Climate Caucus coalition denounced the Earth summit as a total failure, Friends of the Earth called it notably feeble, and Oxfam International summed it up as nine days of bluster.<a href="#_edn134" id="_ednref134"><span class="MsoEndnoteReference"><span style="font-size: 12.0pt; font-family: Times New Roman">[134]</span></span></a> </span>

It is certainly dangerous to make any kind of prediction about the demise of an \$8-billion-a-year environmental gorilla. However, it is possible that Johannesburg will turn out to have been a watershed event in the history of eco extremism. 

At previous summits dating back to 1992 in Rio de Janeiro, discussions had focused on Malthusian limits to growth, planetary apocalypse and global governance. In Johannesburg, the focus was on poverty and on how poverty can be eliminated, and peoples health and environmental quality improved, through private property rights, market economies, global trade, technology, innovation, <span style="font-size: 11.5pt">democratic process</span>es, sustained economic growth, and the right of individuals, communities and nations to make their own decisions and chart their own destinies.

At previous summits, the dominant theme was the struggle between rich and poor between haves and have-nots between a greedy United States and an impoverished Third World. In Johannesburg, the struggle was largely between those whose vision is one of hope, progress and faith in mankinds amazing intellectual capacity against those whose vision is one of fear, worsening environmental degradation and man as the supreme danger to all he surveys. 

Whether this change in priorities and agendas becomes permanent is largely up to those who support Third World rights of self-determination. It will not be easy, but it can be done. To maintain the momentum, they must:

- Challenge BP, Shell and other companies, politicians, bureaucrats, journalists and investor groups to do the right thing on energy and economic development, Corporate Social Responsibility, sustainable development and the precautionary principle. We can do so not only by appealing to their better instincts, but also, where necessary, by embarrassing them publicly.
- Insist on a level moral, ethical and legal playing field for all corporations for-profit and not-for-profit alike. We must demand that NGOs and the foundations and government agencies that fund them abide by the same principles of honesty, integrity, transparency, disclosure and accountability that they have long demanded for the corporate world.
- Confront the persistent socialist ideologies and government infrastructures that still dominate Europe and the United Nations and find new ways to broaden the public discourse in these arenas, where free speech, true press diversity, talk radio, think tanks, internet access and other independent sources of thought are far less prevalent than here in the United States.
- Provide greater financial and other support to think tanks, journalists, politicians, companies and other advocates of private property, free markets, trade, technology, innovation, <span style="font-size: 11.5pt">democracy and</span> economic growth.
- Recruit and support more people in developing countries who want an opportunity to be heard, and give them forums to confront the environmental NGOs and urge market reforms for better futures for their families, communities and countries.

Do these things, and Johannesburg could mark, not just a pothole, but the end of the road for eco-imperialism.
