**<span style="font-family:Verdana;
color:#CC0000">Why we care: The Canadian seal hunt was decimated by outside intruders who, in the name of saving nature, destroyed the sustainable economy of Inuit and Newfoundlanders. As a result, the resource-extracting culture withered and its suicide rate skyrocketed as helpless people felt the unreasoning hatred of well-fed constituencies in the dominant urban culture. Now it's time to turn the tables and outlaw or de-fund the anti-extraction groups that practice this barbaric brand of deliberate destruction of resource-extracting cultures. For this reason, <u>CDFE su</u>pp<u>orts the Canadian seal hunt</u> in its effort to meet the Canadian cultures' need for self-determination, protection from eco-colonialism and for world respect of different lifeways. The following op-ed from Canada's *National Post* reflects a similar view:</span>**

**The new eco-colonialism**

**Heather Myers And Tracy Summerville**

> **June 8, 2004 National Post (CAN)**\
> **<u>[http://www.canada.com/national/nationalpost/news/comment/](http://www.canada.com/national/nationalpost/news/comment/story.html?id=8b213bc)</u>**

**The growth of anti-trapping, anti-sealing and anti-logging campaigns has had profound effects on Canada's Inuit, aboriginal and northern communities. Their way of life has been devastated by groups claiming to represent the greater good while excluding from the public-policy process the communities that bear the economic and social fallout.**

**Tactics employed by the anti-sealing campaign have included spray-painting seal pups with green dye to ruin the value of the pelts, draping the pups with protesters' bodies, media campaigns, and the distribution of photos from the hunt to British households.**

**In the Northwest Territories and Nunavut, where the Inuit had a thriving and sustainable economy based on the use of seals for food, oil and skins, adult seals were hunted by rifle and harpoon, a relatively humane technique that requires skill and patience. Nonetheless, the Inuit sealskin economy was gutted by the anti-sealing campaign, which destroyed the markets for their products.**

**Revenues of \$13-million per year in 1981 (roughly split between Inuit hunting adult seals and Newfoundlanders hunting pups) dropped to less than \$3-million in 1983. Self-supporting Inuit communities were reduced to welfare dependency. This, in turn, led to a staggering suicide rate, as people questioned themselves and their culture in the face of condemnation by the dominant Western society.**

**A key tactic of the anti-sealing and anti-trapping efforts is to portray resource-harvesters as brutal and savage, unsuited to the modern world. Fur use is portrayed as cruel, vain, archaic and grotesque.**

**Another tactic, which began with Brigitte Bardot's exploits on the sea-ice, is to involve Hollywood stars in the fight -- knowing an uncritical public will be attracted by the persona without questioning their professional qualifications as ecologists or resource managers.**

**In the absence of scientific proof of seal endangerment, campaigns have shifted their focus to essentially value-driven judgments about whether sealing, trapping or logging are acceptable activities in the "modern" world. Simplistic mantras decrying these activities have replaced nuanced policy formation. At the same time, the campaigns have moved away from pressuring governments, and are now directly targeting industrial consumers and retailers.**

**This approach exploits the shift in public values relating to the environment from one that values resource extraction to one that venerates nature. This is most noticeable among urban, industrialized populations, among whom anti-harvest groups targeting the seal hunt, trapping and logging present themselves as the sole legitimate representative of the public good.**

**Such tactics appear well-intentioned, but in fact push forward a narrow set of values that treat nature as more important than human communities. Most importantly, the people living next to or with the resources in question are given little say. Michael Kendu of the Sea Shepherd Society offers an example. "If a few people are hurt for the good of the global society, then that's not our problem," he told the Nunatsiaq News, a newspaper based in Iqaluit, NU. "It happens all the time." Essentially, the groups leading these anti-use campaigns act as eco-colonialists.**

**Canada's wilderness appears to have evolved from a resource that was exploited in order to serve European interests one or two centuries ago, to one that is to be preserved in order to serve those interests today. After all, argued Randy Hayes of Rainforest Action Network, "Americans have the power to influence B.C.'s logging practices and safeguard a magnificent landscape they may one day wish to visit."**

**From a northern perspective, the campaigns against resource harvesting are often seen as representing the interests of rich, urban, well-fed people who have destroyed their own immediate environment and now want to save others. Rich people who have a secure livelihood fail to understand that other people do not. To date, Greenpeace has at least acknowledged the profound social devastation that its campaigns have wrought on Inuit communities and issued an apology.**

**But Lynx and the International Fund for Animal Welfare never have.**

**For the people of N.W.T., Nunavut and B.C., and all the other resource-based regions of Canada, maintaining a continued link to natural resources is important, whether for economic, social, cultural or spiritual reasons.**

**Sustainability is obviously in their best interests. But the impact of external campaigns designed to limit extraction must be re-examined. Fashionable environmental goals cannot be achieved at the expense of Canada's northern peoples.**

***Heather Myers and Tracy Summerville are members of the University of North British Columbia's Northern and Circumpolar Studies unit.***

**National Post 2004**
