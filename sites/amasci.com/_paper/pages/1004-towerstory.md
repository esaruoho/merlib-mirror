# towerstory

*Source page: `~ables/mom/rd/towerstory.html`*

By Mrs. Kathleen Glaze Ables

The tower chimed the quarter hour--11:45 a.m.--and suddenly I remembered that I was going to be caught in the crush of the lunch traffic at the Student Union cafeteria if I didn't leave the office immediately. Little realizing that such an innocuous decision would lead me into one and one-half hours of unmitigated terror, I picked up my purse and a mystery novel and scurried from the office of the Department of Romance Languages at The University of Texas in Austin, where I was employed as an administrative assistant while my husband was enrolled in a Ph. D. program.

Leaving the building's west exit, I walked northwest where before me, shimmering in the sunlit heat of August I, 1966, stretched an expanse of concrete making up a portion of the South Mall for the Main Building, from which the now famous (or is it infamous?) tower soars 307 feet above campus.

As I climbed the eleven steps leading to the slightly elevated mall, a muffled "bang," as if someone had struck a large piece of tin with a hammer, disturbed the stillness. The sound seemed to emanate from the tower, and I saw four people descending the steps leading from the entrance of the Main Building. They showed no signs of alarm. Because of extensive repairs after a fire in the tower a year earlier and sandblasting of the outside of the building recently, strange noises erupting from that area were not unusual. After a few more steps, I heard another "bang," a report loud enough to arouse my curiosity but not loud enough to cause apprehension.

Half-way across the mall I noticed that a male student in front of me was walking in the same direction toward the Main Building, while a pregnant woman flanked by two young men were approaching the two of us from the north. The three passed within a few feet, and after I had taken four or five more steps, a loud "wh-oom" startled me. Instinctively I turned toward the group--since nothing appeared to be in front that could make such a noise --in time to see the woman sinking to the concrete as she cried out, "Oh, somebody help me! Help me!" My feet rooted to the cement, as a feeling of horror pierced my consciousness: one of her male companions must have shot her! The men were several feet away from the wounded woman, but both sprang toward her. Another "wh-oom" blasted across the mall, and one of the men fell without a sound, his inert form covering a portion of the woman. The other young man darted toward a low hedge bordering the concrete, jumped to clear it, but as a third shot rang out, he fell to the grassy turf.

At the first shot, the male student who was four or five yards in front of me turned and stepped in my direction. Apparently, he intended to assist the woman, but with the second shot, he wheeled and ran west along the sidewalk. His action and the immediately following third shot triggered the release from my near-hypnotic trance. I ran to the broad sidewalk a few yards ahead and turned west. Puzzled and shaken, I looked over my left shoulder at those three people lying on the mall and tried to assess the macabre scene, but I continued to run as fast as my high heels would allow toward the southwest corner of the Main Building where the young man had taken refuge behind a scraggly tree. As I neared him, he said, "You better get in here by this tree." And I did.

Although I did not know it at the time, Daniel Alvirez, a graduate student reading in a second-floor library on the west side of the mall, had heard an unusual noise, which drew him to the windows--and the panorama unfolded before him like a nightmare. Besides those already shot, Alvirez saw a young man running east on the mall toward Garrison Hall. A shot--and he fell to the concrete just before reaching the safety of the structure. Inside Garrison Hall Dr. Michael Hall, associate professor of history, and Mrs. Phyllis O'Keefe, senior secretary, were talking when the first loud blast interrupted them. As other shots followed, they hurried from the office to investigate, and at the front of the building, they saw the man who had been shot lying a few feet from the entrance. Exposing themselves to the unknown gunman, they carried him inside and called an ambulance and the police.

"He was shot in the back," Mrs. O'Keefe related, "and since he appeared to be dying, I held him in my arms--at least I would rather die in someone's arms rather than on a cold floor. His eyes were wide open--they were deep blue--and as a crowd formed around us, someone said, 'He's dying!' I don't suppose he could hear, but I answered, 'No, he isn't.' But I knew he was."

I had barely stepped off the walk to the side of the male student at the tree when a co-ed ran to us and implored, "What's happening, what's happening?" The student said someone was shooting at people. "Oh my God, dear God, what can we do? Those poor people.., " she cried, as she turned and ran down the West Mall walk. We shouted to her to come back, but she gave no indication that she heard.

Thirty to forty-five seconds passed without shots. During the reprieve a student vaulted full speed up the South Mall steps onto the concrete and dashed to the man beside the hedge. He was Ray Cross, who had been in a graduate seminar in a nearby building. Jack Williams, from the same seminar, was just behind Cross, and Williams ran toward the pregnant woman.

"The woman drew up her knee; then let it slide down," Williams later recalled. A third man in a dark suit also appeared on the mall and moved toward the cluster of wounded.

Cross said he knelt beside the man and asked, "Where are you hit?" Blood was oozing from the wounded man's mouth. "He didn't answer, but he moved his arm slightly to give me the impression that he meant his wound was in the abdominal area." Cross began to take off his white shirt to use as a compress when another shot boomed across the mall. The man in the dark suit toppled to the concrete. While Williams bolted down the steps and into a building, Cross sprinted across the grass, cleared a hedge, and hit the sidewalk at top speed, when he heard someone shout that a sniper was in the tower. "That prompted me to veer to the nearest tree," Cross related.

Before the second group of shots was fired, two secretaries joined the male student and me at the tree. One, Mrs. Charlotte Darehshori, 19-year-old mother of a baby daughter, had seen the first victims hit from her office window. She gave an alarm, left the building by the north door and scrambled across flower beds and shrubs to reach us. Close behind her was Mrs. Pat Wont, mother of three. Mrs. Darehshori exclaimed, "Somebody out there is shot!" The student answered her, "There's a sniper in the tower."

"We've got to help those people," Mrs. Darehshori cried distraughtly as she began running in their direction. We shouted for her to come back--not to leave the tree--since foliage, we hoped, kept the sniper from seeing us, although I felt far from safe because we could see the tower through the branches. Safe or not, however, the tree was much better than that huge, foreboding open space, which only seconds ago had been so calm and peaceful. Mrs. Darehshori by now was over the low hedge and running across the lawn toward the victims. As she passed the center area where the flagpole stands, the sniper squeezed off the shot that hit the man in the dark suit and put Cross and Williams to flight. Belatedly realizing, as had I, that someone meant savage murder, she whirled and dived behind the flagpole, where she spent the next one and a half hours in the burning sun, but where, luckily, the large base provided protection from the sniper.

No one on the South Mall now doubted that a madman was amuck and would kill anyone upon whom he could draw a bead. I remember that the sights sickened me, and I was becoming more frightened by the second as the enormity of the danger to everyone in the entire area became apparent. I wondered if my husband, who was to meet me for lunch, would be shot-- how our six-year-old boy would get along if one or both of us were killed. Having been to my mother's funeral exactly one week before, I knew too well what a great loss death can bring.

Mrs. Wont, reacting with admirable acumen and courage, shouted to people looking out doors to call ambulances and the police, that people had been shot. A crowd of students had gathered on the marble porch of the Undergraduate library, which is located just west of the tower, and Dean George F. V. Parker shouted from the porch, "What's going on over there?" Mrs. Wont answered, "People are being shot. Get back inside!" Dean Parker began trying to get curious students into the building.

A few seconds ticked by; a student in bermuda shorts and a sport shirt sauntered into view due east of us across the mall. He must have heard the shots but had no inkling of the gravity of the situation. Another shot. He fell to the walk.

Three students within the last minute had sought our same refuge, and the six of us huddled in a forlorn line behind the all-too-thin tree as the sniper brazenly leaned over the top of the observation deck and cold-bloodedly aimed at his victims. It seemed that for nearly every shot someone fell: gunshots, people running, bodies in the sun, cries for help, shouting, confusion, wonderment, disbelief--the panorama spilled over the boundaries of my reality.

By now at least seven, including another co-ed, had fallen, and no more than three minutes had elapsed since the first "wh-oom" and the pregnant woman had been hit. Her baby boy later was stillborn and may be considered the first fatality on the mall, since the bullet, newspapers reported, fractured his skull and critically wounded the mother.

The sniper, who had pinned everyone down on the South Mall, now shifted his position to the west side of the tower. People had been alerted, but too many dismissed the sounds as fraternity fireworks, or some other logical occurrence and failed to realize the terrible consequences which would explode upon them in seconds....

We under the tree had no idea how many people were being shot, and even I was appalled by the final toll from the guns of the sniper, Charles Whitman. Counting his wife and mother, who were killed before Whitman entered the tower, and the three murdered at the top of the tower, the sniper killed sixteen and wounded thirty-one.

As the minutes dragged by, I knew when the sniper was shooting south or west--the shots sounded much stronger, sharper, and regularly we heard the whine of a ricochet or glass breaking. Whitman fired more than one gun, but that special "wh-oom," evidently from his powerful and scope-equipped rifle, will never be forgotten.

Were we safe? (I never felt sure.) How many ambulances and police cars were in the area? (The sirens were deafening.) Why didn't help come? {The waiting seemed endless.)

A few minutes later a policeman reached us. Since he was seemingly unaware of casualties, he asked if anyone was hurt "out there." We told him yes, many. He jumped into the shrubbery, and protected by a wall, moved east toward the front steps leading from the mall to the entrance of the Main Building. Somebody said, "I'm glad I don't have his job." Police officers arrived quickly, but gradually, and threw more and more of a barrage against the sniper, but their guns sounded like cap pistols compared to the sniper's rifle.

As in every tragedy, a curious set of circumstances aided some--and meant death or injury for others. I firmly believe that if several unfortunate tower visitors had not interrupted the killer at the instant they did, the sniper would have seen a woman in a blue dress climbing the steps and entering the south edge of the mall's expanse of concrete-- a perfect target. By fate's decree, he began the slaughter inside the tower, and I walked across and away from the death zone on the mall, while the trio of students whom I met--and others--walked into it. Even after the pregnant woman and her two companions were shot, I still did not consider myself in great danger, since no one had any reason to shoot me, or so I thought.

Austin Police Chief Bob Miles was correct when he said after the tragedy, "In twenty minutes we had so much fire going up to the Tower he \[Whitman\] couldn't do much more damage." It is believed that only one person was killed after the first twenty minutes, and my opinion is that all but two of the deaths on or near the campus (one a policeman on the South Mall fifteen to eighteen minutes after the sniper's first shot to the mall) were within the first ten or twelve minutes. The only real solution to have prevented most of the deaths, as I see events in retrospect, was to have had armed (rather than unarmed) campus guards trained for such emergencies and stationed in strategic locations. Only if one or more armed persons could have successfully attacked the sniper in his lair and ended the carnage immediately would the death toll have been substantially reduced.

The mass assassination finally ended at 1:22 p.m. when the sniper was killed after two policemen and a deputized civilian invaded the observation deck. Those who had sought refuge under "our little tree" drifted away from the haven as students poured out of their safety niches and formed a milling mass at the west entrance to the Main Building. Limp, but thankful that the ordeal was over, I realized that the last ninety minutes had given me a new--and improved--perspective of life with the important, in contrast to the mundane, etched more clearly in my mind by each murderous shot of the sniper. Praying that my husband--and others--were safe, I bent down for my purse. Beside it on the ground was the mystery story, and for the first time I appreciated its title: The House at Satan's Elbow.
