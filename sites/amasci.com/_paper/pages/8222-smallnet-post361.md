Dave Segal \< 

 \> contributed:

In reply to Jack Dickerson:

If you need technical information on chargers, I can't help you. But if you just want to go out and fly electric planes it is not much of a mystery. Just buy a quality (Astro or HiTec) peak detection charger that will handle the maximum number of cells you expect to use. Connect it to a 12v battery and you are in business. That's all there is to it. If you want a 12v power supply to feed your charger when it is in your workshop, Astro makes a terrific one that weighs a fraction of what the usual power supplies weigh.

Reference Sources: Larry Sribnick of SR Batteries sells a set of technical notes on all aspects of e-power. And finding back issues of Model Aviation to read all of Bob Kopski's columns will be helpful.

Tom Hunt has sent out a notice that the NEAT Fair 2001 will be held September 14 to 16. For details on this electric-power event, see: \< <http://www.nyblimp.com/NEAT.htm> \>.

Dave Segal

John Rossetti \<

After reading Jack's query I felt I could help him out on a charger selection. When I first got into electrics, about 18 months ago, I bought a seven-cell charger. It worked well enough, but when I built my SR X250 using 10 cells, I had to buy another charger.

I bought a Hitec 335G that will handle up to 24 cells. That's what I should have bought in the first place. And, there are other good chargers available that will handle 24 plus cells.

My newest plane, in the building stages, will use 14 Sanyo 2000's. Again, my new charger is what I really need for that. I should say, however, that when I go flying, I have two tractor batteries that I always take with me, and with them and my two chargers I always have at least one of my seven-cell packs charged so I can fly without waiting. John Rossetti

Ken Park \< 

 \> announces:

First Flight of Cassutt Winter Building Project!

Houston! The project is a Go! That's what should be said in our case. Saturday, November 18 the prototype of the Cassutt took to the air for the first time. The day was sunny but cold with a light breeze blowing down the runway. After the last-minute checks and range check the model was ready for the runway.

I fired up the Blue O.S. LA .15 engine and taxied out onto the runway. I opened up the throttle and she began her run. Just a touch of right rudder was needed to keep her straight on the paved surface. With a good head of steam and running on her mains she had to be pulled upward with a blip of elevator and away she went.

She quickly flew around the circuit with only a couple clicks of aileron to hold her steady and fly hands off. The Cassutt will dive a little coming out of turns if the elevator is not kept in. But this being a racing plane, it seemed perfectly natural to let her zoom down and do low fly-by's.

I did some rolls, and the Cassutt was very axial and fast. I pulled the power back and the Cassutt remained very controllable. The landing was very nice, the nose slightly high and she bled off air speed all the way down to a 3-point landing. 3 or 4 flights later I had a flameout and was really impressed with the very long and flat glide. That gave tons of time to bring her back to the runway again. Controls were still very good power off.

The project is making progress as the plans have been sent to the laser people for processing; and Mark Winstanley and I will soon have the "proof of fit" kit to test-assemble before going into production.

The Cassutt flies fine on a .15; a .25 might make her a real wild thing! Overall the plane has exceeded our expectations for being a great sport and racing plane. If this project interests you, then check out the Small Planes button at \< <http://www.ottawarcclub.com> \>. Ken Park

Sam Brauer \< 

 \> reports:

Recently I've been playing around with some of the smaller nickel metal hydride cells available. I bought a couple of packs from Battery America. The smaller nickel metal hydride cells can be hard to come by, so it took a couple of weeks before they arrived.

Anyhow, I've been using 3 different (4, 5 and 6 cells) 150 mAh nickel metal hydride packs with an HY50F motor. This motor is mounted in a Skylark (Al Lidberg kit) and has a pair of HS50 servos and a Sky Hooks and Rigging receiver.

Since the motor is much lighter than what was originally installed (an HY 50B) I had to move the servos forward from what's shown on the plans -- not a bad idea in any event. The all-up weight of my airplane is around 3.5-4 ounces -- significantly lighter than the manufacturer's version, which weighed in at 6.5 ounces.

I prefer how this airplane flies at its lighter weight -- even heavier it didn't really do all that well in wind.

All of these packs show a fairly strong power burst during the first 15-30 seconds of motor run. I think another way of saying this is that these batteries show a sharper decrease in initial voltage than the nicads we're used to.

The 4-cell pack is a little undergunned for this motor. I'm using a Pixie-7 speed control, and the cut-off starts in after a couple of minutes. Furthermore, the airplane will only climb during the first 30 seconds of motor power -- after that it's hold altitude only for a couple of minutes.

I've always landed with a fair amount of capacity left in the cells, because the airplane won't stay up any longer. For folks interested in a cheap, lightweight battery pack, John Worth at Cloud 9 is selling these cells at \$2 apiece, with a little tab welded on. He's also selling a 4-cell holder for under \$2.

I cut off the tabs, and soldered a connector to the holder -- voila -- flight pack for less than \$10 (excluding connector) which weighs less than an ounce and has a 150 mAh capacity. Coupled with the new GWS R4P receiver and a couple of little servos -- you can have a sub 2-ounce 2-channel airborne system for a glider for under \$100.

I've got some towline gliders around 3 feet span I'm eyeing Anyhow, I think this pack is better suited for receiver rather than motor power.

I plan on using the 5-cell pack with this motor in an indoor R/C scale Aeronca C-3 (the Dumas kit) -- but that's not even on the building board yet. The 6-cell pack with this motor gets my vote for most useful. I've flown the Skylark with this combination and I get a decent rate of climb. On bench running, the motor cutout didn't start blipping the motor till after 8 minutes at full power. This should translate to over a 10-minute flight with reserve.

Downsides to these batteries: when I pulled the airplane out of my car where it had been left at ambient temperature of around mid-40's F for a few hours yesterday, the 6-cell pack ran out of juice in under a minute. At home, the pack charged up fine, and would deliver the 8 minutes wide open.

My best guess is that these batteries strongly object to cold -- worse than nicads. If you're planning on using these batteries in the cold, I'd check them out first. If you like flying in the winter, and are planning on using them in an airborne installation, you could lose an airplane real quick.

Why not leave them in a refrigerator for a couple of hours and see how well they do before using them in an airplane?

Charging these batteries has also been tough. The 4-cell pack can be charged with a regular radio charger, but my Astro 110D has not liked them. Too many false peaks. I think the charge rate is a little too high, so I purchased a Sirius charger for these packs. At \$50 it's not cheap, but the charger is well made and easy to use.

It takes a little over an hour to bring up the 150 mAh cells to full. You could also use this charger for somewhat larger cells (between 4 and 8 nicad cells). It handled my 270 mAh NiMH cells OK as well.

Sam Brauer Norwalk, Connecticut

Now for some material gleaned from "personal correspondence". Dave Thacker \< 

 \> replies to these quoted remarks from an e-mail from JW: "Your proposal for micro equipment sounds good to me. As for the switches, that's something I hadn't thought about. I should have, because from personal experience (all the way back to 1947) I know that R/C switch performance can be critical.

"Of course, since CO2 power is relatively non-oily, switch contact contamination isn't likely to be troublesome in my application. Still, I've suffered from contact corrosion problems in a couple of electric-power free flights. What I thought was declining battery capacity turned out to be high-resistance switch contacts.

"For 40 years in EVERY R/C system I've ever owned, I've used redundant switch wiring. (Remember the old Noble switches, with their multiple rows of contacts on the back? Those were my long-time favorites...I wired them for TRIPLE redundancy and never had a problem due to switches..."

Dave replied: One of my best modeling friends (Archie Phillips) is from Huntsville, Alabama. He tore apart a switch brand I was selling early on. He then showed me a Noble switch and gave me a stern talking to. (Yes, he has about 20 years on me.)

I then began a hunt for double-row knife switches (same style as Noble) and since I found them about 2 years ago I have been selling them exclusively.

Back to JW: "...I'd feel safer if the mini-switch you have in mind for me to use with the GWS "system" is either redundantly wired, or capable of being modified that way."

Dave said: The micro switches that I have are not redundant and cannot be made that way. I normally warn people to only use them in weight-critical non-powered ships. I thought they might have an application for you. However, most E-flight applications (you're almost in that category too) just plug the pack in for the switch.

This is the lightest and best method when it is possible to do so. I am a gram counter, so direct connections appeal to me in smaller, simpler models.

Also, I take a "battery pack" mentality towards switches. Very simply put: "They should not be used until they fail." I feel that the number one equipment reason for a crash is bad or under- charged batteries, and would submit to others that the number two is a poor or worn out (or dirty) switch.

Modelers should date battery packs and switches and consider them unusable beyond certain times without regard to how they test. Battery experts would consider me very liberal saying it is OK to use NiCads for 3 years. But to me this is the absolute cap when proper cycling and finances and risk are all balanced against each other.

A switch shouldn't be used beyond 3 years if it is in a regularly-flown model. Just some ideas.

Dave Thacker, \< <http://www.radicalrc.com> \>

John Oettinger \< 

 \> wrote:

JW - As much as I appreciate your SMALLnet, (read each issue carefully) I cannot agree with your comment that glue fillets do nothing but add weight. The difference between a filleted joint "breakability" and a non-filleted joint is more than just enough to justify the difference in weight. The difference in weight is much less than a gram in any model I build and the difference in survivability is measured in orders of magnitude.

Since I've been doing this for only 50 years, I'm willing to be convinced, but by something other than an opinion since my own opinion is based on many dozens of models that depended upon their glue joints for survival.

This is a common subject in magazine columns, and I can't believe the writers have ever looked objectively at the subject.

John Oettinger

<div class="Joe">

...John, I can see your point; and I should've been more specific. For example, where a wing rib butt-joins a tapered trailing edge, a glue fillet will usually add "separation resistance" without increasing weight significantly.

However, since I'm habituated to notched trailing edges, I didn't think to mention that. Another criterion: properly designed and snug-fitting joints were another assumption of mine. I shouldn't have assumed that! There are LOTS of model designs, both published and kitted, where two pieces of 1/8" square balsa (e.g.) are assembled at a butt joint.

Yet adding a glue fillet around such a junction won't add nearly as much strength as changing it to a tapered "scarf joint".

About a dozen years ago in one of my model-building columns in Model Airplane News, I referred to a then-recent report from an eastern university (I'm too tired right now to go dig up that article so I can specify which university) on the strength of glued joints in wood. I do remember that the maximum strength came from a glue thickness not exceeding .007.

Of course, the KIND of glue makes a difference too!

For "model airplane glue", the existence of a TINY fillet at each right-angled juncture indicates that there's adequate adhesive in the joint itself; that the evaporation of solvent hasn't depleted the glue there... The same is probably true for THIN cyanoacrylate.

Then there are glue fillets and GLUE FILLETS. I've seen the work of some modelers (too many!) whose idea of "glue fillets" is about a 1/4" radius buildup. Especially for the aforementioned butt joint between wing ribs and trailing edges -- OK, leading edges too...

I could go on and on, but I'll end with the same example I used (and pictured) in that long-ago MAN column. I designed, and helped my 6th-grade daughter to assemble, a model bridge made entirely of 1/8" square balsa sticks. This spanned a 16-inch gap, and was about 2 inches wide. It weighed under 3 ounces.

In a test-to-destruction contest, this little bridge held without damage over 250 pounds of load! And there wasn't an intentional glue fillet ANYWHERE on it. Other bridges entered in this event collapsed at between ten and forty pounds.

The secret of the winning bridge was proper glue joint design. The only joints you could call "butt joints'' were loaded in pure compression. Glue fillets wouldn't help there. All the other joints were loaded in shear. Fillets wouldn't do any good there either.

My practice when building a model from an old-time design plan (or any other that features butt joints loaded in bending or tension) is to revise the assembly method (and the form of the parts when necessary) to replace all the end-to-end butt joints with scarf-joined ones. \[JW\]

Gary Gullikson \< 

 \> reports:

I recently completed Ziroli's 35" wingspan Aeronca Champ from his plans and the construction article in MAN. I ordered the ABS cowl and windshield, and got the Titanic Airways GD-280 and NiMh 270 mAh 8-cell pack, Astro 110D charger,and HS-55 servos.

This was my first plane constructed from plans and my entry into electric-powered R/C, even though I have been flying wet-power since about 1950. Nick warns not to build this ship unless you can keep total weight below 12 ounces. Mine came out at 11 ounces with ailerons (whew).

After curing tail heaviness and making major repairs, this little plane flies great. I'm getting up to 7 minute flights with hand launches. The plane climbs to 200+ feet, and I'm now able to keep it within about a 70-foot radius. It's a park flyer, not really a slow flyer. With low dihedral and ailerons, it really needs coordinated rudder with ailerons, and will stall and spiral down if provoked. I imagine that with more dihedral and no ailerons, it would be a little more hands-off stable.

It lands smoothly and rolls out without flipping over in mowed grass. It is a majorly cute little scale-like airplane and what I've learned in making it fly will be useful in sucessfully converting my old Flyline and Berkeley kits, of similar size, to e-power. Gary Gullikson
