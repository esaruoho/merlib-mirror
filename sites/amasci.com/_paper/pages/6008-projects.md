Each homestead off the grid in a remote place tends to have unqiue problems and solutions to its needs. We've jotted down a few words here about how we did things in our part of the Okanogan.... and hey, if you feel like jumping in to help with any of these projects, just give us a call!

### Water

It's a long way from Seattle Municipal water; most folks out here have a drilled well, or a developed spring. Since wells are expensive, and we have lots of other things to think about, we decided to solve the water problem in a different way.

We want to have pretty reliable water without having to worry about complicated systems involving pressure tanks (which need power to run them, another thing we won't have for some time); so that means we need a storage system, as there are no springs uphill from the cabin. We settled on a capacity of around 4000 litres, which we hope is enough for 3-6 months use by a few people on odd weekends. (It's amazing how much your consumption drops when you're not on a water line). Water cisterns can be either poured concrete, or some form of tank - we settled on a sectional tank, because we didn't want to get heavy equipment up to the site. Of course, after that decision there was the great fire of summer 2000 and it wouldn't have made any difference given the amount of general tearing up of the place!

Anyway, we excavated a 2m by 5m by 2m deep hole up towards the top of Broken Foot Gulch, which went surprisingly quickly - three of us did most of the work in a morning. We found packed glacial till at the bottom, which was incentive to stop there, but with most of the tank below the frost line, we were pretty much ok anyway. We have at least 15m of head for the water from that point - I'm going to find out exactly how much when I get the surveyor's transit up at the cabin.

Next, we needed a water line dug; after investigating a ditch witch, and learning that it would only dig 75cm or so down (insufficient to prevent freezing of the water line), we decided to hire a couple of neighborhood teenagers to dig the line. I probed the whole water line route with my trusty 2m steel pole (which also came in handy to site the water cistern), and found that there was no route to the cabin that we could get over 1m of depth over the whole distance without hitting bedrock. So, we decided to have running water only in the spring to fall time - usually, we only go up a couple of times in winter anyway. Before the ground freezes, we'll have to drain the line down at the low point in the thicket behind the cabin. At any rate, the trench is dug now, it meanders 200m through the gulch down to the cabin following the line of deepest soil should we ever wish to try and dig it below the frost line.

This is where we are right now. We have in mind to:

- Hook up valves and stop/waste pipe to this system, and piping under cabin to the bathroom and kitchen area.
- Figure out where to install the grey water system from the kitchen and bathroom areas.
- Locate and drill a well. Needs to be a basic decision of going for water close to the ground, or hiring a drilling rig. Possibly rework the old well on the southern 20 into an irrigation well.
- Finish composing toilet installation.
- Install gutter system to collect rain water and snow melt into barrel. Drain this into a kitchen garden area?\

### Cabin Projects

<div align="left">

When Bob and his construction crew left the weekend of the 4th of July 1999, there was a beautiful, but unfinished cabin. The structure was weatherproof, but we wanted to become more involved with the cabin by doing all the finishingwork ourselves.

</div>

Well, we started with the insulation - probably our least favourite task of all the ones we've done since, but one not requiring any skill whatsoever, to break us in! Seemed like we spent the whole summer hauling an endless series of huge fiberglass batts up to the cabin - we could only fit in two at a time in the wagon. But, by labor day that it was done, which led us into the second major task - installing the woodstove. Winters in the Okanogan are not as bad as those in Wyoming or the Dakotas. Still, in mid-winter the high temps vary from 10 to 20 degrees below freezing, with some periods 30 degrees or so below. For that, you need some heat; the cabin was designed to be heated by a woodstove placed towards the front - in practice this works pretty well - the sleeping area in the loft gets some nice convection currents, which should be even better when we get our ceiling fan hooked up. The exception is the back and the bathroom in particular. One of these years we'll install a propane heater for that area.

<div align="left">

Woodstoves are heavy! Roger and I manhandled it into place and then spent a ridiculous amout of time trying to connect up the various pipes to the chimney. The pipes and chimney cost us about four times the price of the yard sale stove, but talking to others, that seems to be par. At any rate the whole contraption works fine, after an initial experience with a backed up chimney due to wood fibers catching in our spark arrestor mesh on top of the chimney. We dumped the spark arrestor - we're only likely to use the stove when there's snow on the ground outside anyway. An unexpected bonus for us is that we can cook on the stove - in winter we almost never fire up the camp stove now.

Sheetrock; yup - hard, dusty work. Holly's kids helped us get a start on some of the more difficult areas needing many hands, such as the stairwell area, and now, many slices of board later, we're mostly done. I can see how it takes lots of practice to hang this stuff well, not to mention the taping parts, which could take us still longer. At the same time we made a bunch of cable runs for when the PV setup is installed.

The ceiling panels are a result of cruising the discard section at our local lumber yard. They always seem to have rejected tongue-and-groove panels which have knots, or strange colors. For our purposes they're fine though, and we bought all kinds of lengths for our ceilings downstairs and in the loft. It's like a jigsaw puzzle actually - fitting these odd shaped length together, and doing it while hanging 5m off the ground on an interesting piece of "scaffolding", built out of 2x6s supported by our dining chairs up in the loft. It sounds scary, but works pretty well, and is much easier to move around than real scaffolding. Now that we've just about completed the high ceiling, we made a very fine discovery - the loft is *much* warmer this winter than it was the last one, before the ceiling was put in. Arvia is actually comfortable in bed at night (without her gloves and parker).

Initially, we were going to do the stucco ourselves - however, after hauling up a bag of cement and mixing it by hand, then spending a whole morning troweling it onto what amounted to a single slice of wallboard, we gave up. Calculations indicated that it would be around 60 bags and two weeks of solid work for us, not to mention running around for supplies. We decided to hire Alejandro and his stucco crew. It looks very professional now, and nicely complements the terracotta style roof. This pretty much finishes the exterior of the place.

Here are some remaning ideas:

</div>

- Install kitchen cabinets, workspaces.
- Design and build seating arrangements next to the windows.
- Build in some bookshelves.
- Finish the trim around the windows and doors.
- Install blinds for the upper west facing windows.
- Propane hookup for the stove and back heater.

### Forest Projects

<div align="left">

The first order of business was to understand what we had got ourselves into. When we bought the first parcel of land, neither Arvia nor I knew much about trees, other than that we enjoyed being around them. It's been a very educational and rewarding process.

</div>

- *Create a forest-stewardship plan for the property. (done!) Actually, I took a class offered by the Department of Natural Resources last winter, which was invaluable, especially for the field trips, and conversations with other folk interested in forests.*

- File a plan to convert the land to Open Space Timber - this means we can fence out those auful cows!

- Controlled burn. This is an important project for the future of Pinehaven: there's been a buildup of combustible matter since the Whiskey Cache Creek fire of 1977, so to reduce the possibility of catastrophic fire and increase seed germination, 10 acre of so burns should be done. The USFS is doing controlled burns in the NF to the north next spring, so I hope to learn more from that.

  > [Fence out the cows.](images/cows.jpg) These guys are a real nuisance, so they've gotta go! They munch all the nice subirrigated grass, poop in the wetlands, trample forage anywhere near water, and generally beat up the ground.
  > Western line: 3-4 fence posts, plus 300m of line. A few odd bits over the cliffs. 2 1m gates for foot/horse access.
  > Southern line: 6 or so posts, plus 400m of line, adjoining Clarence's fence, 20m line, plus repairs in Clarence's Western line.
  > Southeast line: 4 posts, a gate, and cattle guard over the road, plus 200m of line down to the ravine.
  > Eastern line: repair 30m of line.\

- Chop and stack wood for the winter.

- [Hound's Tongue pull](images/hounds.jpg). This invasive weed is taking over some of the tree stands where there's been logging disturbence. We need to pull the plants before they go to seed. There's also a small amount of Knapweed.

- Extricate logging slash from the wetlands and Lake Arvia. Lookout for that chainsaw!!

- Wildflower ID.

- Count trees. Really! (Actually, we'll be sampling on fixed sites: using the digital camera to record growth of seedlings and trees over time).

- Try out some Western Larch seedlings in a contolled environment on the north facing slopes; first thing when the snows melt. April probably.

- Install bird houses around wetlands; before nesting season.

- Animal track ID during winter snows.

- Locate a swimming hole down somewhere along Antoine Creek.

- Think about using some of the animal trails for XC ski trails.

- Decommission the jeep track up to the cabin used in the construction. Re-seed with native grasses and bushes. A late fall project.

- Haul a fair bit of debris acquired from long departed folk in the Southern 20. Hopefully, we'll hire some of the neighborhood teenagers to help us with this.
