Dr. Michael Hawkins \< 

 \> writes in reply to Graham's query on the Russell Henderson monoplane:

I made a 1/6th scale model of the Russell Henderson a few years back. The engine was an HP .21 four-cycle.

The EAA publication which carried a reprint of the original construction notes for this aircraft said nothing about colour schemes; but from the photos my guess was a royal blue fuselage and silver wings.

There were NO photos of the completed aircraft -- and from my experience with the model, if the full-size craft was completed, I do not believe that it ever flew.

The tail surfaces were much too small. After first attempts to fly, I increased both horizontal and vertical tail areas by about 80%.

Back to the field, six successive attempts at take-off each ended in a vicious ground loop.

Back to the workshop, I fitted a simple helicopter gyro on the rudder.

This time I did get the R-H to take off; and it flew many times with reasonable stability. I still have it. It was published in the January 2001 edition of R/C Model World (British).

I was concerned that Charles B. Russell might have killed himself in his creation; but later I came across a photo of a 1932 design that he had made and which I believe is still flying.

Clearly, the Bulldog breed !

Welcome back to the fold, all fellow SMALLnet enthusiasts.

Mike Hawkins, Bangkok

John Meacham \<

Seeing SMALLnet on line again makes me feel young again, like a kid of 70 instead of the 80 years coming up.

Just like most older curmudgeons I rant and rave about the hobby going to Heck with all the plastic ARFs and RTFs I see in the ads and in the hobby shops today.

But then I think back in memory about starting an O&R .23 with spark ignition and the fuss with a booster battery plus flight batteries and fiddling with the spark advance (right under the prop hub) plus needle valve. With a radio that used miniature gas tubes. (Those were RK-61s; they wouldn't work when new, nor when too old. I think their useful life was 37 minutes the second Tuesday after you bought them.)

An R/C outing was usually a trip to Sepulveda Dam's flood control basin, and a good day was when both the engine and the radio decided to work at the same time. A really great day was when you crashed someplace different than you would have without the radio.

Makes me want to put a new blade in my balsa stripper and cut up some 1/16 squares out of sheet and get a set of plans out plus some waxed paper plus a tube of Ambroid and build me a couple of airplanes. I know I have a couple of rolled-up "Pacific Ace" plans out in the shed and some electric motors that should do the job. (The only kit in my history that never failed to fly. Except for some of those Indian-named kits from Veco.) \[compliment\]

I learned I cannot fly C-L any more. The last time I tried, I got so dizzy I fell down in the street. John

Pat Tritle \< 

 \> relates:

In response to Randy Randolph's comments on whip flying U-control: I flew whip-power in the mid 60's when we lived in Salt Lake City. I never went so far as to actually control the models, but the airframes were cut and built from common corrugated cardboard. The "engines" were molded from plain old- fashioned modeling clay with the size of the "engine" being dictated by CG requirements.

Since the models weren't controlled, I'd play with the CG until it flew a nice straight track at any speed. Then, the line would be "reeled" out until either the front yard got too small to handle it or a comfortable speed was reached. It was great fun for an 11-year-old kid making 25 cents a week! I'd try it again today, but the neighbors already think I'm nuts, so we'll just let that sleeping dog lie!

PAT

Delighted to see SMALLnet is back!

After 70 years of model planing I always enjoyed rediscovering that I didn't quite know it all just yet. Many times I picked up an idea or kink from other readers, or had something of my own to contribute.

Like now: The canopy cement Liquid Stitch. I'll definitely give it a try. I've used lots of stuff like RC56 for other jobs than canopies. I particularly like it for sticking small electric motors in place. For most lightweight films and food wraps used for covering, I like the stuff Dick Henderson told me about several years ago. This is Acrylic Gloss Varnish, an art store product. It is particularly good for sticking Reynolds Plastic food wrap. I paint the frame with it, then pinch the food wrap into place with finger pressure, then tighten it with a hair dryer -- a heat gun is much too hot.

I have a couple of wings ten years or older still tight and unwarped.

Diesels? I still have a small Mills, I think it is around .07, and two Drones, one of which was fitted with a custom made crank after the original crank broke. It never bothered me that the Drones did not have adjustable compression. Nothing to fool with and they ran just fine for my control line models. One Drone I fitted up with a second needle to experiment with adding all sorts of stuff after it was running. Inconclusive results from a not very-well-thought-out program. The other Drone, pristine, is going to be fitted out with a carburetor when I get around to it.

Many years ago I converted a sub-piston Atom to compression ignition by installing an aluminum washer in the head. Ran great, but the castor and ether melted the nitrate dope in which the model (Nieuport 17) was finished. Incidentally I've never had any problem with finger-flipping, but think I'd be dubious about electric starting.

That's interesting about whip control. Have tried a couple of experiments, then flew a small electric with a spinning rod recoup. Worked pretty well. The basic design was a deli-tray pusher with which I've had a lot of luck. This will appear as an R/C project in FM sometime in the future.

Roy C.

Mike Halbrook \< 

 \> says:

I'm not flying at the "Secret Flying Site" at the field of rocks any more. I've left California and am now in Nampa, Idaho. We have a giant dry lake south of town, sort of like an Edwards Air Force Base for model planes.

I have flown Control Line, R/C, and an AJ Interceptor catapult glider out there. One of the issues with SMALL flying here is the 2500 ft elevation. Small engines don't put out a lot of power on a hot day in the summer here. Some of the planes also run into density altitude issues.

My main SMALL R/C model up here has been a Comet P-40 converted to 2-channel R/C with an .049 engine. This is the larger 31" wingspan Comet Super Star model of the P-40E (really a K, M or early N). This plane has the crisscross wing ribs and cardboard tube down the fuselage center construction typical of the "Super Stars".

This builds a light, rugged model. I set it up with ailerons and elevator control. I'm using two HS55 servos, an FMA M5 receiver, 110 mAH battery, and a Cox SureStart engine (since upgraded to a Killer Bee .051). This plane flies quite well in the spring and fall, but won't always get off the ground in July or August.

Because of the wing fillets it is not suitable for handlaunching. The lack of a controllable rudder can be a hindrance at takeoff. Fortunately the lake bed is large enough to do large circles on the ground until flying speed is achieved. Still, it has made for some hairy takeoffs.

Another project I'm in the middle of is a AirHog P-40 converted to electric RC as a parkflyer. It is waiting better weather at the moment as the below-freezing conditions we have currently effect the output of its LiPoly battery.

I hope to catch up on some the other projects you folks are up to now that SMALLnet is back on line.

Best Regards, Michael Halbrook

...Michael, low-wingers such as your P-40's can be hand-launched relatively easily. Use an underhand toss! Grip the fuselage from above, close to the balance point, and launch more or less as you'd release a bowling ball -- only somewhat higher off the ground.

Eric Clutton used this technique with aplomb at Little Rock SMALL meets, flying his several slimmed-down WW2 fighters. (BTW, this year's event is less than 5 months away!) \[JW\]

George Hostler \< 

 \> tells us:

I hit the "half century" mark 9 months ago and I'm finding out that things aren't quite like when I was 20. I've already suffered a couple of herniated disks.

I've never tried whip C/L models, but I remember the profile all-balsa one of the Bell X-1 in Walt Musciano's book on flying scale models. Walt went into great detail on how to fly it.

In keeping with small R/C tradition, recently I picked up a few kits: a deBolt Livewire Kitten 34", Sterling S-23 Hornet 18" and a Lou Andrews S-Ray 50".

The Kitten appears to be in the same class as the Sterling Mini- Mambo 36", but predates it a little as the plans show an RK-61 tube receiver among others, and 67-1/2 Volt "B" supply made of 22-1/2 Volt photoflash batteries (about the size of a 9-Volt but a little longer). As it was set up to handle the weight of the "lightweight" tube receivers and batteries of the mid-50's, it would probably make a fine electric flyer if one so chose. Hal DeBolt was a genius! The Kitten sports washout at the tips for greater stability.

My immediate thoughts are to retain rudder-only control and put a Norvel .061 R/C with extended clunk tank in it. It has a lifting tail.

The Hornet seems to be of the same class as the Veco Dakota, as it is an all-sheet-balsa biplane, but it resembles more of a Liberty type biplane. It was designed as free flight (.020), or U/C (.049). Scaled up about twice would make a good R/C flyer. Some brainstorming tells me that in the same all sheet spirit, one could make the wings Jedelsky style or at least the top one. I paid \$24 + shipping off E-bay, which today is considered a bargain. Back it the mid-60's it retailed for \$3!

One that I've always wanted but has been discontinued for a while is the S-Ray. I remember seeing one in Sherrill's Hobby of Little Rock, AR back in 1977, but I was a college student at the time. Since then I have never seen one available. It appeared to be an ultimate R/O plane at 50" span (423 sq. in.) for .07 to .10. After examining the drawings, the fuselage forward part is rather beefy at 1/8" sides with 5/16" doublers extending from the engine cowl sides to the wing trailing edge.

Lou Andrews (the designer) apparently was anticipating the knocks of an R/O beginner at the helm. (\$75 + shipping off E-Bay, but back in its day is was probably 1/4th that.)

I also picked up a plan of Earl Stahl's Blohm & Voss BV-141 28"-span observation plane off Al Lidberg's plans site: \< <http://www.aalmps.com> \> -- the weird single-engine plane with only half of a stabilizer, and its glassed-in cockpit offset from the centerline. The outline would probably make for a good profile all-balsa or 1/4" insulating foam electric.

\< <http://www.parmodels.com/> \> is Paul and Ralph Bradley's Model Airplane Page that I recently stumbled on. They have scans of many of the old Jig-Time rubber powered airplanes, both Top Flite and Goldberg, that if scaled up would be ideal small flyers. As shown, they can be printed onto balsa from an inkjet printer and made into a reasonable facsimile of the original planes.

Laters, George
