Hi Folks; The Bossman's comment on engines struck a chord.

The first thing to make me not want an engine is when someone has to do something to it to make it work!

When I flew on oilypower, my favourite smaller engine was the O.S. .10 FP -- I've had maybe a half dozen and am keeping my last 6-year old NIB just in case the urge for a light Lazy Bee in the traditional form strikes. This little gem came out the box ready to rock, roll -- and idle. One tank of gas on the ground and you could go fly and rely!

I use the past tense, as I've heard too many reports that O.S. now have more drive to compete with Chinese rip-off prices than to be the best. The .10 and .15 FP's were really great engines for those of us who regard the engine as the thing that spins the prop, rather than a sub hobby in itself.

Which is why I've ended up as e-power-only flier. Pop in a pack, turn it on. For a lightweight calm weather cruiser, you can launch and then start the prop spinning. You can't beat the noise levels for that park across the street from the local church either!

One big downside -- now that we can fly in parks, so can everyone else. It's more than possible that some "consumer" who surfed over a web site that sells plug-and-play BARF parkfliers, is trying to get his latest purchase to work just over the road in his living room -- with his transmitter turned onto your frequency. That he can't spell "Megahertz" won't matter to your radio.

I hate to be a doom and gloom-er, but check out your radio - and maybe even the neighbourhood - really well before you launch in your local schoolyard.

Adam Bryant \< <adamdb@juno.com> \> submitted:

One of the cautions on Norvel's website is to not use a rubber bulb or syringe with a rubber plunger to fuel your Norvel-powered plane. Apparently some of the rubber dissolves in the fuel, and contaminates the glow plug, causing performance problems.

I had a perfectly good syringe with a rubber plunger that I wanted to use, so I separated the rubber plunger "cap" from the plastic plunger, wrapped the rubber cap in thin plastic (I used a piece of grocery bag) then reassembled the rubber cap and plastic plunger.

I have been using this for several weeks now with 25% Byron's 1/2A fuel and have had no problems. The plastic doesn't seem to be deteriorating any, and the rubber stopper is safely sealed away from the fuel.

Adam Bryant, Parker, Colorado

Ian L. McQueen \< <imcqueen@nbnet.nb.ca> \> responded to the previous Posting's plea for CO2 "bulk tank" information with:

JW's request for comments on leads me to make an offer of partial information. The two of us in Saint John who have CO2 motors want to be able to charge them from something other than costly cartridges.

Co-conspirator Roy found a couple of military surplus CO2 tanks once situated in a Canadian Air Force aircraft (10 pound capacity; \$10 Canadian each, in wooden shipping crate with military markings). "We're on our way", we gloated.

To our chagrin, we found that the tank had a bastard ultra-fine female thread in the neck, so no common thread is available to fit it; and the fitting that went into it could not be adapted for our purpose.

However, the information of what we plan to do once we have a way of attaching "things" to the tank may be of use to other modellers considering something similar.

Every city of any size is going to have an industrial gases supplier. We have two or three here in Saint John, an industrial city of a modest 100,000 or so. I went to one and got talking to one of the people "out back" who actually fills tanks, and showed him what I had in mind.

There is a unique thread on the outlet from the valve used for each major type of industrial gas; certainly for oxygen, acetylene, nitrogen, and CO2. This prevents the faintest possibility of someone connecting the tank to the wrong line, either in filling or on site.

This outfit had boxes containing dozens of "surplus" valves, and the fellow gave me a couple for CO2 at no charge. At the front of the store I bought a nipple that butts onto the valve outlet, and what I believe is called a "union nut", which clamps the nipple to the valve -- both made of brass. The nipple is universal, but the union nut is specific to the thread of the valve.

I have a number of nozzles for Telco motors, and they screw onto a 1/8" NPT (National Pipe Thread; tapered) male thread. When we can come up with a way to connect the valve to the tank, we will then get a fitting made up with the 1/8" NPT on one end and a way to attach it to the nipple on the other (probably brazed).

When it comes time to fill the tank, the union nut will be unscrewed to remove the nipple and allow attaching the valve to the filling line. The valve can then be closed and the fitting reattached.

The plant that I went to had facilities for pressure testing tanks. When we finally get this made, we plan to arrange some sort of insulation around the valve to ensure that the liquid CO2 inside the tank remains liquid going into the motor's tank and isn't vaporized by the warmth of the ambient air. (I am assuming that this is useful; anyone with practical experience in this area is invited to share that experience with us.) Ian M

Carlo Godel \< <regiaero@gj.net> \> adds:

Well you asked; so on filling, or having a CO2 bottle filled, the best places to look are welding outfits and places that cater to inert gas welding. They use inert gases to make welds strong, no oxidation. In the past in Los Angeles it has cost me \$8 to fill the 2 1/2 pound Peck tank, and that is cheap to say the least.

That amount of CO2 lasts about a year on the smaller motors (.01 cc and smaller). Make sure you tell the outfit you want liquid in your tank. For the compressed air engines, a tank of nitrogen is handy, but must be used with a regulator to keep the pressure down to 120 psi. Those items are a bit costly, but will last a good long time and fill your tank without making your arms tired.

As for the new Jetex, nothing but problems from my point of view. The new fuels are filthy and plug the orifice while running and blow hot gases back into the airframe: flaming missile time.

The current Jetex does not have the reliability that is essential for good fun. Ignition is iffy at best (1 out of 3 lit) and the thrust is intermittent and not uniform at all. The clogging of the nozzle is most of the problem. When burning, the ash given off plugs the small opening and kills the thrust.

I do not recommend the Jetex at this time.

...Here's some further information on the Gasparin GMW73T Twin that was mentioned in one of the SMALLnet Postings last month. (The following is excerpted from one of my forthcoming Engine Shop columns in Model Aviation:)

"Two of these new motors are available so far: a single-cylinder (GMW73) and an opposed-cylinder twin (GMW73T). Both motors feature high-efficiency gas valves and long piston strokes (1.7 times the bore). The Twin has an unusual tandem CO2 tank arrangement and a "through the firewall" pressure connection.

"The Twin's pistons are both connected to the same crankpin, so that their power strokes occur 180 degrees apart. This motor turns a 7-inch high-pitch propeller for over six minutes at about 2000 rpm. It's also capable of spinning a standard rubber-power type P-30 prop (9 1/2" diameter) for close to 7 minutes at 1250 rpm. Those are park-flyer R/C durations!

"CO2 motor operation is more subtle than it appears. The piston isn't really blown downward by the gas pressure stored in the tank. Instead, what powers the piston is the EXPANSION of a strictly limited amount of pressurized gas, admitted into the cylinder when the moving piston reaches the top of its stroke.

"In Gasparin's new "73" designs, the "gas valve" is only fully open for less than three milliseconds per stroke, admitting approximately half a milligram of gas. The valve closes as the piston starts its descent; and the power that moves the piston comes entirely from the expansion of that tiny quantity of CO2.

"It's quite true that when the CO2 enters the cylinder it's under pressure. However, that pressure is nowhere near the (nominal) 800 psi in the tank. Some pressure is lost from having to pass through small-diameter tubing to reach the cylinder. The gas pressure drops even more as it flows through the tight restriction of the piston-operated ball valve and into the cylinder head. There at last the gas begins producing useful output, by expanding and driving the piston down.

"In that respect, a CO2 motor works much like a glow engine. Its power comes from gas expansion pushing against a piston. A glow engine works by "internal combustion". Its gas expansion results from heat developed by burning fuel. A CO2 motor's gas expansion also requires heat to develop power -- but that heat's obtained from the atmosphere itself.

"A glow engine has fins on its cylinder for dissipating excess heat into the passing airstream. On the other hand, a CO2 motor has fins on its cylinder to GAIN heat from the passing airstream. That's important, because as any compressed gas expands, it loses heat energy. That limits its ability to expand further, unless it can acquire heat energy from somewhere else to replace the expansion losses.

"That's not a trivial consideration with model CO2 motors, as is demonstrated by their noticeable loss in power output when operated in humid weather. Frost accumulates on the cylinder fins then. That interferes with warming by the airstream -- and the motor's power decreases substantially.

"One reason for the Gasparin GMW73T's double tank arrangement is to maximize the quantity of liquid CO2 that can be loaded. Yes, CO2 can remain in liquid form at "room temperature" if it's kept under enough pressure. (The "capsules" used to power pellet guns contain 12 grams of liquid CO2, at approximately 800 psi.) Liquid CO2 is about 1700 times denser than gaseous CO2. That's why, for maximum running duration, it's best to fill a CO2 motor's tank with as much liquid CO2 as possible.

"But that's not easy. During the filling operation, the first liquid CO2 that enters the motor's tank instantly flashes into gas. It's the same situation as if you were pumping water into a red-hot steam boiler. As soon as fluid touches a surface that's much hotter than the liquid's boiling point (about -110 degrees F. for CO2) it vaporizes. And that generates pressure.

"Back pressure produced during CO2 tank filling is what limits the amount of liquid CO2 the tank can hold. When the pressure inside the tank equals that from the external supply, no more inflow can occur.

"It's common for a CO2-power flyer to fill his model's tank; let the motor run while holding the airplane until the tank is empty -- then quickly re-fill. The chilling effect of the CO2 expanding within the tank as the motor runs reduces its temperature considerably. Then when the new filling begins, less "boil-off" occurs, and the tank accepts more liquid. That produces longer flight time.

"The "tandem tank" of the GMW73T twin improves the "liquid- accepting capacity" of the larger (15.5 cc) tank by providing a receptacle for some of the "boil-off gas". That makes loading the tank quicker and more convenient, because you can get good flight duration from each filling without needing to go through a "pre-freeze" routine.

"However, a loss in efficiency occurs. Tests have shown that the GMW73 Twin can hold about 20% more liquid CO2 in a pre- chilled 15.5 cc tank alone than in both tanks (totalling 18.5 cc capacity) filled at room temperature. That means a longer, more consistent power output is possible, at the cost of a lengthier and more complicated refilling procedure...." \[JW\]
