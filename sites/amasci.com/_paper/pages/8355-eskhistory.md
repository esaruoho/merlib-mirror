# History of Eskimo North

#### as told by Nanook

Incorrectamungo.. In fact, I had not seen this film when either the system nor my login was chosen (but I think now that I really MUST)...

Eskimo aquired it's name semi-accidentally. It started life as "STIX", soft-touch-information-exchange (only because the phone number the telephone company gave me was 527-soft and I was trying to figure something to do with it, coincidentally, the voice number they gave me was 527-hard, if that isn't bizzare I don't know what is, credit for noticing that one goes to Glenn Gorman).

Frank Zappa does, for lack of better words, off-the-wall music, kind of a mix of Jazz and Rock with strange stories.

Don't Eat the Yellow Snow revolves around an eskimo boy named Nanook who attacks a commercial fur trader who is in the process of whaling on a favorite baby seal.

Now, before Nanook, and before Eskimo North was multi-user, it used to run on a lowly Z-80 machine, single line. I had written an assembly language driver and modified BASIC I called COMBASIC (because it had a number of extra keywords and functions really geared towards a BBS), and Glenn Gorman had written a BBS program called Minibin which was restricted to working with Micro-Peripherals BUS Decoding modem, and which he couldn't sell because it required their driver.

Glenn was interested in selling Minibin software. I was interested in selling COMBASIC.. So we worked out an arrangement where I'd port the two products to each other, and we'd sell the package and split the returns. Well, it didn't really work out this way, actually Glenn sold the package, I didn't see anything. So then the deal changed to Glenn would sell to small systems, and I to large as I had added many features that no longer made the system fit on a machine with minimal disk storage. But then I lost interest in selling it altogether and got more interested in just running the board.

Now, at the time there were about 20 bbs's in the greater Seattle area, and having two Minibin's online confused the hell out of people. So we wanted to name them so that people wouldn't confuse them. First we tried, "Minibin South" for Glenn's system and "Minibin North" for mine. People still got confused. They didn't understand why if they logged into one they didn't have a login in the other, etc. So we got more extreme. Glenn changed his to "Jamaica South", I thought, what's up north? Eskimo's live up north.. Hence, "Eskimo North" came into being.

Well, shortly there after Glenn changed his back to just "Minibin", and Eskimo North has very much diverged from stock Minibin.

This was during the early 80's, and I also aquired 4 double-sided 80tk drives really cheap. 3mb ain't doodly now, but in 1982-1983 timeframe, it was BIG for a bbs. One of the users of my system modified an Infocom driver so it would use a UID number I stuffed in memory to create a different save file for every users. We had about 13 infocom games and a lot of other stuff online. Eskimo North became quite popular.

Now, a book was written by a fellow up in Alaska, and in the book he mentioned Eskimo North, "Eskimo North... A strange system. You must call at least once even if it's long distance." And with an endorsement like that the name stuck.

It continued to gain in popularity and towards the end of '84 I could unplug the modem at 4am, plug it back in and someone would instantly connect. Going multi-user seemed like a natural step. I knew nothing of Unix, a little about CP/M and OS/9, but eventually opted for Unix as the former two were too limited. I did not want to be stuck with one hardware archetecture and a 64k address space.

At this time I had also obtained a trial account with Compuserve. I was fascinated by some of the things you could do in a multi-user environment, but Compuserve was too expensive for me to use substantially and I was too restricted on what I could do. But this provided a lot of additional motivation. Other influencing factors were the PPS systems which had multi- player adventure games implemented by tying Apple computers together with multiple super-serial cards and custom drivers. Yet another influence was a CP/M system called Mu-Mon that was operated by Heath in the early 80's. It was kind of neat in that you had access to editors and programming languages online.

For those who weren't familiar with it, PPS was Pirates of Puget Sound, run by Dan Cascioppo, Rich Williamson (glitch), Ron McCrae (Super Pirates), Tony Garton (victim).

A model 16B Tandy was selected, which over time we outgrew, upgraded to a Tandy 6000, outgrew, added the 4-mb MMU upgrade, outgrew, and then in 1991 fall moved to a Sun 3/180, which we later upgraded to a 3/280, then to a 4/280, and then split-off news onto a seperate 4/330, then added a second 4/330, then replaced the 4/280 with a 4/670MP, then replaced the 4/330 that was eskinews with a Power-uP equipped IPX (80mhz), which is basically where we are at today.

When we moved to a Unix platform, I needed a login. The ONLY eskimo name I knew was 'Nanook' from Frank Zappa's "Don't Eat the Yellow Snow", and so that is what I used. Well, here we are today, Eskimo North is still Eskimo North and I'm still using Nanook as a login.

So that is where both the system name and my login came about.
