Bill Lee \< 

 \> adds:

Posting 431 mentioned getting kerosene for use in model diesel fuel. If you want to go to the trouble of trying to get the jet aviation fuel, ask for Jet-A.

However, I have found that the kerosene typically sold in the home improvement centers for kerosene heaters works equally well, and is often easier to find. Look for cans labeled "K-1" or "1-K" or similar. This stuff used to be crystal clear, but it now has a red coloring added to distinguish it from other kerosenes and their approved uses.

Also, the fuel blend JW recommended is quite appropriate for lapped-piston plain bearing engines; but a "modern" engine with perhaps ABC or AAC piston/liner and ball bearings doesn't need anywhere near 30% oil. 20% is more than enough.

Remembering that horsepower comes from the kerosene, and you can easily decrease the ether content and increase the kerosene. 30%-35% ether is usually sufficient.

As for the use of "ignition improver", I have found that it greatly improves the handling (starting, setting of the needle and compression) when used. Your caution to not use too much is well made, and I recommend 1-1/2% as a rule-of-thumb.

Regards, Bill Lee

<div class="Joe">

Hi Folks; Still around, though you probably would tear up my SMA non-membership card if you saw the electrics I've been messing with!

On clear windows, and lightweight see-through covering: Document laminating film -- "Doculam" is the trade name that gets bandied around mostly -- is clear once heated (it's a little 'frosted' as it comes off the roll) and has heat-sealed stickum like our beloved coloured films. You apply it like "\*.Kote", heat-shrink it; and there you have a tough, if very see-through covering.

Ideal for windows too. This stuff came in Lazy Bee kits for that purpose. As the dear old Bee had flat panes for its windscreen, the Doculam did well for all the windows.

If you'd rather keep the insides of your model from view, you can tint laminating film by very lightly spraying the glue side. The secret is 'very lightly' and, to my amazement, the stickum still performs its appointed task. I have tried spraying the stuff on test pieces -- a piece painted in some form of "Krylon" resisted blatant attacks by rough sandpaper.

The good news is once you've tracked this stuff down, it's cheap per square foot. The not so good news is that there's a lot of square feet in every roll. The rolls tend to be narrow, to match laminating jobs rather than model aircraft covering jobs. It's pretty tough stuff; have heard of models way over SMALL sizes being done in document laminating film.

"Dave Campbell admires Randy Randolph's designs" -- And shows remarkable taste!

I picked up Randy's BeeTween plan when it came out in MAN. Was still living in England, but I had access to the USAF base stores, thanks to my dear wife Sue, and her DOD contract job 'over there'.

One look at the BeeTween and I rushed home to build one! The "Pfeffer" 0.6cc diesel, a gorgeous hand-made Swiss sideport, made a lovely noise, but BeeTween only got to a fast taxy on tarmac on that one! Figuring Randy knew what he was at, the nose got a C\*x .049 of some ilk, and my new low-winger proceeded to fly like it looked -- great!

After a spell of confusion amongst my clubmates -- as in, how could it do aerobatics with no ailerons, a little bitty, whiney engine; and not needing a truck to haul it around in. Soon after, I often had a line forming to fly her!

After a while, I borrowed some of BeeTween's 'numbers' to make a low-winged R/E model of my ownsome, with a PAW 55 diesel up front. Mine was a sort of sleeker, though still a rectangular box, shape -- and was 'streamlined' by leaving off the UC and launching with an under-armed toss. This too baffled many as it did two or three rolls down the field.

Its best story had to be when I'd landed it (pancaked it down with the motor running -- the PAW 55 ran about I don't know how long on one single ounce) and left it in the pits. A few minutes later, I'm chinwagging with some guys when I hear an unmistakable buzzing noise. There's one of our large model enthusiasts -- nothing under an engine from a small Honda motorcycle -- standing there, holding my little low-winger, with the PAW buzzing away happily.

He'd been so intrigued at something smaller than his stabilizer that he'd picked her up and flipped the prop. With fuel where it needed it, the PAW responded by starting!

Fortunately, he was strong enough to hang on to the model until we all stopped laughing and I wandered over to demonstrate the technique of placing a digit over the air intake until the PAW finally admitted it needed air to run, and quit.

Dave - if you ever get the chance, meet with Randy. He is one great guy!

Tim Hamel mentioned flying Lite Sticks and Speed 280's with lithium batteries.

I only fly electrics these days -- all sizes from indoors to 6-pound aerobatic models with around 3/4 kilowatts of power. Tales of 'different' chemistry batteries abound, and there's no doubting that they work.

However!

If you are the sort who calls an electrician if a light bulb needs changing, there's a lot of air miles left in the dear old Nicad cell.

My favourite model for flat calm parkflying is Mountain Models' "DuskStik" \< <http://www.mountainmodels.com> \> for the best range of laser-cut parkflier kits around -- how's about a fully- aerobatic parkflier with retracts, for one ?).

This does fifteen minutes off a pack of 300 mAh Nicads I bought from "All Electronics" -- surplus phone packs, \$2.00 for 3 cells. 6 cells does the GWS-A impelled Duskstik fine, and the commercial plug/socket system I idly used made the pack cost over 50% more.

I've even used these cells for much shorter flights in my Mountain Models "Switchback" fully aerobatic parkflier.

Conclusion? If you know enough to experiment with 'odd stuff' -- more power to you as you lead the way to new ideas. If you don't want to tinker, stick with the known stuff: it works.

The other conclusion is that folk who don't have a parkflier are missing out on a lot of fun!

For those folk who want to try electric power, it is now so 'plug and play' compared to scant few years ago, it's amazing. But it requires both a little basic understanding of electric flight, and knowledge of what is for sale will save you spending your hobby dollarpoundpesos on wheel re-invention.

Rather than trot out the already written, off with you to \< <http://www.ezonemag.com> \>.

Start with the FAQ (frequently asked questions) area, then browse the discussion forums. These contain the questions from folk who want to know, and answers from e-flighters world-wide.

Be polite -- check out the "search" function: someone might have supplied the information you seek last week, and it clutters the databases if folk keep asking the same questions over and over again. About every facet of e-flight has its discussion area -- there are separate ones for 'micro models' and "parkfliers", for example. Nice friendly group of folk, all with interest in e-flight.

If it flies under infernal consumption power, it can fly with electric power -- possibly even more so at SMALL sizes.

Regards, Dereck

Sam Brauer \< 

 \> amplifies:

I can help some of the SMALLsters with e-power suggestions. Don't know if I count as a "maven" yet, but I can take a stab at it.

First off -- a useful resource that bears repeating is \< <http://www.ezonemag.com> \>. Lots of e-flight folks hang out there and discuss EVERYTHING.

With regards to Larry Dodd's question about the Sky Knight -- Assumption \#1) the weight of 15 ounces is for the airframe only. The recommended power system seems like it easily would weigh about that by itself. Therefore, as a rough guess, the completed airplane is going to weigh about 2 pounds ready to fly.

Larry asked about NiMH batteries. Therefore I'm assuming he's interested in duration. (Note -- if you plan on flying in wintertime, stick with Nicads.)

For an airplane weighing 2 pounds, not a floater or an old-timer, I'd prefer 175 watts and up. Thus, he needs a 7- or 8-cell pack. Right now the best energy density I know of in a pack in that size is the 4/5 AUP (manufacturers code) NiMH cells with 1700 mAh capacity. I haven't played with these cells yet, but the reports coming in are very favorable, and I'll probably buy a couple of packs soon. An 8-cell pack should weigh around 12 ounces, which is lighter than (or the same as) the batteries originally specified for the airplane.

About the Piece of Cake: Hobby People is selling an assembled 8-cell 1100 mAh NiMH pack for (I think) about \$25 that's getting rave reviews. This pack performs the same as the old 500 AR Nicad cells, with 2x the capacity at the same weight.

It's also cheaper than a pack of 500 AR cells (and better for the environment). That'll haul the airplane around easily.

Another option would be 1100 AAU Nicads; but folks who've used both types of packs seem to like the NiMH cells more. On the Piece of Cake though, it shouldn't make much of a difference.

A word about batteries: batteries are the fastest-changing part of electric flight. It's quite clear that the replacement of Nicads by NiMH cells is well under way, and that there are now good NiMH cell replacements for Nicads in every size and ampere range. (Note that in a critical radio application, I'm still using SR Nicad packs. I've had too many other packs act funky -- occasionally dumping charge when exposed to heat or cold.)

On the horizon, there are some new lithium polymer cells which may be worth looking at, being sold by FMA. These cells are not astronomically priced, and don't need sophisticated chargers. In other words, I wouldn't invest a lot of money in batteries and chargers right now -- they can become obsolete very quickly.

A simple charger that will handle lots of requirements is the Hitec CG-340. It sells for \$40 or so, and handles 90% of what any SMALLster could ask.

A word about motors: there has been a revolution in electric motors over the past few months. A few years ago there were 3 major classes of electric motors: cheap can ferrites, moderate to expensive cobalt brushed motors, and expensive brushless motors.

Today, the cobalt brushed motor is disappearing, and inexpensive brushless motors have taken the middle ground. I think for most modeling applications, these motors make a great deal of sense.

In comparison with the geared ferrites, they offer much better performance and flexibility, along with no maintenance, for about 1.5-2x the cost of a cheap ferrite motor and junky gearbox.

I hate junky gearboxes -- to me, life is too short to deal with stripping screws, stuff that doesn't fit, noise, stripped gears, etc.

If you push the motor at all, ferrite motors need maintenance or replacement on a somewhat frequent basis -- that's also annoying.

The flexibility of the newer brushless motors is breathtaking. I have a Mega 16/15/5 (basically a motor the size and weight of a Speed 400; around 2 ounces) that outperforms both a Speed 400 and a Turbo 450 -- a motor twice its size. In terms of performance, I mean turns a prop at higher rpm at LOWER power input -- i.e. increased duration or improved performance on the same batteries and prop -- frequently both.

These brushless motors are selling for \$75 or so (between \$130-150 with controller, or about 1.5-2x the cost of a ferrite and controller.) Note that you cannot use a brushless motor with a brushed motor controller. In other words, the major investment in e-flight is not necessarily the motors. It's usually the controllers, -- so you should think carefully about what your future plans are.

Also note that the performance of the current crop of brushless motors is pretty darn good. There isn't a lot of room for dramatic improvement. In other words, motor and controller prices may fall (maybe by 25-33%) over the next couple of years, but the performance of the motors and controllers won't change all that much.

I brought up the Mega 16/15/5 because actually, I think you could use it in both the Piece of Cake and the Sky Knight. However, if you're going to buy dedicated motors, then I'd look at something a bit larger for the Skyknight -- probably a Jeti 15/4 or a Mega 22/10/5 or Mega 22/10/6, and a higher-turn Mega (maybe a 16/15/7 or 8) for the Piece of Cake. (A word about motor nomenclature: The motor manufacturer often specifies the size of the motor with the first digit or two. It's not consistent, but the last digit indicated the number of turns. This relates to how large a prop the motor can swing: higher turns = larger prop.)

I wouldn't bother with a gearbox in either case. The gearbox adds significant weight, cuts into efficiency some (gearbox losses) and a good one is at least half the cost of a motor. From my standpoint, I'd rather buy more motors than gearboxes.

Makers of good inexpensive brushless motors: I've used both Jeti and Meg; both are reasonably done. The Mega's have a much higher rpm limit than the Jeti -- useful with a gearbox, not necessary otherwise. There is also the AXI rotating can motor -- haven't tried one yet. All of these motors are available in configurations so you don't need a gearbox for most models. All of these motors in SMALL-relevant sizes are less than \$100.

Sam Brauer Stamford, Connecticut

<div class="Joe">

...John, I've used Acme tanks myself for years. However, I always unsolder one end and inspect the tank interior before using it. That's because most of the all-brass Acme tanks I've bought turned out to be far less neat-looking on the inside than they do on their exteriors.

Evidently Acme used corrosive solder flux in assembling these tanks. Two of the ones I rebuilt had substantial greenish-blue corrosion -- looking much like copper sulfate to me -- and the brass fuel tubes were etched down to paper-thin-ness. They snapped off as easily as a strand of dry spaghetti.

I took the tanks completely apart; wire-brushed off all the corrosion; then re-assembled using electronic solder with resin flux (clean brass needs nothing stronger!) and new lengths of tubing. I've had no trouble since. \[JW\]
