Howard Metcalfe \< 

 \> appreciates:

Many thanks to David Williams and Ed Baumgartner for their help in sourcing razor blades. Apparently those lovely snappable double edged ones are still available in South Africa. However I couldn't wait to mess around with fine razor blades like wot I used to cut my fingers on. I realised that I had a supply close to hand but hidden inside some 'Bic' disposable razors of the type I had only left off using a year or so ago.

5 minutes of careful surgery provided me with 3 pristine razor- shape blades (dummy, they WERE razors). The blades are 10mm x 37mm, only ..1mm (4 thousandths) thick, and ready to use without having to snap anything, as the blade edge goes right to each end.

I had forgotten how beautifully they cut balsa. No angling of the scalpel handle with these to try to get a vertical cut.

This is how you do it. Break off the front plastic edge guard and the front portion of the end protectors with pliers (the plastic breaks easily) to reveal the entire cutting edge. Keep the pliers and your fingers clear of this edge. Mount the handle in a vise at a suitable angle, so that the cutting edge is uppermost and the blade is vertical.

With a fine razor saw (how appropriate) cut down through the plastic under the blade. Keep the saw teeth away from the razor's edge by placing the saw just below it. That's not as tricky as it sounds; it takes longer to write this than to do it, that's for sure.

In reply to Ron Boucke' \< 

 \> JW wrote, "...prop oscillation during starting procedure is a CERTAIN sign of excessive compression. Remember: mini-size diesels start best with REDUCED compression; you then increase the compression to attain smooth running...."

I can't keep my nose out of it here. Joe nicely simplifies the problem but is only partially right IMHO. It is a combination of excessive compression and excessive fuel that causes propeller oscillation. The prop may oscillate for a bit; then without changing anything, the next flip of the prop may start the engine, as the excess fuel has been burnt off (or ejected through the ports).

Too much of a prime means the engine needs less compression, too little with a cold engine may even require increased compression. There seems no substitute to learning your engine by experience and developing a feel for it. Sorry guys, but then that is the charm of diesels. We do like to persevere.

Congratulations Ron, you're getting there. Keep those fingers flicking, Howard \< <http://wwwmetcalfehm.freeserve.co.uk> \>

Dave Larkin \< 

 \> amplifies:

As Joe says, if the propeller on a model diesel engine oscillates in the starting process, the motor is overcompressed. Always back the compression off half a turn after priming, and turn the engine over gently to check for flooding before flicking. It sounds as if Sam is over-priming. Prime one or two drops against the wall of the piston, not into the cylinder. I recommend using a Valvespout for fueling; preferably the little banjo one. Dave

As you are probably aware from Ken Parks' submissions, the Ottawa Radio Control Club (ORCC) has been running a club project to build the Randy Randolph design "Chips". So far, Ken has shipped out 32 "kits" to interested club members. Ken and I built the "proof of concept" versions, and have successfully flown them at the ORCC winter fun fly on February 12. (Temperature was -20 Centigrade, rising to -7C with the aid of chili and much camaraderie).

Unfortunately the only casualty during the day was my Chips. This is the second crash I have had with this model (within 2 weeks) with different receivers. The first time was with an RCD 535 mini receiver, and the second was with the Futaba 7 channel receiver that came with the Futaba Conquest radio. Both receivers were mounted in the same position in the airframe, at the top of the canopy with the antenna exiting and strung to the top of the fin.

Both aircraft spiraled in when turning onto final approach. Weather conditions both days were excellent and airspeed appeared to be acceptable. I have covered the canopy in chrome trim sheet to give a glass effect but I was wondering if the trim sheet could have some effect on receiver reception? I was under the impression that these sheets were all-plastic and so should not affect reception.

Does anyone out there have any similar experiences, or offerings of explanation?

I know temperature can adversely affect battery performance and the receiver battery voltage was down, but not in the red, when tested after the crash. This could have been a partial cause -- but could the trim sheet also be a contributing factor?

One final word about the Chips. This is an excellent flying air- craft, easy to build (and repair - I have that experience) and an all-round joy to behold. I would recommend this aircraft to anyone.

Roy L. Clough, Jr. \< 

 \> has recently joined our group, and contributed:

When I first started flying small electrics with RussKit and Revco slot car motors and the battery packs out of used Polaroid cart- ridges, I used whatever plastic rubber model props I guesstimated they would handle.

I'd mount the prop by slipping a length of black neoprene fuel line over the shaft, then drilling the prop to slip over it. The trick was to slide the prop up to the shaft, then stretch heck out of the tubing so the prop would slip over it, then cut the tubing flush. With the tubing "bushing" under compression, the prop was centered and locked in place, but could easily be pried off with a small screwdriver.

Unfortunately, when I ran out of this particular size of neoprene I was not able to replace it, so I developed an alternate method. This works great for little "camera" motors and Williams' Brothers 4 1/2" plastic props. First, wash the shaft in 91% rubbing alcohol, then cut a 1/16" long piece of 1/8" O.D. Plastruct tubing. (Try a model RR shop for that.)

Force the Plastruct "washer" over the shaft -- close to, but not binding against, the bearing. (This is a glue stop.) Now put a single drop of thin CA in the front side of the propeller hole, touch it to the shaft, then reverse the prop and push it up to the tubing sleeve, and let it set for a couple of hours. Built literally dozens this way and never had a prop loosen.

For larger motors in direct drive, from HiLine Elf size to Mabuchi 550's, I drill and bush the propeller hole with a length of Plastruct tubing, then drill and tap two 4-40 setscrew holes through the prop hub and the plastic bushing. I mount all my direct drives this way, and again, have never had a prop fly off. The reason for the Plastruct tubing bushing, instead of metal, is that in a crunch, it will deform, and generally save a motor shaft. Prop adaptors, even if they fit, are a liability in that they greatly increase the overhung leverage and make the shaft much easier to break or bend in a crash.

Although I build a lot of tractor models, pushers are my favorite for electric power. One interesting experiment was with a rebuilt Hobby Shack Spirit of '76 foam glider with a stalk-mounted Mabuchi 540.

This could be turned either way. Sure enough, there was a slight performance increase in the pusher mode. This plane gives a consistent 7-minute flight on a seven-cell 1500 mAH pack.

Speaking of 7-cell packs, I had a puzzler with a Duratrax Super 1500. I accidentally over-charged this one to the point it was so hot I couldn't hold it in my hand. I picked it up by the wires and dunked it in a hand basin full of cold water, and left it for an hour.

I thought it would be ruined, but this pack now takes a deeper, longer-lasting charge than any of the others. I thought of trying this with one of its sister packs, but I'm not sure for how long the overcharge was applied, or if this was a fluke and I'd really ruin one if I repeated the exercise.

Have fun, Roy C.

In response to the Wormwood Scrubster, I have successfully con- verted one of those Styrofoam gliders to R/C. It is powered by a TD .020, and has two sub-micro servos, a 250 mAH battery pack, and a Tetra sub-micro receiver.

I replaced the horizontal stabilizer and elevator with one made of Coreplast (available at an art supply store for about \$2 for a 16" x 24" sheet).

As you already know, the original material on the glider is very flimsy. I use a computer radio, and the control setup utilizes elevon mixing. I used some nylon strapping tape as a skid, and to reinforce the wings.

The glider is the Comet "Skyraider". It flies great. The biggest problem I have run into is that I usually break a prop on landing.

I installed a nose wheel on a piece of music wire, so the prop is not the first thing to hit the ground on landing any more.

Mark Wayne

In Posting \#285, (coming up to the third century, Joe), Tim Dannels wonders whether one of his McCoy .098s will fly his "Zilch" at 8,000 feet asl. You're up against three things there, Tim:

1\. At that altitude the engine can't suck in as much oxygen with each "in breath", so to speak, so won't produce as much power as it will at 1,000 feet.

2\. The propellor is not as efficient in the thinner air.

3\. The wing won't produce as much lift. I would suggest putting BOTH of those little engines on the airplane!! Speaking of which, in my own Yo-yo days, before I got hooked on R/C, I built a P-38 from a kit (by Sterling if I remember rightly), a profile model for two .049s. The Black Sheep club in Burbank, California has been flying indoor and outdoor C/L Electric scale for just about 20 years, and have shown them off at the IMS show in Pasadena for many years. They generally consist of a plain old Guillow Kit WWII fighter powered by an Astro 020 with 4 to 6 cells on board. Using a 6" prop and a normal charge time gives us a good 3 minute motor run; and at the show and in the gym we fly on 16' lines. Yeh, short lines -- that's why only three minutes. We'd be able to fly only once a night otherwise.

The models fly pretty well and are a lot of fun if you fly combat, 'cause they don't really have enough power to do a good wingover or loop. The aerobatics are reserved for the smaller lightweight custom jobs with thin wings and lots of control area -- but must use large battery packs to fly the whole pattern.

Way back when I set the USA speed record for C/L electrics at the Electric Champs at Mile Square park at 36 MPH, it was a 15" span with an Astro 020 in the nose. The record has since gone way past that, but then we have learned so much more since then.

"If it does not work that way, try another way; eventually you will get it right" L. Bleriot.

Happy flying with good friends, Carlo

In the last posting it seems that I inadvertently caused some confusion. First off, I think the motor/gearbox/battery power system in the Hobby Lobby Fokker Triplane is inadequate.

Second, I've been in contact with Pat Tritle, the designer of the Sopwith Triplane I am building, and he put in a strong suggestion to use electric instead of glow to power the model. Looking at the size of the cowling, which would require at least a 7 inch prop to have some effective blade area outside, I concurred with Pat's recommendation. His recommended motor in the Sopwith Tripe is a Speed 400 with a 2.33:1 gearbox, swinging a 9-inch (maybe a 10- inch?) prop. I have a hunch that this Triplane would be under- powered with a geared Speed 280.

Since I wanted to lighten up the airplane some (the structure is quite light) I talked to Kirk Massey at New Creations R/C about going to a Speed 300 motor instead of the Speed 400. In order to swing an 8-inch prop it's necessary to use a 5:1 gearbox to keep the current load within reason. The problem is that the gearbox available, a Horst unit, is molded to fit on a Speed 280.

I took a Dremel and ground the interior of the gearbox to fit the Speed 300 -- a task which took about 5 minutes. (Pull the labels off the motor too). The gearbox is not brilliant, but may suffice for the job at hand. Having now tested the unit in hand (I don't really recommend this) it certainly has good pull using an 8-cell 350 mAH nickel metal hydride pack. I may also fly the airplane with a 7-cell 600 AE nicad pack as well.

If anyone is contemplating building this airplane, I certainly recommend buying the kit. The laser cutting of the ribs alone makes the price of the kit worthwhile -- there are an awful lot of ribs in a Triplane! However, this is not an airplane for someone who has not built quite a few other planes.

The plans are sketchy with very little detail. There are basically NO construction notes, so you're on your own. I'll let you guys know how it flies when finished -- probably another month or so.

Sam Brauer
