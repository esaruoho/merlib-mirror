> 1894 -- US: At the first regular convention of the American Railway Union, delegates vote unanimously to urge a boycott of Pullman cars. 
>
> > The union is acting in sympathy with workers at the Pullman Company who have been on strike since May 10. 
> >
> > The boycott begins June 26, when switchmen on a number of lines out of Chicago refuse to switch Pullman cars. They are instantly fired, leading other workers to walk off in protest. Soon virtually all 26 roads out of Chicago will be paralyzed & all transcontinental lines stopped. 
> >
> > The struggle extends to 27 states. An estimated 260,000 railroad workers will join the strike which is eventually crushed by federal troops who take over the city of Chicago.

1925 -- <span id="CapeBreton"></span>Canada: During a mine workers strike against the British Empire Steel Corporation (BESCO) in Cape Breton, drunken company police charge on horseback beating all who stood in their path, then ride through the school yards, knocking down innocent children while joking that the miners are at home hiding under their beds.

A company president had sneered,

> > "We hold the cards, \
> > they will crawl back to work..."

\


A gathering of angry strikers was charged by a wall of armed company thugs on horseback. One miner was wounded & trampled by horses. Gilbert Watson was shot in the stomach. William Davis was fatally shot through the heart.

The striking workers swarmed the power plant, overpowered company police & marched them off to the town jail. For several nights afterward, the coal towns were under a state of seige by the miners. They raided the company stores to feed their starving families & then burned the stores to the ground to eliminate the last symbol of corporate greed & servitude in the Cape Breton coal fields. The company stores never re-opened after the coal wars of 1925. 

The miners promised no man would ever again work the black seam on what is now called Davis Day. They have kept their promise to this day. In local coal mining communities, many store owners still close their doors in respect for deceased coal miners & children take time from their studies to reflect with their families.

The collieries are not worked and the day is observed as a civic holiday in the mining towns. A monument in Davis Square tells the story of the 1925 strike.

1970 -- US: The Helix, Seattle's first underground newspaper, folds.

> Paul Dorpat & associates published the first edition of Helix & readers quickly snapped up the first 1,500 copies of the 12-page, multi-colored "counter culture" tabloid.
>
> The Helix grew out of discussions at the Free University of Seattle, an alternative college in the University District, & reflected the rapid rise of "underground newspapers" such as The Berkeley Barb, San Francisco's Oracle, & New York's East Village Other.
>
> In addition to Dorpat, Tom Robbins (Another Roadside Attraction), Gene Johnston, Ray Collins (illustrator), Scott White, & Gary Finholt contributed to the first issue.
>
> Helix published a total of 125 biweekly & weekly editions before folding today.
>
> See Walt Crowley, Rites of Passage: A Memoir of the Sixties in Seattle (Seattle: University of Washington, 1995). <http://www.historylink.org/output.cfm?file_ID=1990%0B%0B>\

> In September 1969 Local 598 Fisher Body workers took on GM in the longest strike by the UAW in GM history.

> In September of this year it becomes nationwide. The 67 day strike at General Motors in the Fall of 1970 is a classic example of the anti-employee nature of the conventional strike, perfectly illustrative of the ritualized manipulation of the individual which is repeated so often & which changes absolutely nothing about the nature of work.
