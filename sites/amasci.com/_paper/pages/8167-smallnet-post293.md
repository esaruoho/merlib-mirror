 \> replies to this query:

    > I recently got a small color video-camera and video-transmitter 

    > to install in a plane.  The flight pack is about 50 grams, so I will

    > have to see how small an airplane-engine that can take this payload.

    > Anyone out there with experience of live aerial video?

I have been flying small remotely-piloted R/C models for the last 5 years. There are many things to consider when attempting aerial video work, and the most important ones are: what is your goal for the system?

Do you want to fly remotely through the video, or just record aerial images? What range do you want? The answers to these questions affect system size and cost dramatically. In your case you already have a video system.

First you must familiarize yourself with its operation on the bench. Make sure you can properly supply power to the camera, video transmitter, and video receiver. Do you see a picture? Do you have the correct antennas? On the airplane, I use a dipole antenna oriented vertically. My ground station uses either a monopole antenna or a directional one aimed at the aircraft. Antenna type has the biggest effect on system range, so make sure you have the correct equipment (and licenses) for your frequency. You will also have to set up a ground station that can be operated in the field.

I use a small battery-powered Sony 8mm VCR with built-in screen, but it costs \$900 for this! The ground station with VCR recorder can easily cost more than the airplane.

If your bench tests are successful, try operating the video equipment near your radio system. First do a range test with the transmitter antenna collapsed. Then repeat with the video system operating near the radio system. If the range is reduced more than 25% I would not fly the system. You can experiment with shielding the video transmitter board or physically separating the components, but the only solutions that have worked well for me are: (1) Use a PCM radio system, (2) Separate the power supplies for the video and radio systems, (3) buy video equipment on a different frequency. (I use 2.4 GHz.)

After the range tests, test your camera outdoors. Most CCD and CMOS cameras are sensitive to Infrared frequencies and if your camera lens doesn't have the correct coating, it won't work properly outdoors. You can buy an IR cut-off filter from Edmund Scientific and glue it to the camera lens if needed.

If all this works, find a stable, easy-to-fly airplane that can lift the video equipment. Install the camera so that it can just see the horizon in level flight (you want to see mostly downward). Ground test all your equipment again for radio range, vibration, oil on the camera lens, etc..

On your first flights transmit and record the video but don't try flying via the camera. Watch the video tape several times to learn what flying looks like through the camera. Learn key navigation points around your flying field. For your first flights through the video camera, have a spotter watching the plane at all times, and stay within 1/4 mile! It takes time to learn to fly and navigate. Do not attempt any aerobatics or rapid turns, because you will get disoriented!

Remember that video systems need a clear line of sight between the transmit and receive antennas, so don't fly where trees, buildings, etc. will block the signal path.

I could probably write a book on this whole subject, but these are the key points.

I hope this helps, Steve Morris\

Dealing with some of Lars Pettersson's questions:

There is no aftermarket carb available now for the Cox 049. Ben Tarnofski, who made the Tarno carb, ceased manufacturing a long while ago, and, sadly, is no longer with us.

I experimented with the 049 Tarno carb in a many-stage experiment in the late 1970s. I started with a Medallion 049 with a TD front housing to take the Tarno. Then I fitted a QRC piston/cylinder with muffler. This worked well, but the idle was too fast for taxiing.

So I added a head that was tapped out for an idle bar plug. This knocked quite a bit of power off the top (which I didn't need for the Boddington design I was flying -- a 32 inch Micro Super, I think -- but did produce the required idle.

About 4 or 5 years ago Cox produced a nice little engine called the TD 05 R/C, which had a neat carb and a torpedo muffler. It ran well and throttled nicely down to about 5,000 rpm. The muffler was not very effective in limiting noise or reducing gunge, as there was an annular space between the cylinder and the muffler manifold.

I did a side-by side test of the TD 05 R/C and the then-new PAW 049 R/C diesel. The performance of the two engines was almost identical on smaller props (6-inch) with the PAW starting to pull away on 7-inch and bigger props; much better on an 8-inch prop. The Cox throttled more smoothly than the PAW, but the PAW went down to a lower idle. I'm writing from memory (a bit dangerous these days) but can dig up the test data and mail it if requested.

I think if you could scare up another Tarno 049 carb, you could throttle your TD well enough for a Lazy Bee. Alternatively, hunt for a Cox TD 05. Or buy a PAW 049 R/C and go diesel. I've never flown my Cox TD 05 R/C. I am quite satisfied with my PAW 049 R/C in my Tomboy, which has also been the home of earlier PAW 80 and 100 engines, including the 80 Classic and the 100 Classic.

Another alternative, of course is the Norvel.

VA has produced a very nice R/C version of his 049 Mk II with a transverse muffler at the back. But this is only in prototype form at the moment, and he is being encouraged to develop it with a less complicated piston design and with an airbleed on the carb. Don't look for it for a while.

By the way VA's latest reproduction design is a 0.5 cc, (0.03 cubic inches for the metrically-challenged) ED 2.46 Racer. There is also an 049 cubic inch Racer made by somebody else in Russia and available from Tomas Mejlik; and this one, unlike the smaller VA one, has ball bearings (Woody Bartelt of Aero Electric has a few of the 049 versions in stock).

Those who used the ED Racer in the past will remember that it was a marvellous engine, very user-friendly and powerful for its day (at least until the Oliver Tiger came along). If you don't recall the Racer - it looked like a McCoy racing engine but had exhaust stacks on both sides, and had 360 degree circumferential porting. It had rear disc induction. Very smart with a black crankcase and red cylinder fins. And of course, a compression lever, though an optional glow head was available. I have an 049 version, but I haven't run it yet.

For the Brits, the VA 0.5cc Racer will be available from Dave Banks. As a side note, Mejlik had the mini-replica Racer made at the suggestion of Richard Barlow, of Toronto, who supplied him with an original Racer as a pattern.

I was most interested to see Tom Anderson's comments on the Wasp .061 Chinese clone of the Norvel.

Back to Lars - Kavan make nice flexible props in 6 X 4, 7 X 4 and 8 X 4 sizes. There are two versions, yellow and black, the latter being a little stiffer and stronger. Irvine distribute them. I don't think the soft Cox props are made any more. Cox used to make the Conquest .15 and it is now available from RJL/Mecoa. But it is a very different type of engine to the TDs. I couldn't recall whether a throttled Medallion .15 was marketed, but I'll take your word for it Joe, certainly there were 049 and 09 Medallion R/Cs with a rotating butterfly valve/spraybar interlinked with an exhaust restrictor in the 1960s.

Don't be put off by Lars' comments on the West Coast of Ireland. Certainly the weather is changeable, but when it is good, it has to be one of the most beautiful places. When I left Ireland to join the RCAF I gave my second Tomboy (of the five or six I have built) to a friend, and it flew away with its floats to meet a watery grave somewhere off the coast of Kerry.

In theory, the Veron Cardinal kit is still available from Amerang in England. But in practice, I haven't been able to get hold of a kit and I've been trying for a couple of years for a customer. I've tried both Irvine and Flair as sources. Hobby Supplies South used to list it, but it is a long time since they had any. I have a Cardinal in my basement, almost completed by my son some 15 years ago. When I'm finished with moving house, to Picton Ontario, I may re-finish it in Airspan and fly it, with a DC Merlin for power.

Best regards, Dave

....Greg, each model has its own optimum propeller: size, pitch, and style. The 6-3 Top Flite prop I use on my Starling (now about 23 years old, and due for retirement) provides the best climb of any prop I've put on its Pee Wee. The "stock" Pee Wee 4 1/2 inch prop turns up about 5000 rpm faster on my old Pee Wee than the 6-3.

This brings up a topic that nobody seems to have said anything about on SMALLnet: the "modern trend" to use powerful, fast-revving engines in R/C models -- and equip them with inefficient propellers.

I can see the reason for that. If the lowest reliable idle speed you can

achieve is 5000 rpm, then you want a prop that delivers very little thrust at that speed.

That approach doesn't seem quite logical to me -- but I guess it's little different from owning a car with a 400 hp engine, and using that for commuting to work and shopping trips to the mall. \[JW\]

I have a Cox 051 with a carb in a Pilot "Baby". I had the same problem with throttling as Lars describes. Here is a cure. Find a Cox .049 exhaust restrictor throttle; ACE sold them once.

Install the exhaust throttle along with the carb; run a wire from the exhaust throttle to the carb wire. Use a small wheel collar to fasten them together so both are adjustable, and go.

I know it sounds complicated, but it really isn't. If anyone wants a diagram, E-mail me and I will send them one. If you use this setup, the little 051 will act like a 40.

Phillip T. Boyd

....Mark, the weakness of the Davis Diesel conversion head for Coxes is the teflon disk that serves as a sort of contrapiston. That simply CANNOT stand up for long to the temperatures and pressures in the combustion chamber. That's why you get so many "extra disks" with the Davis Cox diesel conversion kits.

However, Davis's CO2 conversions of Cox engines are mighty good! For a while they weren't available. I heard that was for lack of a good charging valve. Bob Davis had been buying those from Bill Brown -- but after Bill had his stroke in 1996, production of those valves stopped. Evidently Bob Davis has found a new source, because he's advertising his Cox CO2 conversions again. \[JW\]

At long last I've finished my Clancy Aviation Yard Bee. I covered the wing with Micafilm and the fuselage with dope-n-tissue.

This is the first time that I have used tissue on wood, and even though there's too much dope, the effect is beautiful!!! The rudder and elevator were sewn on. A Hitec AM three-channel radio, three FMA S80 servos and an SR Batteries 150 mAH Receiver pack. For power I chose the premier SMALL engine; a PAW .03 R/C.

For an assortment of reasons my Yard Bee is heavy (14 ounces). However, the 03 can turn a Cox 7x3.5 at almost 10,000 RPM, (with Aerodyne fuel).

This little plane shot out of my hand and tore across the sky. The control throws available without a computer radio are a bit much, so the plane is "Responsive". When I flew Dereck Woodward's Speed 400 Yard Bee it was a tame event. I had hoped for a schoolyard flyer. We will see. To slow this thing down I got my hands on some 8x3 BY&O props.

Question, when I was breaking in my PAW .03 on a test stand I used a Cox 6x3 (13,000 RPM) and did not worry about idle. After several 1/2 ounce tanks of PAW 23% castor fuel, I tried a Cox 7x3.5 (9,300 RPM) with a 3,500 RPM idle. Perfect, I thought. The other day in my back yard using Aerodyne fuel and the BY&O 8x3 prop I got 9,300 RPM at full throttle and an idle of 5,000 RPM???

When I first started the engine, maximum speed was 9,100 RPM and about 4,000 RPM idle. That would die off after a few seconds. After a couple of restarts I tried leaning the mixture at top speed. The leaner mixture got me a couple of hundred RPM on the top and a very steady idle at 5,000+ RPM. At this idle the throttle barrel was COMPLETELY CLOSED.

I had the compression set so that with a 1/16 turn less compression and it would "cackle", 1/8 turn less compression and it would misfire. Why won't it idle nice any more???

Oily in Maryland, Francis S. Person

<div class="Joe">

....Francis, I've had the same problem. Mine was caused by an air leak at the throttle-to-case junction. Make SURE that the O-ring under the carb body is tightly compressed; and that the setscrews are nice and firm. (Not TOO tight: the wall thickness of the lower portion of the throttle body is quite thin.)

One other possibility: the spraybar fuel orifices may be misaligned. With the throttle wide open, look carefully down the barrel in good light. You should NOT be able to see even the edge of a fuel orifice. If you can, make the necessary adjustment... \[JW\]

</div>
