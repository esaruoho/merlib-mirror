Frank Hodson wrote: "I build a lot of electrics, both FF and R/C. Motor shafts never seem to fit the props. The best thing I've found to use as shim/spacer is soft aluminum tubing. Cut tubing a hair shorter than the hub thickness, then split lengthwise..."

Frank -- getting a prop onto an electric motor at any size from a biddy little FF to a quarter-scale giant is a bear! Everything revolves around the dreaded "Prop Adapter", a device almost guaran- teed to not quite fit well and be slightly out of dynamic balance.

I just did a kit review of a high-end brushless powered sports model, and the prop adapter I ended up using cost close to half the cost of the kit! While you can be cut some slack when dealing with the throbbing power of a 3-cell FF rig and a 4" prop, by the time you hit Speed 400, you better get it right!

If anyone wants to mess with fitting one guy's stuff to another's widgets, Astro make the best gearboxes going, as you end up bolting a prop on the front just like the engine boys have done for years. There's been rumours of folk fitting Astro 020 brushless boxes to S400s, but it requires some "fudging", and Astro pinions need hefty pushers and pullers to get off and push on.

Mostly, getting a prop onto an electric so it all can do an honest day's aviating is going to take that cussed determination I mentioned earlier.

Someone asked about the use of water-miscible varnish in lieu of dope. I have used that successfully for a few things. The last time I re-covered my original Hanger Rat, I misted a light coat of varnish mixed 50/50 with water, and it looks like it's going to last forever compared to dope. That may be because I crushed the tissue this time, so I have no shrinkage to deal with.

Another project where I can recommend water-tinned "varnish": I built a small pod & boom glider a few years ago by sanding a block of foam to shape and using a mix of varnish and water to laminate several layers of Silkspan over the plug, just like glassing it. That works great for compound-curved sections. When dry it sands to a fine finish, and I used hardware store spraypaint for a final finish with no problems.

Cheers, Tom from Buzzard Bluff

....Tom, I tried using Artists' Acrylic Gloss Medium & Varnish on various tissue-covered test panels a few years ago. The stuff went on very well, both brushed and airbrushed. And when dry the finish looked marvelous. However, after a week or so of "curing", the tissue became as brittle as eggshells.

But I have used Acrylic Medium in the same way you suggested, as a "fiberglass substitute". So has Emmett Fry. But water-thinned finishes -- "varnishes" and "urethanes" -- don't work well on thin, previously unfinished sheet balsa. Ron Boucke' \< 

 \> effervesces:

Drum roll please.....trumpets.......hit it! From the ashes of disaster grow the roses of success, a statement right out of Chitty Chitty Bang Bang! Yes, my PAW 55 started and ran out the entire tank; not only that but I got repeat performance on the second tank :\>) MANY thanks to all of the advice from the diesel gang.

It's always best to step back and review every step, every process, and record what you do. The first problem was my aching back, literally! The test stand I was using is fine for a quick start and run; but this engine took a lot longer to get going. Result, a nagging back ache being bent over the darn thing. That's a great way to siphon off your patience! Got myself a block of wood, 1 x 2, and quickly fashioned a mount for the engine and secured it in a Panavise with the large round heavy base.

Second, I got myself a chair to sit down in and work on the engine. I discarded the film canister fuel tank and found on old metal 1/4 ounce square tank in my collection. I used a small diameter neoprene fuel line to fit the small brass tube on the tank and had to insert that into a larger short piece of Tygon to fit the PAW.

O.K...NV closed, and a tiny drop of fuel at the exhaust, I began working the compression screw until I received a slight bark. I tried to repeat that bark until they were predictable. O.K. now; fill the tank. Open the needle two turns and draw the fuel just up to the spraybar. BTW, I reset the sprayer holes facing downward, both of them. One small drop of fuel to the closed exhaust port, a couple of flicks and bingo, a rough- but continuous-running engine.

It was now running strong enough to be able to play with compression and fuel. Final NV setting came to 1.75 turns open. Restarts were not immediate; I had to back off the compression screw a good half turn; and in the flicking process, she did some kind of razzle-dazzle with the prop, a back and forth kinda thing.

Ultimately, she started again. The temperature outside was in the teens. It didn't seem to bother ole PAW. Sorry for the long blurb, but I was excited about this one, folks!

Regards, Ron

<div class="Joe">

....Ron, prop oscillation during starting procedure is a CERTAIN sign of excessive compression. Remember: mini-size diesels start best with REDUCED compression; you then increase the com- pression to attain smooth running. (It usually takes a couple of alternate adjustments between needle and compression screw to obtain the smoothest run AND the best idling reliability.) \[JW\]

</div>

Dan Arnold \< 

 \> suggests:

In Posting \#285 it was noted that some props don't produce adequate thrust until they are "dressed" with sandpaper. About fifteen years ago there were some articles in the model airplane press about how to install triangular stock "stall strips" on the outer 1/3 of wing leading edges to make pattern planes snap roll more easily.

Perhaps the mold flash or factory standard sharp leading edges are stalling the prop's airfoil.

Dan Arnold, Charleston, South Carolina

<div class="Joe">

....Quite right, Dan! The leading edges of all model props should be smoothly radiused, such that the front of the blade airfoil is half-round. Added efficiency (perhaps 5%) can be had by sanding the FRONT surface of the blades in a tip-to-tip direction, with something like 360 grit abrasive. The idea is to provide "turbu- lator scratches" approximately parallel to the blade leading edges.

Keep the undersides glassy smooth, however -- and DON'T radius the underside of the blade trailing edges ! Especially on APC props, rounding over the blade trailing edges can cause noticeable loss of thrust. \[JW\]

</div>
