<td data-bgcolor="#FFFFE1"><strong>H</strong>igh wage-earning, legally married couples have complained about the “marriage penalty” for years. In 2001, the United States Congress passed new tax laws to lessen, and eventually remove, the penalty. The new law will be phased in, beginning in the tax year 2005, and the penalty will be completely eliminated in 2009.
<p>The income tax penalty for being married is due to the structure of the tax brackets, as well as the amount of the standard deduction.</p>
<p>Basically, if you place the financial information for two parties on a joint return, it results in a tax that is often higher than the sum of the individual’s returns. When a couple has only one breadwinner, however, the joint return can frequently prove advantageous.</p>
<p><br />
<strong>Consequences for Same-Sex Couples</strong></p>
<p>This year, I had clients, life partners, who played every rule to their advantage. Also, they were able to partake of some tax saving due to one of them being the second-parent adoptive mom of her partner’s child. However, they were still disadvantaged because they did not have the right to marry.</p>
<p>Each partner must file Form 1040 as a “single” individual. One is a stay-at-home-mom and earns less than $2,900. The other mom is the high earner, and can claim her partner and as well as the child as dependents.</p>
<p>The high-earning mom can take a deduction credit for some childcare costs, since the homemaker’s lack of income doesn’t rule out the credit, as it would on a joint return.</p>
<p>Since they are not married, the breadwinner can file as head-of-household and use lower tax rates than she would, had she filed as “single.”</p>
<p>Finally, since the filing requirement threshold is higher than the homemaker’s income, the homemaker doesn’t need to file at all. Her income goes completely untaxed, whereas it would be taxed on the joint return for a married couple.</p>
<p>With all such legal advantages it sounds like the lesbian couple would pay less tax than a married couple. Unfortunately, this particular couple will pay $1,980 more taxes this year than a married couple with the same financial setup!</p>
<p>This is primarily because the joint-filing married couple is able to earn more income in the lower tax brackets than the head-of-household category. The head-of-household rates are better than the single rates, but not as good as the married joint-filing rates.</p>
<p>So, use your tax strategies wisely, but beware of the “<em>non</em>-marriage penalty.” Legal marriage has advantages!</p>
