## 2004 Books

Current book is at the top and the rest of the list is in chronological order, top to bottom. This is my current year's reading, to date. It will be updated as I get to it. I'm not reading as many books as usual, this year, as my current schedule just doesn't permit reading more than one a week, if that. I haven't included pure reference books or magazines in this list.

And, yes, it's been quite a while (July 2--Seabiscuit) since I updated this page, and may be a while, again, since I'm travelling a lot in October and will be very busy with several projects. I'll do my best to keep this updated more regularly, though.

\
\

### Reading right now

*The Wizard Hunters*, by Martha Wells. The first book of the Fall of Ile-Riene series by the author who wrote *The Death of the Necromancer*. So far, as charming and quirky and outright weird as Necromancer, but apparently about 20 years after the events of that book. This one features Nicholas Valiard's daughter and a war related to the events of the previous book. Pretty interesting, so far, but I'm not reading very fast....

\

### Completed

*The Anubis Gates*, by Tim Powers. (Started at Christmas, but finished at New Years.) As always, beautiful pace and language in this tale of time-travel and sorcery spiced with ancient Egypt and Samuel Taylor Coleridge. Terrific stuff!

*The Handyman*, by Carolyn See. Odd little "literary" book about an young artist finding himself, and a lot of other people, in Los Angeles. Not as funny as I'd been led to hope for, but still very well done. An easy and satisfying read about a decent guy and the mind of an artist in development. I do wish the painter actually existed, though, as the descriptions of the paintings are delightful and I'd love to own some of "Bob's" work if I had the space.

*Death of a Stranger*, by Anne Perry. Looks like the last of the William Monk novels. Not bad, but a disappointing contrivance of coincidence, and determined blindness to a very obvious clue in order to create a high-tension, thriller ending. The first book was the best and while this started out with some of the same feeling, it didn't pay off as well in the end, as the emotional devices re-used began to feel like mere repetition rather than recall.

*The Syndic*, by C. M. Kornbluth. Much like his collaboration with Frederick Pohl on *The Space Merchants*. Stylish and political with some really oddball characters. Enjoyable, but not to be taken too seriously.

*Portofino*, by Frank Schaeffer. Literary fiction written by a screenwriter about being a born-again Christian kid on vacation in Italy in the 1960s. Gag-worthy. What started out as a loping, mildly intriguing story didn't get interesting until page 150, then came to an end, but not a resolution. I found the narrator a decent enough human that I wondered what will become of him, but the premise and his repulsive, abusive, fundamentalist family and the pointlessly maundering story turned me off too thoroughly to bother with any other books by this author. Has nice turns of phrase, but what small humor and descriptive charm I found in it was insufficient against the rest of the dreadful slog.

*Do Unto Others*, by Jeff Abbot. The first Jordan Poteet mystery. A decent, small-town cosy featuring an unusual lead for a cosy: a male librarian who is straight and under 40. Not bad, but got a little histrionic near the end with the sort of small-town revelations you expect from a pot-boiler. Good writing, though. Nice.

*Altered Carbon*, by Richard K. Morgan. An excellent, hardboiled Tech Noir detective-thriller from an up-and-coming British author (and he's my age!). A violent, adrenaline rush of a book with the grim beauty of a John Woo film and the gritty linguistic style of Raymond Chandler with the censor turned off. Similar techno-premise and set-up to David Brin's *Kiln People* but without Brin's social conscience showing through. Since the underlying premise allows people to die and be "re-sleeved" in new bodies, whole new vistas of psychological and social horror are possible for the plot complications and the author uses his tech very well. Solid, believable world (with a few small exceptions) and a lead character who could have been despicable, but had me rooting for him every step of the way. Morgan has the art of suggesting the most gruesome and horrific details by *omission and suggestion* down to a deliciously awful art. On the down-side, the author does pull a little slight-of-hand with a couple of plot details at the end, which don't qualify as Deus ex Machina, but they do stop the reader from putting all the pieces together for themselves before the narrator does. While reading this, it seemed to me that Morgan is still refining some of his technical writing skills and style, so there are places where the seams show a little, which is the only thing keeping this book from earning glowing kudos from me. Apparently, this is the first in a series and I'll be looking for more from this writer. However, if you don't like rough language, explicit, filthy sex and violence, don't read this book! It's raw.

*Buzz Monkey*, by Sam Hill. More oddball mystery from a brand new author. Twisty, fun, well-paced, introduces a charming series character--Top Kiernan--and his crew, but has a few first-novel, first-in-series flaws. <a href="nfiction/BuzzMonkey.html" target="blank">Reviewed.</a>

*Lost in a Good Book*, by Jasper Fforde. Second of the Thursday Next series of humorous, surreal, literary mysteries. Pretty good, except for some too-obvious and hard-stretched puns on the names of the doomed (Agents Phodder and Kannon? please...) and not quite as roaringly funny as the first. Leaves a huge, dangling hook of "To Be Continued" at the end, but, over all, delightful. Not quite the stellar dash of the first book, but it would be hard to top that one, though, the cast of characters from out of actual and fictional books is so delightful, it's hard to cast even the shadow of an aspersion. I'll be looking out for a copy of the next one--*The Well of Lost Plots*--when it's released in trade paper, here, in a year or so. (Can't afford more hardbound and, besides, anticipation is good.)

*Hardcase*, by Dan Simmons. Straight hardboiled noir crime. Not as good as a Richard Stark novel, to whom the book is dedicated. Better than OK, but not as spectacular as I'd hoped. Still, if I hadn't read it after Altered Carbon and Buzz Monkey, I'd rate it higher.

*Better Off Dead*, by Katy Munger. Either the last or second-to last of the Casey Jones mysteries. Decent mystery, with a tendency to produce slightly improbable post-facto explanations, but mostly good and satisfying. Not as edgy as *Bad to the Bone*.

*North of Nowhere*, by Steve Hamilton. Second of the Alex McKnight series. A hardboiled whodunit on the shores of Lake Michigan filled with a cast of unlikable characters headed by an unlikable hero in a plot which goes on too long with predictable twists. Not outright bad, but not good enough to pursue more of.

*Audios Muchachos*, by Daniel Chavarria, translated by Carlos Lopez. A weird tale of an ambitious young woman in Havana in 1969 who turns to a clever twist on prostitution to find a way to a better life outside Cuba, and ends up entangled in sex, business and a dead body. Goofily charming in places, very racy, often outrageous and definitely not what one expects. Although my Spanish is not good enough to be certain, I suspect it lost a little in translation. A few characters break down a bit late in the story and there are some clumsy sentences and constructions here and there, but, over all it's not bad. And it has a happy ending-- just not the one you expect.

*The Cabinet of Curiosities*, by Douglas Preston and Lincoln Child. Seventh of the Preston/Child collaborations, of which I've never read any of the previous. An annoying book. Starts out intriguing and realistic with the discovery of a long-hidden serial murder site in modern New York and a search for the historic killer, but quickly becomes improbable, far-fetched and openly manipulative of the reader. Very long with 150 pages of repetitive description and action at the end. And yet I wanted to read the whole damned thing and stayed up until 03:30 PST to do so! Commercial fiction at its alcoholic best; you know it's awful but you just can't stop drinking it. A book for people who *must* read, even if there's nothing *to* read.

*The Madness Season*, by C.S. Friedman. Science Fiction set in a future in which Humankind--and most other species throughout the Universe--have been subjugated by an alien race which shares a collective mind and has little use for or understanding of "individuals." Rather good with several interesting plot twists on vampire stories, aliens and cultural assimilation. Not the ending I expected at the beginning of the book, but pretty much the only likely one by the end, which gave it a slightly pat feel right at the end, but not a major complaint. Pretty decent SF, but not earth-shaking.

*Damned Lies and Statistics*, by Joel Best. A non-fiction book on thinking critically about statistics. Accessible writing and an easy style, but essentially pretty light social chiding about our tendency to think of statistics as fetishistic objects with a magical power, to engage in innumeracy and allow ourselves to believe what we should think about more critically just because the numbers are large or seem to come from authority. Occasionally, the examples show up a certain fuzzy imprecision in basic literacy as well as numeracy which ought to upset a few more people than it probably does. After all, as the author points out through the math, "the number of shootings doubled from 1954 to 1984" means that the number was twice as large in 1984 as in 1954, not that the number grew geometrically each year for 30 years. Good book for shaking naivete loose, but sadly not very available and not likely to find itself in the hands of everyone who ought to read it.

*The No. 1 Ladies' Detective Agency*, by Alexander McCall Smith. First of the Precious Ramotswe series. Very sweet mystery novel of connected short stories to form a longer arc. Smith's narrative style reminds me a bit of Kipling, as un-PC as that may be and his feeling for Africa comes through clearly in his loving descriptions of the land and people . Over all, a pleasant and satisfying read.-- On longer reflection, I find I'm not as satisfied with this book as I'd like. Certainly not the hilarious novel it's often represented as and considerably more melancholy than I had expected from the reviews. Some of the protagonist's observations and thoughts are ironic and quirky, but the humor often plays on rather black themes and observations which are not particularly deep.

*Smoke and Mirrors*, by Neil Gaiman. Short stories by the author of the *Sandman* graphic novels and *American Gods*. Weird. I find Gaiman rather disquieting, funny in stretches which make the darkness of his fantasy all the more uncomfortable to me, rousing unpleasant memories of all my childhood nightmares of improbable and even funny things amid the horror. Probably shouldn't have read the whole thing in one big rush as it left me a little appalled and disturbed. He has a wonderful mastery of form, however, mixing various difficult poetic forms with his prose forms, tossing fairytales and folktales on their heads with a strong sense of the macabre and the dreadful, stirred with a Lovecraftian spoon. A couple of the stories were familiar, read in American magazine reprints and not remembered until I saw them again. Rather creepy stuff, really.

*The Well of Lost Plots*, by Jasper Fforde. Better than the second one, but beginning to labor the concept which made the first book so much fun. Still good, but Fforde is flexing his world out of line with his original premise and it's not holding my attention as well, as a result.

*Virtual Light*, by William Gibson. Not one of his best. Disjointed tale of largely unsympathetic characters which doesn't really get its act together until about page 150 of 300. Slapped-up, weak ending with an epilogue which indicates Gibson is more interested in his wild-cat world of the Golden Gate Bridge than in the story he built it for. Not crap--some interesting ideas--but his language is a little tired and neither the story, nor the tech is anything special in Gibson-land.

*Death and the Penguin*, by Andrey Kurkov, translated by George Bird. A Ukranian writer of obituaries and his pet penguin get into trouble when one of the file-obits actually runs. Odd. Very odd. I suspect that the cultural humor lost a little in the translation as I found the book more weirdly melancholy than funny and (this is a spoiler) I was disappointed not to know what became of the penguin! (I liked the penguin, best.)

*Hearse of a Different Color*, by Tim Cockey. Baltimore undertaker Hitchcock Sewell's second outing among the untimely dead. I needed a laugh.... Unfortunately, I didn't really get one. Perhaps I didn't see the funny bits but, aside from some clever rejoinders, there was nothing funny going on and most of the dialog and characters were neither new nor funny, merely mildly amusing at best and rather predictable at worst. Hitch is a clever character, but the book is only average. Not bad, just average Mystery genre fiction.

*The Guards*, by Ken Bruen. Irish Crime Noir. Bruen, a former English teacher, plays with the traditional structure of writing very well and the literary references, alone, are mightily impressive, though you don't have to have read the authors referenced (or James Joyce) to get most of them. A spare and stark book, carved of words as much as written. Grim, but very well-executed. Staunchly noir, bleak and Irish and, like the Irish, suddenly funny and beautiful among the dread.

*Broken Angels*, by Richard K. Morgan. Second of the Takeshi Kovacs novels. More in the vein of a SF military adventure than the Hadboiled genre of the previous book. It's pretty good, but not as good as the first one. The beginning drags a little, pacing is not as tight and the sense of amazement brought on by the first book never really develops. Lots of tech, lots of political and social philosophy, a bit of sex and rather less violence, though it's just as gruesome when it occurs as in the first book. Bit of a twist at the end, but, the book felt a bit like it was written to a formula developed in the first one, but without the fine edge to Morgan's razor. Still sharp and not in any wise a bad book, but even the revelations about Martians which drive the final third don't carry the compelling weight of the events of the first book and Kovacs is not as consistent. Here's hoping book three--due out in a year or more in the UK--will be a bit out of the mold.

*To Say Nothing of the Dog* by Connie Willis. My second time around with this time-travelling farce of Victorian manners. Oxford Historians use the Temporal Net which featured in Willis' award-winning *Doomsday Book* to hunt for the elusive Bishop's Bird Stump. If you've read Jerome K. Jerome's *Three Men in a Boat* and liked it, you'll find this hilarious. I do. Still funny after all these years.

*The Whole Wide World*, by Paul McAuley. A Science Fiction murder mystery/thriller set in a dystopic future London suffering from the aftermath of "the Info Wars" and broad censorship for the "Protection of Children". Gritty, occasionally repulsive, rather clever and thoroughly engrossing. It doesn't speak well of censorship or what breeds under its skin....

*The Last Hot Time*, by John M. Ford. Urban Fantasy with a touch of Crime and a dash of Thriller. Enjoyable treatment of the "Elfland bleeds through" themes which have been severely overdone in the recent past. In this one, Chicago takes a turn backward under the aegis of Faery and a gang war like something straight from Al Capone's days may hang in the offing over the head of a naive young man called "Doc". A birthday present from my friend Josh. Lots of fun and much better than many of its cousins in the sub-genre. Reminiscent of *The Never-Never* and *One Foot in the Grave*.

*Jennifer Government*, by Max Barry. A satire about globalization and Marketing hype. Starts out rather wicked and clever, but eventually gives in to a certain heavy-handedness in its rhetoric against over-bounding capitalism and consumerism. Not bad, really, if you don't mind a bit of over-the-top here and there. Quite funny in parts and outright absurd in others.

*Spares*, by Michael Marshall Smith. A Science Fiction thriller from former SF writer, Marshall, who's now moved into Mysteries (*Straw Men*). Bad things happen when a down-and-out cop, escaping from the horrors of his destroyed life, ends up liberating some "spares" from a farm where these clones are raised to be spare parts for their wealthy twins. About two-thirds of the way through, the plot takes a sudden turn into a direction I hadn't expected and the ride got a lot weirder. But still very fun. Very Noir. Very Marshall. Won an award and deserves it. Sometimes grim and gruesome, but well-made. A lot like his later novel, *Only Forward* in tone, pace and feel. A much more solid ending, though still a bit less than perfect. Still, grand stuff. (Amusing coincidence: Marshall is a friend of Paul McAuley's and names the police chief in this book after him.)

*Rat City*, by Curt Colbert. Another bit of hardboiled Detective Fiction from the crew at Ugly Town (Go, Uglies!--yeah, they rejected my novel, but they are cool guys in spite of it. If it's from Ugly Town, it's guaranteed to entertain, at the very least.) Not a Noir, strictly an *homage* to Hammett and Chandler and the old-fashioned hardboiled detective school. Set here, in Seattle, in 1947, PI Jake Rossiter and his Girl Friday, Miss Jenkins, try to discover why a bookie named "Big" Ed tried to shoot Rossiter one morning while he was eating his toast. The road to the answer leads the reader past bad cops and good ones, drugs, crime, sex and witty quips in the seamiest parts of Seattle's past. Colbert executes the language and details just a bit more broadly than the masters he's plainly bowing to (men are always referred to as "joes," no one seems to smoke a cigarette, generically, but to "take a drag" of their Camel, Chesterfield or Phillip Morris). This is not satire, though its overly broad strokes occasionally bring it close, it never quite crosses that line. Tightens up and accelerates into a well-paced ending to a fun mystery featuring all the hallmarks of the period's best pulp writing as well as good historical research into Seattle's seamy past. Fun!

*The Monkey's Raincoat*, by Robert Crais. The first of the Elvis Cole mysteries. Good, tight, well-paced and satisfying, though it never breaks the basic "PI in over his head" mold. Elvis agrees to look for a missing husband and child and gets dragged into the ugliest side of Hollywood in the late 1980s. Saving his client and her son from the consequences of her husband's folly stirs up a nest of viperous drug dealers which place Elivs and his partner, the taciturn and delightfully psycho Joe Pike, on the war path. Nothing you haven't seen before, but so well done, it's absolutely worth the price. I plan to read more of Mr. Crais.

*Keeper*, by Greg Rucka. First-novel thriller about bodyguard Atticus Kodiak protecting an abortion-rights doctor and her Down Syndrome daughter from violent anti-abortionists. Interesting beginning which didn't pay off, for me. The supposed stone-professional bodyguard makes mistake after mistake, becomes personally involved, torments himself and others with his feelings of guilt and Rucka never manages to build enough tension without resorting to obvious ploys. The "shocking" ending is mostly annoying. Not crap, but quite disappointing.

*Seabiscuit*, by Laura Hillenbrand. Non-fiction account of the life and times of an American race horse, on which a movie I haven't seen was based. Pressed upon me by a friend, it's grabbing my attention more than I had expected it to, though I find the writing style a little more journalistic than I'd really like to be reading in my free time. Pretty good, over all and far more engaging than I had expected.

*Demolition Angel* by Robert Crais. Not as sharp as his Elvis Cole books and transparently film-option bait. Crais may have done a lot of research into explosives, but he still missed a few tricks on psychology and guns. Disappointing, really. But I was expecting a lot.

*Fluke*, by Christopher Moore. Whales have secrets unguessed by all but a really weird researcher who has a lech for his way-young assistant. Amusing and outrageous, but loses a little steam in the last third and drags a bit to a slapped-up ending which isn't as satisfying as one hoped. Very funny, in parts, to the point of making other bus riders stare, however.

*Lamb*, by Christopher Moore. Well, I knew how it was going to end, but it still seemed a bit depressing and predictable. Lots of mileage on Biblical jokes, but occasionally a bit too much re-tread. Christ as a coffee-holic? OKaaaay.... As with a lot of Moore, fun, goofy adventure, for the most part, interrupted by moments of trying to be deep which generally end up being maudlin or too long and an ending which seems a bit out of the blue and slapped-together. Not bad, but could have been better and maybe I'd have found a lot of it better if I'd had a deeper background in theology. Still, the Jewish jokes were pretty funny....

*Hard Rain*, by Barry Eisler. Holy shit! Talk about out of the blue! I picked this up in a drug store on a whim and was sucked right in to the tale of a professional killer who is trying to live a low-profile life in Tokyo after stepping on the Yakuza's toes. Action sequences to die for (hahah) which were entirely not expected from a white-collar Ivy-league lawyer. But the acknowledgments explain all in that department: he picked up a lot of technical savvy about fights and violence from <a href="http://www.nononsenseselfdefense.com/" target="blank">Marc "Animal" MacYoung</a>, whom I'm lucky enough to know from my dark, dust-scented past. A great little hitman thriller, well balanced and well-paced and full of great details.

*Wasteland of Flint*, by Thomas Harlan. A complete surprise. Fantasy/Science Fiction that takes place in an alternate future, based on a past in which the Aztec empire rules not only the World, but the known Universe, as well. An archaeological expedition turns into a search for answers and an attempt to hide civilization from an ancient enemy. Very long and slow-moving, but so dense with ideas and ramifications not fully explained that you have to read it slowly. Remarkable. First in a series.

*Across the Nightengale Floor*, by Liane Hearn. First of the Tales of the Otori series. Another remarkable book. Beautiful in prose and plot. Fantasy about a fictional Japan in which a boy born to be an assassin, but raised by pacifists, becomes the son of a noble house and re-learns his heritage to serve his adopted father against a tyrant, only to have to choose between his adopted family and his birth clan. Amazing stuff, beautifully written. Lovely interplay of complex and subtle plot lines and characters. Second book is already out (*Grass His Pillow*) and the third promised very soon. Jump on this one! It's great.

*The Hollow Chocolate Bunnies of the Apocalypse*, by Robert Rankin. British paperback a bit hard to find, here, but you can pick one up at Borders. Hilarious in places and merely odd in others. A noir crime-fantasy about a boy who goes to "the Big City" to seek his fortune and teams up with a teddy bear to solve the murders of Humpty Dumpty and other famous Nursery Rhyme folk in Toy City. A bit hysterical and heavy-handed at the end, but mostly good and lots of fans for noir fans and those who like there nursery rhymes a bit more... raw than normal. Not for the squeamish or prudish, though.

*Sacred*, by Dennis Lehane. Not quite Lehane's best work in the Kenzie and Gennaro series, this third book suffers a bit of "I saw that coming", even though it's full of plot twists and reversals and sudden changes. Once a certain point was past, I started to expect the plot twists to such a degree that I only had to ask myself "what would be the least likely--yet curiously predictable thing--which could happen here?" and it happened. Still very good writing, but not Lehane's best, by a solid distance.
