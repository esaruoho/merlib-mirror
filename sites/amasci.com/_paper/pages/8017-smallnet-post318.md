Bernard Cawley, Jr. \< <Bernard.Cawley@PSS.Boeing.com> \> contributed:

I just have to respond to a couple of the comments made in response to the query about an equivalent electric power system to replace an .049 (which one - a Babe Bee or a NorVel??).

First, the simplicity comment from Charlie: "Gas is so simple by comparison. You get an .049, a prop, and some fuel, and you have a power system. Simple as can be."

This is only true because you KNOW what ".049" is and know that you need to choose different fuel, different props and whether you need a separate tank or not; if so how big, how to place it, how to plumb it, and so forth -- when you're talking about a Babe Bee or a Wen-Mac or a NorVel or an AME. You know what fuel. You KNOW what prop - already.

I can do the same thing: Grab a 6V-wind Speed 400, a prop adapter, a 6X3 prop (a good brand for gas is a good brand for electric), a 7-cell 500/600 mAh battery, and any BEC speed control that can handle at least 15A -- and I have the equivalent of at least a mild throttled and muffled .049 (I'm not sure which one that would be), fuel tank, 20% nitro fuel, the same prop, a throttle servo, and a source for power for the radio system.

If I knew nothing about engine displacement, fuel composition, the principles of tank placement, needle valve adjustment, glow plug/head heating and so forth I'd be just as much at sea trying to start with glow power as the fellow who asked about an ".049 electric- power equivalent".

As to the cost, I also disagree with the fellow who said that starting out in electric costs at least twice as much as glow. There have been a few comparisons done, and if you choose quality stuff in both the glow and electric lists of equipment you total up, electric does start out some more expensive. But running costs are zero.

The ONLY piece of support equipment you need for E-power is a good quality peak detector charger. I usually recommend one that costs \$100. BUT, if someone were planning to stay towards the small end of SMALL (.049 equivalent again) there is one I can think of that works well, and sells for less than \$40.

That \$9 Speed 400 does require a \$5 prop adapter to be complete, and if you want to control it you need a \$35 (or more) speed control, but as I said before you now have the engine (throttled and muffled) and the throttle servo (and receiver battery) equivalent. Props are props (and good ones are good ones regardless of what device applies the torque to spin them, though some are better than others at different RPM ranges). Choosing batteries is kinda like choosing fuel blends (how much nitro, what kind of oil) and tank size.

But I do agree, there are more variables, and each variable has a wider range. Even cheap, relatively inflexible motors like the various S400s (no matter who relabels the Mabuchi 380 - Graupner, Robbe, Multiplex, etc.) can, by juggling props and cell counts, be made to deliver a wide range of power. (Sorry Fred, but a 6V Speed 400 really is just a 35-turn Mabuchi 380PH or SH with the distributor's label slapped on). Add gearing and the number of possibilities skyrockets.

And, as yet, the "tribal knowledge" of E-power is not widely distributed. It's only been around for about 25 years, and available at some local shops a lot less than that. And most local shops don't really know anything about it (and what they do know is apocryphal stuff by way of R/C car guys!). So, there is some more to learn and it's harder to find.

In the end, though, Charlie's advice is dead on - get familiar with some of the terms - and the Hobby Lobby catalog is a good place to look (though some of their advice ISN'T), and find some experience here, or on the Eflight list or the various web sites - starting with Ezonemag.com. Another's advice to get Bob Aberle's book "Clean and Quiet" is also very good. While a little outdated in the details (e-power is still evolving quickly, though not so fast as computers!), it is the best such reference around.

Bernard Cawley, Jr., Auburn, Washington USA

Mike Kerry \< <mgk@kenda.co.uk> \> attacks the same topic:

Re: E-power being "a jungle out there" -- it could be.

I came into E-power to beat a big-mouthed fellow member, who then left. I read a lot, asked questions of Electric modellers, disregarded 99% of the answers. Remembered a previous interest in "7 and a half PG" (7.5 ccs of fuel, 10 minute max) so obvious; - 10 minutes of engine run !

To maximize run time and produce prop draft a gearbox is needed, so first bite was Speed 400, Mini Olympus and 9x5 folder, full power 7 Amperes. Next gearbox was 4.5 to 1 (Cosmotech), same motor, 13x7 folder, full power about 3.3 Amps, which just happens to be very near the maximum motor efficiency drain.

The powered glider has been mentioned in a previous posting, but now has a fuselage made of 1/32" balsa and carbon fibre, all-up weight 17.5 ounces (including 7 cells at 500 mAh).

It's a delight to fly, in the right conditions ! (like less than 5 mph wind); climbs well, responds well to thermals. The mylar covering causes a sort of whistle as it glides past.

This was my first attempt at electric, and it works. Thanks to most of the writers on E-power, 'cos without them I'd still be trying.

I read Mr Woodward's discourse on IMA, visited them (on the web), bought a "Disco". Its construction is impressively simple. Now awaiting a trip to the local 'Office Superstores' for a bit more Tyvek to complete the hinging of the overgrown flaps at the back.

Remember, it's not too weak, it's too heavy ! or, simplicate and add lightness. Mike Kerry

Leonard Neumann \< <neumann@charter.net> \> advises:

Carl Dowdy was looking for a muffler for his newly-acquired G-15 engine and I e-mailed him directly. But for anyone wanting an aftermarket muffler for an engine that has muffler mounting lugs, we either have one or can have one made.

Although we provide these primarily for the controline stunt crowd looking for a light-weight muffler that doesn't cut power, several R/C fliers have purchased these mufflers as well, and for the same reasons.

They cost more than factory mufflers, but they are individually hand made. Tube (expansion) mufflers--similar in operation to factory, but lighter--cost \$29.95 at a weight of about 1.2 to 1.3 ounces.

Tongue mufflers--a long extension plugged at the end with a number of smaller holes drilled in the side for exhaust dispersion--and these are a real favorite with the controline stunt crowd--weigh, typically, 3/10 to 5/10 of an ounce, and cost \$14.95. Price includes mounting bolts (US threads) and attached pressure tap. Shipping is extra, but generally runs 55 to 77 cents.

Dave Fritzke \< <DFRITZKE@ci.crystal.mn.us> \> adds:

In reply to Nick Trubov about mufflers for Super Tigres. I had good luck using a K&B .20 Sportster muffler on my ST .23 (G20).

I had to drill out the unthreaded bolt holes in the crankcase just a teeny bit, but it left plenty of metal behind to do the job. The bonus was that it was MUCH quieter than the stock ST muffler.

If the .19 and the .23 share a crankcase (????) it should work. I have heard that some Super Tigres had "sub-piston induction" (like Cox engines) and do not do well with ANY muffler. (I think I saw this in a MAN review of an early .23.) I had no problems with mine. Dave Fritzke

...Patrick, we've covered this topic before in SMALLnet; briefly, "chrome MonoKote" (and similar products) get their metallic look from METAL -- a microscopically thin yet continuous vacuum-deposited layer of pure aluminum. And that definitely attenuates RF signals! In some cases the "chrome covering" can act as a rather efficient "RF shield", depending (as you suggest) on orientation. \[JW\]
