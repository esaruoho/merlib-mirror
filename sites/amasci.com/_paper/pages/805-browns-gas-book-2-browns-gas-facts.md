<p>The gas burns with a clear flame. The gas generator supplies gas at 280 - 320 k PA (40 to 60 psi). The flame contains hydrogen and oxygen and no other elements. <p>INTERACTIVE COMBUSTION EFFECT</p>
<p>Theoretical flame temperature is 2,210 to 2,900 degrees C. However, it changes with different applications. Tests have been made which exceed 6,000 degrees C. Further tests have been conducted indicating in excess of 8,400 degrees C. (8,400 C. = 15,1 52 F.).</p>
<p>The flame produced when Brown's Gas is ignited under 40 to 60 psi pressure is initially yellow in color and quickly reverts to a neutral blue cone with a long extension of a pale red/blue flame. There are several distinct regions, called mantles, wit hin the flame's sheath. The remarkable property of this flame is that it is NOT formed as a set of explosions, but is formed as a set of implosions. Consequently, the classical theory of combustion products, highest temperature region and other specifics must be revised. The central blue cone is the region separating the inner sustained vacuum from the continuously forming implosion produced and it is in this narrow hand that the novel combustion situation is sustained.</p>
<p>All fuel types, including gasoline, LPG, butane, propane, diesel fuel and natural gas have constant combustion or burn temperatures. Brown's Gas flame, upon application to an element or compound of elements, changes its temperature due to an interact ive combustion property. This is the unique characteristic of Brown's Gas.</p>
<p>There is no theoretical temperature limit to the applied flame as the environment of the combustion will determine the extension of incremental calorific energy supplied. The outer mantles surrounding the blue cone region prevent oxygen from interfe ring in the combustion process. In fact, the mantles located within the central hot region form an inert substance as found in modern TIG and MIG welders.</p>
<p>In that each material, such as soil, rocks, metals or liquids have a different atomic makeup, each material will burn, liquefy or sublimate (turn into a gas form) at its own respective temperature. In each case, when Brown's Gas is administered to a ma terial, the temperature of the Brown's Gas changes. To date, the capability to define the high end temperature of generated by the gas has not been possible due to the lack of appropriate testing equipment.</p>
<p>The flame produced from this gas is capable of drilling holes in high-temperature refractory products in seconds. It turns brick to glass.</p>
<p>To illustrate the temperature range, it is possible, using the same gas flame pressure (with no change in flow rate) to both (1) successfully weld aluminum sheet without a gas envelope at 660 degrees C. and (2) sublimate (vaporize) tungsten at approxim ately 6,000 degrees C. Again, this is accomplished in seconds with the same flame with no increase in volume. (See the Video tape). The intriguing explanation for the large range of calorific response when the flame is applied to different materials is go verned by the rate of mono-atomic absorption of hydrogen on the surface of these materials. For example, when the flame is applied to aluminum, white heat isn't the immediate reaction as it is when applied to brick. Instead, the flame may be shown to prod uce water on the aluminum by condensing the steam in the mantles on this hard conductive surface. The reasons for this low temperature reaction are three-fold.</p>
<p>A) The flame temperature is not high in its natural state. B) The aluminum is a good conductor of heat. C) The hydrogen in the heated region is only mildly absorbed into the aluminum.</p>
<p>However, when the flame is applied to tungsten, the heated metal surface readily absorbs mono-atomic hydrogen, thus releasing the additional calorific energy obtained from the interactive division and absorption as subsequent surfaces of the metal are exposed to the applied flame. The process accelerates under the high temperature build-up, coupled with the shielding effect of the surrounding mantles of water which, incidentally, are poor absorbers of hydrogen.</p>
<p>The salient features of this combustion process are that nascent hydrogen is readily absorbed in most elements, and especially when this reaction occurs with a neutral flame and water is re-cycles through dissociation caused at elevated temperatures wi thin that environment.</p>

<p>Inventor Planning Hydrogen-Powered Car</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>By Robert Lindsey</p>
<p> </p>
<p>(Special to The York Times)</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>Los Angeles, April 20 - Sam Leslie Leach, the inventor of a</p>
<p> </p>
<p>controversial process that he contends can economically separate the</p>
<p> </p>
<p>hydrogen and oxygen in water, says he has refined his design and</p>
<p> </p>
<p>begun building a system that will be capable of running an</p>
<p> </p>
<p>automobile on hydrogen derived from water.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>Mr. Leach's invention has been the subject of both mystery and</p>
<p> </p>
<p>controversy since he said in 1976 that he had devised an</p>
<p> </p>
<p>economically efficient means of splitting water, a contention that</p>
<p> </p>
<p>promised a cheap source of hydrogen as a replacement for fossil</p>
<p> </p>
<p>fuel.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>Mr. Leach, a multimillionaire professional inventor who has</p>
<p> </p>
<p>several basic patents in the field of optics, has been trying to</p>
<p> </p>
<p>interest the Federal Government and industry in his concept for more</p>
<p> </p>
<p>than three years, but has been largely ignored.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>For the most part, scientists have ridiculed the concept,</p>
<p> </p>
<p>arguing that it violated basic laws of physics. Any system of</p>
<p> </p>
<p>splitting water, they contend, has to consume more energy than it</p>
<p> </p>
<p>produces.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>Positive Evaluation</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>Mr. Leach has refused to discuss the details of his system or</p>
<p> </p>
<p>how it purportedly works. but last spring an innovation research</p>
<p> </p>
<p>center at the University of Oregon financed by the National Science</p>
<p> </p>
<p>Foundation evaluated part of the technology over a period of two</p>
<p> </p>
<p>weeks and concluded that, that based on its analysis, it did not</p>
<p> </p>
<p>violate the laws of physics or thermodynamics.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>The center said that the process appeared to be technically</p>
<p> </p>
<p>sound and have commercial potential, but its report did not dampen</p>
<p> </p>
<p>skepticism in the scientific community.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>Page 1</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>Two critics of the system, Howard Riese and Donald Bunker, both</p>
<p> </p>
<p>professors at the University of California, argued, for example,</p>
<p> </p>
<p>that it was impossible for such a system to work as Mr. Leach</p>
<p> </p>
<p>contends because, in effect, it would be a "perpetual motion</p>
<p> </p>
<p>machine." The inventor denies such a characterization.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>In an interview, Mr. Leach said that he had declined to make</p>
<p> </p>
<p>public any details until he had protected his rights to the process.</p>
<p> </p>
<p>Last fall, he received a patent on some elements of the process.</p>
<p> </p>
<p>Last week a second was issued by the United States Patent Office.</p>
<p> </p>
<p>After its issuance he agreed to give some details of how the system</p>
<p> </p>
<p>purportedly works.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>How System Operates</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>In its simplest terms, he said, the process utilizes a</p>
<p> </p>
<p>lazer-like device to generate ultraviolet radiation that</p>
<p> </p>
<p>photochemically splits steam into oxygen and hydrogen. It then</p>
<p> </p>
<p>utilizes the electrostatic forces that normally bind electrons and</p>
<p> </p>
<p>protons in water vapor (and which are released in the water-</p>
<p> </p>
<p>splitting action) to maintain the reaction.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>In 1922, Niels Bohr, the Danish theoretical physicist, first</p>
<p> </p>
<p>defined the electrostatic forces that bind electrons and protons as</p>
<p> </p>
<p>"extranuclear" energy. Mr. Leach's contention that he has found a</p>
<p> </p>
<p>way to use the energy in the way he describes is likely to evoke</p>
<p> </p>
<p>additional skepticism from other scientists.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>But he asserts that the process he utilizes to maintain the</p>
<p> </p>
<p>water-splitting action is identical with one observed by astronomers</p>
<p> </p>
<p>in energy interactions that occur in gaseous nebulae, the great</p>
<p> </p>
<p>masses of interstellar gas that absorb ultraviolet radiation from</p>
<p> </p>
<p>stars and re-emit it as visible light.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>The following is a more detailed account of how Mr. Leach says</p>
<p> </p>
<p>the system works:</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>The reaction is started with an input of electrical energy from</p>
<p> </p>
<p>outside the system, from a battery or electric line. This</p>
<p> </p>
<p>energy is converted, by using an "optical pump" and other</p>
<p> </p>
<p>components, into large amounts of ultraviolet radiation of a</p>
<p> </p>
<p>specific wavelength that is precisely tailored to ionize</p>
<p> </p>
<p>hydrogen and oxygen molecules in the steam that has been fed</p>
<p> </p>
<p>into a tubular reaction chamber.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>The chamber is flooded with the radiation. During the</p>
<p> </p>
<p>ionization, electrons are momentarily liberated from their</p>
<p> </p>
<p>atoms and molecules.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>Ionization and Radiation</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>Microseconds later they are recaptured and recombined with the</p>
<p> </p>
<p>proton or nucleus of the atom. At this point, the energy that was</p>
<p> </p>
<p>required to ionize it reappears and radiates away.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>This radiation then ionizes another molecule. Very soon a chain</p>
<p> </p>
<p>reaction begins that involves millions of molecules and atoms.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>The process's concept, Mr. Leach said, manipulates the</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>Page 2</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>recombination of electrons and protons as hydrogen and oxygen</p>
<p> </p>
<p>instead of water vapor. Some of the hydrogen, he said, can be used</p>
<p> </p>
<p>to generate electricity to continue the initial input to the process</p>
<p> </p>
<p>and, in effect, be SELF-SUSTAINING AS LONG AS WATER IS PUMPED INTO</p>
<p> </p>
<p>THE SYSTEM.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>In 1975, before he publicized his work, the Presley companies,</p>
<p> </p>
<p>a southern California home builder, acquired an option on the</p>
<p> </p>
<p>process from Mr. Leach for use in home heating. The Securities and</p>
<p> </p>
<p>Exchange Commission investigated the company and alleged that it had</p>
<p> </p>
<p>issued false statements regarding its capabilities. Subsequently,</p>
<p> </p>
<p>Mr. Leach reacquired the option for the same price Presley paid for</p>
<p> </p>
<p>it.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>Mr. Leach asserted that he had demonstrated the validity of his</p>
<p> </p>
<p>theory in 11 experimental machines that split water into hydrogen</p>
<p> </p>
<p>and oxygen. He said that the machine now being built for use in an</p>
<p> </p>
<p>automobile was of a more sophisticated design and was intended to</p>
<p> </p>
<p>drive a 245-horsepower automobile.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>A spokesman for a company that is assembling the device under a</p>
<p> </p>
<p>contract with Mr. Leach said it was hoped the machine would be ready</p>
<p> </p>
<p>for testing in early summer.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>Scientists have tried for more than a century to separate water</p>
<p> </p>
<p>into its two components, oxygen and hydrogen. Electrolysis, nuclear</p>
<p> </p>
<p>reactors and other means have been employed to do so, but every</p>
<p> </p>
<p>method has consumed far more energy that the hydrogen that was</p>
<p> </p>
<p>produced.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>The availability of a cheap source of hydrogen would have</p>
<p> </p>
<p>immense implications for the world economy. Not only could hydrogen</p>
<p> </p>
<p>be used as a substitute for gasoline, but it would also be used as a</p>
<p> </p>
<p>replacement for home heating fuels and other energy sources.</p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p> </p>
<p>NYT April 21,1979</p>
