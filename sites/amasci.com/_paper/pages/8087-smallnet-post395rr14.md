Andy Woitowicz wrote: ....."how does "free-porting" \[sub-piston induction\] in a crank inducted engine contribute to getting more power/rpm?"

I would guess that the mixture drawn from the venturi is richer than optimum, and additional air drawn under the piston skirt dilutes that mixture to the point of correctness.\
\
As to why sub-piston induction would work against proper throttling, imagine that the engine had been set for a correct mixture when running wide open and set to balance the air drawn in under the piston. Then imagine the same engine with the venturi partially blocked by the barrel of the carb and the engine was running at, say, half of maximum speed. Assuming that the carb was still supplying the same air-fuel mixture, the space under the piston would have twice as long to admit air, and the pressure inside the crankcase should be lower than with wide-open throttle due to the restriction of the carb. One would expect more than twice as much air as normal being sucked in under the piston, with a reduced volume of air-fuel for it to mix with. The mixture would then be considerably leaner than at full throttle.\
\
Adding a muffler would cause the induction of exhaust gases, with little oxygen, thus keeping the air-fuel mixture a little closer to what is required.

In the last post I questioned whether it could be said that mufflers and sub-piston induction contributed greatly to power loss. My feeling was that the simple act of muffling any engine, sub-piston induction or not, was the cause of power loss and I promised to test the idea. At the field, with the Norvel on my Stik, top end, with muffler, tached at 20K. Taking off the muffler and after adjusting the needle to compensate for lack of muffler pressure to the tank, my tach gave me 19.7K. A 300 rpm loss. A little over 1.5%. Keep in mind that the Norvel has sub-piston induction. If you're thinking that this is because the Norvel mufflers leak, not so. This muffler is from the .074 and is bolted to the .061 by way of a custom made exhaust header.

From David Larkin \< <dslarkin@blvl.igs.net> \>\
Obviously Andy and I are going to have to agree to disagree as our experiences differ. Concerning PAWs, I'm speaking from the experience of running hundreds of them.\
First of all, PAW make an excellent, if simple, throttle, for their smaller engines. It has a fixed airbleed. This will give an idle down to 3,500 to 4,000 rpm which is sufficient for most sports designs, and a great deal better than has been available for most production 1/2A glow motors. If a low idle is not obtainable, then the fault is usually a misaligned spraybar. This, I surmise, has a variable mixture effect, changing the mixture as it rotates within the barrel. For some time now, PAW have been using a spraybar with two holes, but these are not positioned 180 apart. Their orientation is critical - with the carb off, and looking up the throat from the engine side, the two holes should just be symmetrically visible at the full throttle position of the barrel.\
\
I have recently had to replace the entire carb assembly on a PAW 100 1 cc (0.06 cu in for those unmetricized), to restore the idle, but this is very unusual. The original carb had a sloppy fitting barrel which is likely to have been caused by vibration from the use of a make of propellor known to be hard to balance. Eric Clutton suggests that adding a ball joint on the throttle arm may aggravate this condition.\
\
The idle on a small PAW may 'roll' slightly, which misleads some people into setting it at an unnecessarily high figure.\
\
On the bigger PAWs, a 'Varijet' carb is provided or available which is a true variable mixture carb. With this, an idle down to 1,000 rpm may be reached, and 1,500 is typical, far better than most engines with normal good carbs. Eric Clutton has suggested it would benefit from a variable air-bleed, but the factory counter that, on the rare occasions (usually caused by altitude) that this might help, they have a simpler solution - they can offer replacement needles of varying taper.\
\
The throttling on the smaller PAWs is not very linear, so it helps to adjust the geometry of the linkage to improve this. But in practice, the non-linearity is of little consequence. On my PAW 049 R/C in my 'Tomboy' I bring the throttle right back for cruise, then start my approach by bringing the throttle trim back halfway, taking it all the way down just before the flare. The TOMBOY, being originally a free flight design, is a 'floater' for which a good low idle is needed. Touch and goes are possible, both with wheels and floats. How often have you seen 1/2A models shooting touch and goes (and in this case with only rudder and throttle)?\
To sum up, if a PAW with the simple carb will not idle, the cause is usually a misaligned spraybar - commonly caused by the owner 'adjusting' the carb lever when installing the engine, and not realizing the likely problem if the spraybar rotates out of position. In fairness, there is nothing about this in the otherwise excellent PAW instructions.\
\
I certainly agree with Andy that it would always be nice to have an adjustable airbleed, but there are other factors to take into account to get good performance from a carburettor. However many of the better older R/C diesels, including the Taplin, did have an adjustable air bleed\
Concerning Mills engines and sub-piston induction, the original Mills engines were beautifully fitted, to close tolerances, But they were notorious for being the opposite of 'blueprinted' engines. Consequently dimensions and performance varied widely, as did the amount of sub-piston induction. However the sideport design was such that ease of starting and handling were maintained.\
Cox may very well call sub-piston induction 'free-porting' - but the term sub-piston induction has been in use and accepted since the 50s.\
Concerning mufflers and throttling, the early R/C engines just used R/C carbs. It was soon apparent that an improved idle could be obtained if the exhaust was simultaneously restricted, and rotating exhaust valves (Cox Medallion R/C) or flaps were fitted. Bear in mind that variable mixture carbs were not usually used - apart from the Johnson carb. Once mufflers were introduced, the first engines had these flaps within the mufflers or exhaust stacks, as in the case of the OS 49 R/C. Then it was soon realized that the mufflers provided enough restriction to make the rotating devices superfluous. Meanwhile people have continued to use exhaust restrictors by themselves as a means of throttling, as in the case of the ACE (and Cox) sleeve throttle, and the exhaust throttle on ED marine diesels.\
\
For Dick Scalone, the British magazine Model Flyer does a very nice plan for an F-86 EDF. Plans are also still available (from the designer Phil Smith) for the 1950 era diesel powered free flight F-86 from Veron, but this is not very scale.\
Best regards\
Dave Larkin

From david owen \< <owendc@1earth.net> \>\
Several comments :\
Oscar, the PAW 55 crankshaft thread is 4BA. Major dia .1417", 38.5tpi, tapping size .1181".\
\
Rust in diesels: This is not normally a problem and it may not be related to the oil or oil content. I have seen it only once and in this case the engine rusted overnight. A long extension had been fitted to the exhaust and the model was hung nose down. My guess is that acid, one of the components of exhaust gas, had condensed out on the walls of the extension and run back into the engine overnight. The cure is simple. Hang the model tail down, especially if a muffler or tailpipe is fitted.\
\
Oils for diesels: The typical sport diesels commonly used have cast-iron pistons running in steel liners, what are often referred to as a 'lapped' engines. Castor has proved far superior for this set-up than any synthetic oil,giving cooler running, less wear, superior protection against corrosion and a mess on the model. Take your pick! The term 'lapped' is misleading, as few if any production engines would have a lapped piston or cylinder. Ground and/or honed yes, but not lapped, as the process is too time-consuming for anything more than a one-off. I exclude earlier Enyas, which did have a piston finished with a lap. The piston and cylinder were never lapped 'together'.\
\
Sub-piston induction: This is the correct term adopted to describe the intake (or induction) of supplementary air into the crankcase via an opening under the piston, whether this is arranged as separate ports as in an Orwick, or through the exhaust slits, as in an Elfin for example. Sorry Andy, but the term 'free-porting' is in the same league as 'two-cycle' and four-cycle'in my opinion. The typical piston-ported layout (eg Mills) is not sub-piston induction in the sense of providing a supplement to the primary induction.Some piston-ported engines do also have sub-piston induction, although this may not be intentional in all cases, but a consequence of inlet port height and piston length being juggled to give acceptable timing. Sometimes the term 'side-port' is used to describe a piston-ported design. Whether this is a reference to the port being cut into the side(back)of the cylinder, or its location at the side under the exhaust, as in an O&R, is a matter of conjecture. Certainly, an adjustable airbleed is required for proper mixture adjustment. Earlier and even some current diesels do not have this feature. I'm not sure about 'all modern ABC engines' having sub-piston induction, though, having not found this feature on three chosen at random. These were the Thunder Tiger 07, VA 049 Mk2 and Magnum Pro 40. Not that I expected to find it, as it still definitely presents a problem when a muffler is added.Gig Eifflaender is definitely right in this regard!\
regards\
Dave Owen

Andy W. wrote:\

> "On the matter of diesel throttling. I do have direct experience and I agree with Chris Murphy. I've done several PAWs and none had sub-piston induction, yet all throttled poorly.
