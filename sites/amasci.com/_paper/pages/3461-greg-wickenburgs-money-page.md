# Greg Wickenburg's Money Page

*Source page: `~greg/thrasher/bart.html`*

**Bart Thrasher**

Excerpt from Bloody Bibb, A County History on Bibb Co. Ala. V. Donald Elam, 1985, Elam Enterprises

The Miller-Thrasher Gang

Jess Miller had a large estate near River Bend, which is about halfway way between West Blocton and Centreville. Bart Thrasher lived near Scottsville. The two men had been friends for years; and, with the help of some "reliable" henchmen, they put together one of the most vicious, hard-to-break ring of criminals that ever set foot in "Bloody Bibb" County.

Miller and Thrasher stole cattle around the area and transported the animals to other localities so that they could not be identified. The cattle were then butchered and sold to coal miners. Some people in River Bend and Scottsville discovered where their cattle were being taken. With the help of neighbors and lawmen from Centreville, they went in search of their stolen cattle. Even if the owners were successful, they could not prompt the local courts to prosecute the thieves. You see, the gangs in the area "owned" the courts of Centreville.

As a further example of the Miller-Thrasher gang's cunning ways, they once bought a pair of mules and tied them to the railroad track one night. Needless to say, the mules were destroyed. The men then collected the insurance money for their loss.

On one occasion, Miller and Thrasher stole a whole herd of cattle from a man named Davenport, who lived in the River Bend community. The thieves drove the herd across Tuscaloosa County into Pickens County where they were sold. Davenport learned through an old negro named Reuben Farrington that his cattle were being driven west. A posse of citizens tracked the thieves to Coaling, in Tuscaloosa County. It was night when they arrived, so the men bedded down for the night. The Miller-Thrasher gang, however, knew the route well and traveled through the night. The thieves made the cattle cross an old ford above Tuscaloosa, which had been used to settle the county. This was truly an outstanding feat, for it is difficult to get livestock to venture into water which they do not know the depth of, especially in the dark.

The posse caught up with the gang at a creek where they had finally stopped for rest. The cattle was encircled by a pen made of wooden rails. Suddenly the thieves spotted the posse! Several rounds were fired, but none hit their mark. The herd was startled and bolted through the rail fence. The outlaws ran for the shelter of the woods, for they saw that they were out numbered for probably the first time ever. The posse rounded up most of the 60 heads of cattle and drove them back to "Bloody Bibb" after alerting the Pickens County sheriff to be on the lookout for the Miller-Thrasher gang

In those days, if you managed to escape from the county or even the general vicinity of where you had committed a crime, you were virtually assured of avoiding arrest. As far as can be determined, no charges were ever brought against the MillerThrasher gang for this particular crime.

The Davenport cattle-stealing episode occurred in the spring of 1891. Of course, no witnesses dared defy the Miller-Thrasher gang; but, citizens in the north end of "Bloody Bibb" got together and organized a faction called the vigilante committee to put an end to the lawlessness.

Miller and Thrasher had as their trusted henchmen Alec and Henry Smith, brothers; George Cook; Bud Redd, a vicious young thug; and two cousins, Jim and Will Morrison. These men would steal, rob or kill for Jess Miller and Bart Thrasher.

Henry Smith had been arrested in January of 1891 for having stolen cows in a pen at his cabin. Miller had apparently helped him steal the cattle. Smith, a weak-willed person, had wanted to cooperate with the authorities so as to keep the noose from around his own neck. Miller posted his bond; and, believing that hisgang had been betrayed, Miller questioned Smith about what he had told the sheriff. Smith denied having "snitched." However, as agitators will do, some of Smith's enemies carried tales of disloyalty to Miller.

Miller wanted to give Smith the benefit of the doubt, so he had his henchmen keep surveillance over the man through the spring of that year. Smith seemed to be loyal. He participated in several more dirty deeds with the gang, and nothing got back to the authorities concerning the outlaws.

In June, however, Smith got on a week-long drunk and boasted about the cattle stealing incident and also mentioned a couple of murders that he knew about. Word quickly got back to Miller and Thrasher about this incident.

When he got sober, Smith of course denied the stories that he had told anything; but, the gang leaders had heard too much. They assigned Will Morrison and George Cook to kill Smith. The two stalked their prey relentlessly. Apparently they were successful in their endeavors because Smith was found dead one morning in late June. The victim was discovered just outside a fence surrounding the house of a Mr. Partington near West Blocton.

After hearing of this latest murder, the sheriff began an immediate investigation. He, along with some deputized offficers, surrounded a house where Miller, Morrison and Cook were staying Will Morrison, it appeared, had left the county. Apparently he expected to be arrested and took flight to avoid his fate Miller and Cook, however, were arrested; they chose to surrender rather than face certain death at the hands of the "mob." The sheriff was no more than a fixture and went along with the citizens who had, for all practical purposes, taken the law into their own hands.

Miller and Cook were taken to the Bibb County Jail in Centreville, where they had to await a hearing before three justices. The two men hired a team of attorneys from Tuscaloosa by the name of Hargrove, Logan & Vanderfraff. The state was represented by Colonel S. W. John, of Birmingham, and county solicitors L. M. Smitherman and L. M. Clements. The justices presiding over the case were Justices Wallace, Hays and Gist. On the day of the hearing, a Monday in early July, 1891, the Centreville Courthouse was full of people anxious to see "the book thrown at" this gang of men who had flaunted all of the decent aspects of a civilized society.

The state's witnesses were escorted from Blocton by a guard of 60 men as a protection to their persons. Miller and Cook were brought into court looking stolid and defiant. They figured this hearing, like those in the past, was just another farce. They had spread fear all around them to stifle any opposition to their outlaw domain. Who would be brave enough to come forward with information against them? They thought this hearing would be just another notch in their infamous careers.

The first witness for the state was Dr. W. C. Cross, who had examined the body of Henry Smith after he was killed about one hundred yards from Partington's fence. Smith had, as determined from the hurried tracks left behind him, been trying to reach the Partington residence for safety; but, he never made it. He was found lying face down with his hat in his hand. The fact that his hat was in his hand fed fuel to the belief that he had been running hard before he was shot. Two .45 caliber rifle balls entered Smith's body, one in his head near his right ear, and the other in his back. Either bullet, according to Doctor Cross, would have caused instant death. Evidently Smith had been tied up with a rope, for one was found nearby. It was believed that he had gotten loose and was running for his life when he was shot down.

The state's second witness was Nancy Smith, sister of the slain man. She testified that before her brother was shot, Miller wanted her to let her own daughter work out Smith's bond so that he could leave the county. Miller feared that Smith might incriminate him in the stolen cattle ring After hearing the talk that was going on, particularly between the Smith family, Miller decided to put Will

Morrison on Smith's case in order to throw everyone off the tracks of the real killer in case there was a slip up. Evidently Smith was tipped off about this from one of the gang members, probably Alec Smith, his brother. Henry knew that Miller had contacted Morrison and that he was being stalked. On Tuesday evening before his death, Smith walked to Miller's house to try and talk things over. He then planned to visit George Cook for a talk that would hopefully save his life. He made sure that his sister Nancy knew where he was going and stated that he would be back by daylight if Morrison did not kill him first. Miller seemed friendly to Smith and even offered to pay his way to Mississippi to get him out of the state for a while. Smith, however, was afraid that knowledge of his whereabouts was just a trap so that Miller could have him killed that much more conveniently. Smith had been afraid for several months that he had outlived his usefulness as far as the gang was concerned. He expected to be shot from ambush at any time.

The day the hearing for Miller and Cook began, extra precautions were taken to guard the witnesses against Miller and Cook. It was hoped that Bart Thrasher or the Morrison cousins would show their hands and try to spring the two prisoners, or better still, that the gang would try to intimidate or kill some of the witnesses. This would put the gang right where the law wanted them. Some suspicious moves were made by Miller's friends but nothing tangible occurred so that the guards or deputies could make an arrest. It was really a case of nerves! It was a welcome relief on the second day of the hearing, Tuesday, when daylight came to what had come to be known as "Bloody Bibb"

Tuesday morning, James Holder was called to the witness stand. He acted as coroner in the Smith case, though it was considered to be in a mediocre capacity. Suffice it to say he could dress a body for burial and try to determine the cause of death by common sense and a little detective work. Holder, who lived near the Partington house where Smith had been shot, testified that on the night in question, he learned that the body had been found around eleven o'clock. After daylight, Partington sent for him, and he examined and dressed the body. There was no such thing in those days as embalming; a body had to be buried within 24 hours, especially in the summer.

Holder also testified that on a previous occasion, Jess Miller had sent for him and told him that there was some work he wanted done. In his words, he said, "I went, and he asked me what I thought about getting Smith out of the way. Suspicious, I asked him how, and he said he had heard of men being dumped or just killed and done away with. I told him I would not do that sort of business."

The next witness for the state was John Kirkland. He had serious doubts about testifying against his former boss. However, in view of the security and the guarantees from the prosecution on immunity from various other charges, Kirkland agreed to cooperate. He said that Miller had told him there had better not be anything said to incriminate him or his gang. Miller had warned him to keep whatever he knew to himself.

The next witness called to the stand was Fayette Smith, a brother of the deceased. He was too frightened, however, to testify. After viewing his dead brother's body, it is not surprising that he would fear for his own life if he told what he knew.

At this time, Edward Anderson, a deputy for the county, testified that when he arrested Miller, the suspect said, "Don't worry, I'm not going to run off because I haven't killed anybody."

John Kirkland was recalled to the stand. He swore that Miller had asked him if he thought it was a good idea to put strychnine in Fayette Smith's whiskey. Miller seemed to think Smith had been running his mouth about the gang after being sworn to secrecy.

A man by the name of Jim Brown, who was a former MillerThrasher gang protege, was called to testify next. Brown said that Miller had asked him if he would help send Henry Smith away or help get rid of him in some manner. He testified that he refused to help in this dirty deed.

The third day of the hearing began in a quiet way. The crowd had thinned considerably since the beginning The fear of the unknown and plain human curiosity had both been quenched.

On the afternoon of this third day, Jim Brown was recalled to the stand. He testified that Miller had once talked to him about his putting poison into Fayette Smith's whiskey. Miller had said that he would furnish the poison, and Brown was to administer the potent drink to Smith. Brown was to do the actual deed because Smith evidently trusted him and would do most anything Brown asked of him.

After this testimony, the state rested their case. The hearing seemed to be in favor of the prosecution since at least a few people had gotten brave and spoken out against Jess Miller and George Cook. The first witness' testimony encouraged the rest to come forward. The two outlaws were transferred to the Birmingham Jail in Jefferson County for security reasons where they were to await the action of the Bibb County grand jury.

A ticklish case that had not been brought up at the hearing concerned the theft of some cotton. Up to then, the owner of the stolen cotton was reluctant to testify. After Miller and Cook were jailed, there was a possibility that he might be persuaded to bear witness. It happened this way: Edmond Parker was a black cotton farmer. He owned land left to his family by some good white people named Parker when slavery was abolished. Edmond's wife had died, and he and their two sons ran the farm. They worked hard at their job raising King Cotton.

The Parkers packed their harvest into wagons, which were then parked under a big shed. When the following Saturdays arrived, the three would haul their wagons to the gin in Centreville. One Friday night, four wagons disappeared from the Parker's farm. Father and sons followed the tracks in hopes of discovering where their harvest had disappeared to. They were not successful, however, because there were many tracks going in every direction.

The eider Parker reported the theft to the sheriff in Centreville. The county officer advised Parker to put something in his cotton that could be recognized or that would stop the gin as it was processed. Hopefully this would provide a clue as to the thieves. Parker asked not to be identified out of fear for his life He did, though, place pine kindling, or splinters, in several of his wagons full of cotton.

In late October of 1891, two more of Parker's wagons were stolen. As hoped, they showed up at the local gin. The authorities were promptly called in to investigate. Two men, who were "underlings" of the Miller-Thrasher gang, had brought the cotton to the gin. They, of course, denied knowledge of the stolen cotton. Spence Steele and Charlie Steadman had a look of fear in their eyes like that of a child caught with his hand in the cookie jar. No amount of threats, however, could break their code of silence. They were held in jail in lieu of a very high bond, which the prosecutor hoped would quickly help them get to the truth about the splintered cotton.

After a week of lying in jail, Joe Wise, a brother-in-law of Spence Steele, came to visit. As Wise was leaving, a deputy stopped him and asked if he knew anything about the theft. Wise's eyes darted from the deputy's face to the jail cell and then to the ground. He tried to avert the deputy's questioning gaze. Wise stuttered and stammered and said, 'I can't talk 'cause I don't want to get killed. The Miller bunch will get me for sure." The deputy, Jack McCool, dismissed the man with a shake of his head.

The following day, Deputy McCool decided to pay a visit to Wise's house to see if he could persuade the man to make a statement against Miller. Taking a choice quart of whiskey from the sheriff's office, McCool rode his horse to Wise's house on the outskirts of Blocton. He knew that Wise liked to drink, and nothing will loosen a tongue better than a drink of whiskey. Deputy McCool spent the entire day with Wise who, after a few drinks, "spilled his guts" about the Miller-Thrasher gang He also promised to testify before the grand jury when it convened.

Deputy McCool rode back to Centreville quite pleased to know that he had a witness against the forces of evil in "Bloody Bibb." McCool felt so good about this that he rode to see Edmond Parker, who reluctantly agreed to also appear before the grand jury regarding his splintered cotton. Parker was nervous, edgy and afraid for his life, but McCool assured him that the culprits were in jail and would remain there. This assurance did not completely convince Parker, though. He said, "I'ze worked hard all my life, and I ain't beholden to no one, but I can t see why an old niggah like me has to be mistreated. I'ze taught my two boys to be polite to white folks, but deputy, dey can't understand all this stealing us is having to put up with. Naw suh, I'ze don't need no protection except de Lawd's. I'll pray about dis and try to be in court."

As he rode back to Centreville, Deputy McCool had to admire the old negro's courage; but, he did not share his enthusiasm about the Lord taking care of him. McCool believed in the Lord; but, he knew how tricky the Miller-Thrasher gang was when it came to witnesses, especially black ones.

Jess Miller and George Cook were still incarcerated in the Birmingham Jail. Their lawyers were trying every trick in the legal profession to get them released on bond. The prosecution in "Bloody Bibb," of course, objected for fear that their witnesses would suddenly disappear. The prosecution decided that if they could not get all of the gang at one time, they would break the back of the organization by trying the two men they had in Jail, Miller and Cook.

When the grand jury reconvened after Miller and Cook's hearing, in the spring of 1892, strange things happened. Joe Wise "lost" his memory, Fayette and Nancy Smith had left the state, and Spence Steele and Charlie Steadman could not remember anything either. Then lo and behold, when the baliff called Edmond Parker, he could not be found! No one remembered seeing him in the courthouse that day!

The grand jury was sequestered that night at a hotel in Centreville for their protection. Deputy McCool and John Kirkland headed for Parker's house, which was between Eoline and Scottsville. Their worst fears were confirmed when they met Parker's two boys on the Scottsville road. They were scared half to death. According to them, their father had disappeared the night before while they were fishing on Schultz Creek. A half-finished supper and signs of a scuffle outside the house attested to the fact that something violent had happened to their father. Another suspicious thing was that two of the Parker's horses were gone from the barn.

McCool and Kirkland investigated the grounds and house for hopeful clues. The two boys, Tobe and Jessie, were scared to stay at home so they were taken to a cousin's in Brent where they would be safe. McCool and Kirkland then headed back to Centreville with heavy hearts to report to Sheriff Crawford what had taken place.

Edmond Parker had kept his shotgun handy ever since he had talked to Deputy McCool. He feared there might be a leak from somewhere about his having agreed to testify before the grand jury against Jess Miller. It was not safe in "Bloody Bibb" when things were at their "best"; and, now that he had consented to tell what he knew about Miller, he had an added fear for his life.

Parker's shotgun did him no good, however. A slight March breeze stirred the air causing his kerosene lamp to flicker as he sat eating Suddenly Parker felt a breeze on his neck and felt that someone was near him. Turning around from the table, he glimpsed a man creeping up on him! His glance came too late for a terrible blow struck his head, and he was knocked unconscious. By the time he hit the floor, another man had come into the kitchen. The two men picked Parker up and carried him from the house. They flung the poor man across a horse that they fetched from the nearby woods. Parker began to regain consciousness and pushed himself from the horse in hopes of escape. As scared as he was and using super-human strength, he tried to kick and fight his way free from his abductors. Another blow to his head subdued him, though. Parker was then tied by his hands and feet and thrown across the horse again.

Not content just to abduct Parker, the two thugs caught a couple of horses in the barn and led them behind as they headed for an old mill pond near Scottsville. Once there, the two men searched Parker's pockets for money or any other valuables and then unceremoniously tossed him into the pond. But before they dumped him in, they tied a sack full of rocks around Parker's body. The two men watched for a moment as the still water churned from the impact of Parker's body. The water became smooth again, and the bullfrogs sounded their lonely chorus as the life of an old negro slipped from him at the bottom of the ten foot pond. One of the murderous pair said, "He can't talk now! Let's go." They then headed back to the main Scottsville road leading the stolen horses behind them.

The next morning, Sheriff Crawford, feeling by now that the county was even bloodier, took his deputies to Edmond Parker's farm. Seeing the unfinished supper plate on the table and noticing the sign of struggle outside the house, the lawmen followed the horse tracks up the road. When the tracks reached the old mill pond and stopped, the officers correctly surmised that Parker was at the bottom of the pond. The sheriff enlisted the help of citizens around the Scottsville community to break the mill pond dam. After the water was low enough, it revealed the ghastly sight of the drowned negro man.

The murder of Edmond Parker and the lack of witnesses in the Henry Smith case caused feelings to reach a height that had never before been reached. The local vigilante committee met the next night and collected money to pay for someone to fight "fire with fire" and to clean up "Bloody Bibb." A telegram went out to the Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad Company (TCI) requesting the assistance of one of its most efficient deputies. Henry Cole was sent at their solicitation. Cole had made a name for himself as a fearless foe to thieves and murderers in the camps and railroads that TCI ran. The committee asked Cole to bring the leaders and members of the Miller-Thrasher gang to justice. When asked how they wanted this done, they replied, "It won't do any good to arrest them 'cause they'll go free. To answer your question, Mr. Cole, bring them in dead, feet first."

Cole took the assignment to kill Bart Thrasher and Jim Morrison. Later Alec Smith and Bud Redd were added to the list. D. S. Morrison, of Woodstock, Alabama, told of seeing Henry Cole at his father's house as a boy. He remembered overhearing Cole and his papa plotting to kill Bart Thrasher. D. S. said Cole was a very affable, kind, well-educated man.

The attorneys for Jess Miller and George Cook petitioned the court for the release of their clients. The court really had no choice because of the lack of evidence and witnesses. I guess the old Indian saying "Heap know, few see" applied. This meant that no matter how many people know you are guilty, if no one testifies that they saw you commit a crime, you are considered not guilty.

Miller and Cook left the county after their narrow escape from justice. They evidently wanted no part of Henry Cole and his reputedly "bush-whacking" ways. Some say the two held up in a cave behind Lucite, a small hamlet adjacent to West Blocton. The story goes that certain people fed them for a while until Miller's wife disposed of some of his property. Then, according to rumor, the pair of outlaws went to Texas where they formed a new gang. Bart Thrasher got word that he was first on Cole's list of bounties to be collected. He wrote a letter to The State Herald in Birmingham explaining his side of the controversy. That letter, copied verbatim, follows:

*To The State Herald*

Friends and other I wish to say to the people of that country that I has seen a gud many tales in the papers printed about me and I will leave it to the majority of the people if I haven't had enough to make a man fight snakes the way I have bin treated for the last fore years and don't think I have had justis and now I have lost all hopes of ever seeing any more pleasure in this world. But I am going to continue trying to keep out of the way. I no that the of ricers is trying to kill me. I have not done any of them any anger. I see in the paper where they say I look bad and pale that night at Coleburg You have jest heard one side of the thing They say that I have had but two meals that week. I want everybody to no that I don't suffer for anything in the fix I am in I don't have to steal it nether I exspect some of tines men could live on half that I git well I feel just like I was drove in to all of the trouble I am sorry ever thing has happened like it is I don't know how to express myself like a heap of people bee cause I don't no much but I no enough to keep out of Henry Coles way.

My friend I wish you would fix this few lines up the best you can you see what I am trying to git at. Fore years ago this last July my father was waylade and last January my brother was waylade and shot eight men hid themselves 75 yards from the roads side and shot him and then sum people calls a brave trick. Tell sheriff Mor that I would like to have som of them field glasses that them deputies is talking about looking for me with.

Will close yours Bart Thrasher

Cole read the letter Thrasher had written. When asked if he should reconsider his plans to kill Thrasher, he replied, "He made his own hell; my job is to stop him, and that I will do."

Cole began to concentrate on the whereabouts of Thrasher. He got information that the outlaw had gone to see a woman that lived on the trail leading from Blocton toward Vance. Thrasher stayed with this woman when he felt it was safe to do so. It was said that he had a strong intuition for danger. He stayed in the woods studying the nature of animals, and especially the chirps of birds, as an indication of danger.

Thrasher went into Blocton one afternoon for food from the commissary. While there, he had a few drinks. He let his guard down and began to boast that he could not be arrested. Someone in the store relayed this avowal to a deputy sheriff who happened to be near. The deputy went to the door of the store and peered in, but the bright sun outside temporarily blinded him so that he could not see well in the darkened store. As soon as Thrasher saw the rifle the deputy carried, he shot. After making sure the deputy was dead, Thrasher simply stepped over his body and headed back toward his hideout. The murderer was seen by various people traveling this trail and reported the fact to Henry Cole. Cole watched the path day and night for a couple of weeks in hopes of encountering Thrasher. Alas, one evening just as the sun was setting, the outlaw came down the trail. On this particular evening, Cole had climbed a big oak tree so that he could better observe the path. Thrasher passed just under the oak tree; and when he was 40 feet away, he became suspicious and turned as he took his coat off the barrel of his 30-30 rifle which he carried over his shoulder. Cole called out for the bandit to halt, shooting Thrasher at the same time. Thrasher jumped and fell over the bushes beside the trail. Cole remained in the tree all night because he was not absolutely certain that he had killed his target. When daylight finally came, Cole eased himself down from his perch. It had been a long night, and he was very stiff and tired. He spotted Thrasher's boots just beyond the path and tiptoed up with his gun safety off. Thrasher was indeed dead, shot through the heart.

Cole reported to the vigilante committee that he had killed the first man on his list. He carried Thrasher's pocket watch which had his initials on it to prove his claim. The local authorities checked the body for positive identification. A cousin of Thrasher's and a deputy named Suttle who had known him all his life both provided the identification needed. The body was taken to the morgue in West Blocton where it lay until late in the afternoon. Thrasher was then taken to the Caffee Cemetery above Blocton and buried beside his wife who had died several years earlier. Edna Elam, the author's wife, points to Bart Thrasher's tomb stone in Caffee Cemetery

Was Bart Thrasher an outlaw because he wanted to be, or was he double crossed and pushed to the breaking point and thus had to hide and kill those who betrayed him? The answer will never be found, for all that is left of Thrasher lies under a slab alongside other Thrasher family members. One has a feeling of hopelessness as one ponders the questions that arise when visiting the grave of this man. The silence and eerie quiet of the cemetery are all that remain of a strong man who lived recklessly.

Henry Cole next turned his sights to ridding the county of Bud Redd. Redd was a light man with red hair and sparkling blue eyes and stood six foot three. Although Miller and Thrasher had the reputation for being tough, Redd, though not a leader, was "hell on wheels." Pound for pound, he was tougher than anyone in the Miller-Thrasher gang Redd got into an argument with one Harvey McCool in a drinking establishment in Blocton. He cut McCool so badly that he had to be sent to Montgomery on a train for hospital care. McCool never did fully recover his health.

Cole traced Redd to Birmingham but could not locate him. Cole preferred to kill him in Bibb County anyway since he was working for a faction of people from that county. And too, the people of Jefferson County may not have appreciated a bounty hunter doing his killing in their county. Cole reluctantly returned to "Bloody Bibb" with the intention of making it a little bloodier. Redd had gotten word that he was being sought by Cole, however, and did not return to Bibb County for a while

Cole instead turned his attentions to Alec Smith. He received information about Smith from a jealous cousin who lived near Duncanville, in Tuscaloosa County. The cousin suspected Alec of having an affair with his wife and told Cole that he would set Alex up for him to kill or capture. Cole traveled to a spot on the Tuscaloosa County line below Vance, and waited two days and nights eating out of a can and sleeping on the ground. The third day brought results, for the jealous cousin came riding along in a wagon going to the mill with Smith sitting beside him. At a prearranged spot, the cousin stopped the wagon to check the wheels. Cole eased out of the bushes and told Alec to throw up his hands and surrender. Smith grabbed for a rifle he had laying against the seat. Cole literally blew half of Alec's head off with both barrels of his ten gauge shotgun. Jealousy had paid off handsomely for the law, even if it was bounty hunting

Next, Cole started to trail Jim Morrison. Rumor had it that Morrison stayed holed up in a cave on the Cahaba River, venturing out only when his instinct told him it was safe.

At times Morrison caught a train from Blocton and traveled to Birmingham to "let his hair down." Staying holed up in a cave could get a man down. When he came back to Bibb County, Morrison brought his food with him, thus avoiding the chance of someone giving him away when he went to get groceries.

An alert railroad brakeman alerted Cole to Morrison's hiding place. Cole lay in wait for Jim Morrison for about a week. Finally on a Sunday afternoon, he spotted Morrison walking along a path from the river to the railroad grade where he caught the train to Birmingham. He was dressed in his Sunday best and did not seem to suspect that he was in danger. Cole called for him to halt. As Morrison turned to run off the path, he pulled his pistol from his coat. Cole cut him in half with his ten gauge. Cole flagged the train and carried Morrison's body into Blocton where it could be identified. This occurred in early March of 1907.

Henry Cole continued traveling by train back and forth to Jefferson County checking on Bud Redd's whereabouts. He needed to kill Redd in order to get his payment from the vigilante committee. They held the money until Redd was killed.

On April 1, 1907, Redd came on the train from Birmingham. He got off at the edge of West Blocton, fearing that Cole would kill him and his partner with him, Charlie Jones. The next morning, Redd and Jones caught the train to Eoline and lay around there all day. They were almost broke and needed to make a "score." Getting involved in a dice game did them no good because they lost what little change they had.

That night, the two broke into Daniel Ward's store in Eoline and stole some clothes and shoes. They found no money, however. They walked to Redd's father's house near Centreville and left the stolen property with him. The old man gave the two train fare to Tuscaloosa. They went there in search of work, while staying on the edge of town and dodging the law all the while. Finally the pair got a job cutting logs near the little hamlet of Buhl, just outside Tuscaloosa.

Meanwhile, someone in Eoline told Bibb County Sheriff Oakley about Redd and Jones being in Eoline the day before Ward's store was burglarized. The lawman and his deputies went to Redd's father's house. When the old man slammed the door in their faces, they kicked it down and handcuffed him when they discovered the stolen clothes and shoes. Old man Redd was taken to jail when he would not tell them where Bud was.

Someone discovered that Redd was in Buhl and notified the sheriff. Henry Cole was in Birmingham, so the sheriff sent his most fearless deputy, John W. Reddick, to check out this information. Deputy Reddick left Centreville on Sunday afternoon, April 8th. Once in Tuscaloosa, he enlisted the aid of Tuscaloosa County Deputy Zim Darden. The two officers went to a house in Buhl where they were told Redd was staying Reddick knocked on the door while Darden went around to a window by the chimney. When Reddick received no reply, he knocked the door down. In so doing, he received a load of buckshot which killed him almost instantly. The buckshot entered his neck on the right side and came out under his left arm. Deputy Darden was instantly covered by the men in the house but managed to duck away from the window and run into a patch of woods. He made his way to a Mr. M. B. Thompson's house where he telephoned the news of the tragedy.

Upon receiving news of the murder, Sheriff Latham of Tuscaloosa County, six deputies, Coroner J. Hol Fair, and several other volunteers left Tuscaloosa for the scene of the shooting The murder occurred about one o'clock, and the sheriff and his men arrived at Buhl about six o'clock.

After killing Deputy Reddick, the two culprits, Redd and Charlie Jones, took the pistol off the officer's body and left the house for a nearby swamp.

Bibb County Sheriff Oakley was notified of the death of his deputy about nine o'clock that night. The sheriff end a number of friends caught the through freight train for Tuscaloosa.

After an inquest was held concerning Deputy Reddick's murder, Deputies J. P. Suttle and R. L. Avery, intimate friends of the deceased, accompanied the remains to Georgetown, Kentucky, where his parents lived. Deputy Reddick had made himself a fearless reputation as a fine law officer while very young He served as a deputy under former Bibb County Sheriffs Vance: and Crawford. When Sheriff Oakley was elected, he was appointed chief deputy.

Bud Redd was the last of the Miller-Thrasher gang members left in "Bloody Bibb " The other members had either been assassinated by Henry Cole or disappeared from the county.

A few years earlier in Redd's infamous career, he served a sentence in the Bibb County for various crimes. It was during this stay that he had his first encounter with Deputy John W. Reddick. With the help of several others, Redd overpowered the deputy and escaped from the jail. While swimming across the Cahaba River, Redd was shot through his left hand. This is how his grudge against Reddick began.

After the murder of Deputy Reddick in Buhl, Redd and Jones fled from the scene. They came out of the Sipsey Swamp, crossed the river at Tuscaloosa, and continued up the Black Warrior River. This was familiar territory for Redd no doubt, for he had driven cattle across the area at one time with the MillerThrasher gang

The two men were starving for food, so they headed for the home of a Mr. Wetcher, an old friend of Redd's. Wetcher lived at Coaling, a small hamlet in Tuscaloosa County. Redd told the old man that he had killed Sheriff Oakley. The murderers in fact thought they had killed the sheriff instead of Deputy Reddick. Wetcher fed the pair, and they returned to hiding in the woods. They reappeared the next morning for breakfast too.

In the meantime, Mr. Wetcher, not wanting to get involved in anything as serious as murder, notified the authorities of the approximate location of the two men. After receiving the information, Sheriff Oakley went to Birmingham and secured the dogs of Sheriff Higdon. These dogs had a fine reputation for tracking a man and had helped bring some dangerous characters to justice. The posse was told by their informant, Mr. Wetcher, that the two men would eat supper that night at one of two places. The members of the posse surrounded the home of Wetcher and a neighbor, who Wetcher said Redd also knew, and waited for night to come. According to their informant, their quarry would not come to get food until after dark.

The men in the party waiting at the Wetcher home were Deputy Henry Cole, Albert Cleere and George Villersof Tuscaloosa. Henry Cole was restless as he waited for night to fall. He moved about forty yards away \*om Cleere and Villers. He felt a twinge of pos sessive jealousy toward the two deputies. It was as if they were encroaching on his territory. How many times had Bud Redd slipped through the traps Cole had set for him while he hunted down the other members of the Miller gang and shot them one by one? C ole wanted Redd badly. The other man was not so important, for in Cole's mind, he was just an accomplice that Redd had picked up along the way.

As twilight closed in, Cole thought of waiting for Bart Thrasher from the limb of a tree. He took in all of the sounds that filled the night air as he waited. A hunter by nature, Cole had dreamed as a kid of being a bounty hunter after reading some stories of Wyatt Earp and Doc Holliday. But hell, thought Cole, they were just outlaws turned lawmen at an opportune time. Funny he thought, so was he. Cole got into trouble as a boy in Jefferson County about a girl and had to leave home. Cole rode the horse into Birmingham and turned him loose. Later Cole's father came and found him and made him pay for the horse. The neighbor pressed no charges. Later the Tennessee Coal, Iron & Railroad Company hired Cole to guard its railroad yard. Then he was sent to Blocton to work around the coal mines they owned there. He obtained a commission from the Bibb County Sheriff and worked in conjunction with them and later was hired by the Blocton vigilante committee.

Snap! A stick broke a few yards away. Cole's thoughts came alive to the situation at hand. Another sound, and the hairs on Cole's neck bristled. He got that cool feeling in his stomach a hunter always gets, then a dryness in his throat almost caused him to cough. "Oh, no! Please Lord, don't let me cough now,' he thought. Cole cocked his ten-gauge shotgun and stared into the woods in front of him. He heard Villers and Cleere whispering Then he saw the outlaws! Redd and Jones were cautiously approaching the Wetcher house. Redd cocked his shotgun as he drew nearer. Upon hearing the gun cock, Cleere and Villers fired immediately, and Jones fell to the ground and threw his gun away from him. Redd fell to the ground but arose. Cole emptied both barrels of his ten gauge into Redd's chest. He observed the outlaw fall down again, away from the view of the other officers. Redd rose again, grunted and moved in to the bushes. Cole had reloaded quickly and fire another shot in the direction that Redd had gone. Then all was quiet and still. The three officers converged on Jones, who lay on the ground unhurt. He begged the lawmen not to shoot him. He was at once handcuffed.

One of the deputies went to the other house that was under survellance to report the news of the shooting The officers immediately came with the dogs and circled the woods but could pick up no tracks on the other side. This suggested that Redd was still in the vicinity of the woods where he had been shot. It was not advisable, however, to close in on the outlaw. He had nothing to lose if he ambushed another lawman. So the posse built a fire nearby and waited out the night. When it was light enough to see, the officers and dogs stealthily crept into the woods. The dogs began to whine, and the posse found them sniffing the dead body of Bud Redd. From the appearance of his body, it seemed that he had died the night before. The buckshot, undoubtedly from Cole's gun, had pierced Redd's heart and lungs. How he got up and stumbled on seemed to be a miracle.

Cole was observed cutting another notch on the stock of his ten gauge. This was his bounty hunting gun. Bud Redd seemed to be the last notch. "He's been lucky to live this long,' Cole thought. The posse then caught the morning train at Buhl and rode back to Tuscaloosa with Jones handcuffed to a deputy.

Upon questioning, Jones confessed to having fired one of the shots that killed Deputy Reddick. He claimed, however, that Redd had him under his power and threatened to kill him if he did not shoot the deputy. He admitted that he had shot Reddick in the shoulder, and Redd shot him in the neck. Like most punk criminals, Jones tried to lead the officers to believe that Redd had used some phenomenal psychic power over him and had made him enter into a life of crime. Jones also related that for the last several months, Redd had supervised him closely and told him what to do, including the burglary of Daniel Ward's store in Eoline. Jones said he was sitting in his house playing the banjo when Redd rushed in hollering that the officers were coming and would have to be killed. The two men reportedly talked about killing lawmen if they were crowded and then fleeing to Texas where, according to Redd, they would be safe from extradition. Redd feared Henry Cole, saying that Cole had "bushwhacked" all of his friends in the Miller-Thrasher gang. He knew that Cole would show him no mercy since he had outsmarted the bounty hunter on several occasions.

After reaching the jail at Tuscaloosa, Jones appeared quite cool and collected and talked freely to the officers. He did not seem to realize the seriousness of the charges against him. He also volunteered the fact that two of Redd's brothers were in the house where Reddick was shot. He said that he would gladly exculpate them by assuring the officers that the pair of brothers had absolutely nothing to do with the murder. In those days, justice was swift, especially in a case involving the murder of a law officer. Judge Foster, Tuscaloosa City Judge, immediately called a special session of the grand jury for an indictment against Jones. The end of Bud Redd's infamous career was considered the most important advancement in the goal of peace in "Bloody Bibb" since the shooting of Jim Morrison several years before by Henry Cole.

Jones was brought to trial, and a competent lawyer was appointed to defend him. The attorney was so competent in fact that he was able to play up the story of Jones' being under Redd's influence and power so convincingly that the jury was unable to agree on a verdict. The case ended in a mistrial! At a later trial, the jury recommended life in prison without parole. Jones died in prison years after his second trial.

After killing Redd, Henry Cole collected his fee from the vigilante committee. Cole decided to take some time offend "let his hair down." He thought he had the bluff on all of the people in "Bloody Bibb,' but he found out that he was not so popular after all.

How much Cole got for killing the four men is not known, but it is known that he had a lot of money with him on the night of May 1st. He got drunk that night at a saloon in Blocton and began bragging about his bounty hunting exploits. A big coal miner told him what he thought of a "back-shooting son of a bitch," as he put it. The miner then got up and started leaving the saloon. Cole shot the man through his arm with his pistol. Before he could get another shot off, the miner's friends mobbed him. They would have killed the boaster had another officer not intervened. Cole was escorted to the train station and left for Birmingham.

This incident occurred in May of 1907. Cole started working in Ensley, still in the employ of the Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad Company. He also worked as a deputy for Jefferson County. Jack Brown accepted a job in Blocton in Cole's place, since Cole's appearance in the coal mining town would probably have met with hostility. Cole worked in Jefferson County for the next three years in the capacity of railroad detective and deputy sheriff. He did not always meet with the approval of fellow officers there. The stigma of his bounty hunting days haunted him. He began to drink heavily, probably to salve his conscience against memories of the killings he had been paid to perform. No matter how you look at it, killing for hire amounts to cold blooded murder.

Detectives who worked with Cole from time to time found it hard to approve of Cole's unorthodox methods of arresting people. It was as if he wanted to cause a shootout over trivial things that came up pertaining to the railroad company. A lot of times, there were cases of burglary of railroad cars, and Cole wanted to shoot the suspects instead of arresting them and taking them to jail. It seemed that he had developed a personal possessiveness when it came to Tennessee Coal, Iron and Railroad Company property. In June of 1909, Cole shot to death one Albert Nix, of Johns, Alabama, which lies in the lower part of Jefferson County. There was much anger displayed by the citizens of J ohns because they felt Cole could have carried the man to jail rather than shooting him for such a minor offense as burglary.

John Wren, a detective from Birmingham, had words with Cole concerning the manner in which he had shot Nix. Wren vowed to dig up enough evidence to indict Cole for murder.

In March of 1912, Cole let some of his old traits surface when he shot and killed Felix J. Ellard near Lewisburg, in Jefferson County. At first, a man by the name of Thomas Duncan was arrested, indicted and sentenced to life in prison for the murder of Ellard. However, further investigations by detectives later revealed that Cole had actually killed the man! The devious man had testified that Duncan was the guilty party. Duncan, having no witnesses in his behalf and no money for a defense counsel, was tried and convicted on Cole's testimony.

Detective John Wren had just received enough evidence in the Albert Nix killing to get an indictment against Cole when the Ellard murder occurred. Cole was indicted by the grand jury, tried before a jury of 12 men and convicted by Judge C. A. Senn. He received a ten-year sentence after the jury deliberated all night. He maintained that Nix had tried to kill him with a knife, and that he shot the man in self-defense.

Thomas Duncan, who was kept in a separate part of the jail for protection against Cole, testified against the dishonest man. An indictment was secured against Cole for the shooting of Felix Ellard in March of 1912.

While in jail awaiting an appeal on the Nix murder conviction and a trial in the Ellard case, Cole talked at length with the sheriff of Jefferson County. Sheriff McAdory asked him if he had really killed 18 men. Cole denied this question, stating that he had only killed eight men, all of which were in self defense. This could hardly have been true, for he shot the Miller-Thrasher faction of outlaws and seldom ever gave them a "sporting chance" to defend themselves.

Cole must have lived through hell since he had sent so many men to jail and to the grave. He became despondent while lingering in jail awaiting his fate in the two murder cases. He must have wondered if it had all been worthwhile. His despondency continued, and he stated that he had lost the will to live.

On Christmas day in 1912, Cole had some visitors at the jail. In view of what happened later, it was believed that one of the callers slipped poison into his food. The night after Christmas, Cole's cell mate said he had been awakened by Cole. Cole said that he was sick so his cell mate put a cuspidor under his bunk. The ill man was breathing hard the next morning, so the turnkey was notified. The prison physician was immediately called in. As soon as the doctor made his examination, he stated that Cole would not live. The dying man was taken to Hillman Hospital. The doctors did all they could, but Cole died without regaining consciousness.

Henry Cole died at the age of 57, leaving a wife, two daughters and a son. Some of the most prominent men of Jefferson County were his pallbearers.

Is there something sinister about killing men if it is in self defense or done as a "civic duty"? There seems to be evidence that murder, even if it is done in the name of justice, may reverberate around a man and make him lose the will to live. Cole substantiated this belief. Consider also F.B.I. Agent Melvin Purvis who was instrumental in shooting down the John Dill linger gang in the 1930's. Purvis shot himself 30 years later. Consider also Judas, the betrayer of Jesus Christ. After accepting 13 pieces of silver, Judas also killed himself. He hung himself in grief over his dirty deed. The answer to the murder for hire dilemma will probably never be known, for these bounty hunters took their secrets to their graves and to the Great Unknown.
