<td width="100%"><h1 id="classical-guitar-lessons">Classical Guitar Lessons</h1>
<p>The purpose of my Column "The Art of Finger Dancing," is to give the classical guitarist of any level new techniques and insights to advance their creative thinking. With this column I hope to teach the traditional, because within the traditional lies a foundation. However, I hope to stimulate the guitarist with inspiration to reach for new sounds and concepts above and beyond the traditional. "The Art of Finger Dancing" column is designed to be a technique column because only through technique are all things possible.</p>

<h2 id="also-by-logan-l.-gabriel">Also by Logan L. Gabriel</h2>
<p><strong><a href="20000627.html">Rock Guitarist as Classical Guitarist Part 1</a></strong><br />
<em>by Logan L. Gabriel (27 Jun 2000)</em><br />
Over my six years of teaching classical guitar to rock players there seems to be a recurring problem.  The right hand.  Yes, the right hand.  The left hand is not the culprit, it has been put through the ropes time and time again with scales, chords, so on and so forth.  The right hand has never let go of the pick, and when it has the fingers usually assumed the hand position of a folk or blues player and lacked stability, accuracy, tone, volume and any real control.</p>
<p><strong><a href="20000704.html">Rock Guitarist as Classical Guitarist Part 2 - Finger Movement</a></strong><br />
<em>by Logan L. Gabriel (04 Jul 2000)</em><br />
In the last article we discussed hand positioning, and hopefully you have been practicing the exercise I gave you.  You've no doubt have listened to classical guitarists before and have noticed their warm, balanced, tone.  If you have been practicing the exercise I gave you will probably have noticed that tone and volume is something that is lacking.  Do not fear, this article will put you on your way to good tone and clarity.</p>
<p><a href="20000711.html"><strong>Rock Guitarist as Classical Guitarist Part 3 - Finger Placement and Finger Preparation<br />
</strong></a><em>by Logan L. Gabriel (11 Jul 2000)</em><br />
I was originaly going to write this article about just finger placement, then it dawned on me that finger placement and finger preparation go hand in hand. Finger placement deals with playing the string from the same point of the finger. Finger preparation is just that, preparing the finger in such a way so that the muscles responsible for finger action are trained to place the finger at the same spot each time.</p>
<p><strong><a href="20000718.html">The Art of Practice</a></strong><br />
<em>by Logan L. Gabriel (18 Jul 2000)</em><br />
Ever since you have started your instrument you have no doubt heard everyone say "practice, practice, practice".  Well, what does that mean?  Is practice the monotonous repetition of certain exercises and chords and scales?  Is it the same thing day in and day out?  My answer is hardly not.  Practice, like your instrument needs to be practiced.  (2000-07-18)</p>
<p><a href="20000725.html"><strong>Molding and Shaping</strong></a><br />
<em>by Logan L. Gabriel (25 Jul 2000)</em><br />
The guitar is a kind of voice box, it's language is abstract but very communicative and exacting. Just as the voice needs inflection in order to convey emotional content so does the musical line. The tools of the trade are simple in theory but difficult in practice.</p>
<p><a href="20000801.html"><strong>Posture</strong></a><br />
<em>by Logan L. Gabriel (01 Aug 2000)</em><br />
How may of us just grab the guitar, take a seat and begin to play? How many of you really understand the importance of proper posture, it's effect on your playing and risk of injury.  More and more I see beginners sit in the way their favorite player does, without regards to their body.</p>
<p><a href="20000808.html"><strong>Scales, Warp Factor 10</strong></a><br />
<em>by Logan L. Gabriel (08 Aug 2000)</em><br />
Before we dive into the realm of developing speed, let's dispel a few misconceptions about it.  Firstly, speed in and of itself is irrelevant. The second misconception of speed is that most people believe in order to play fast you must practice fast.</p>
<p><a href="20000818.html"><strong>Speed Bursts</strong></a><br />
<em>by Logan L. Gabriel (18 Aug 2000)</em><br />
While it is helpful to know your scales and play them quickly, it is not necessary to play them at break neck speed for lengthy periods of time.  One of the things that can help us develop stamina to hold out during those passages is the daily practice of speed bursts.</p>
<p><a href="20001016.html"><strong>Improvisation in Classical Music Part I</strong></a><br />
<em>by Logan L. Gabriel (16 Oct 2000)</em><br />
When guitar began to be taught in universities there was a lot of emphasis given to what we call the "standard repertoire." This is why if you pick up a cd of classical guitar music the program on the disk is pretty much made up of music by a few composers.  Hence, people have been hearing the same thing for so long we are doomed to play what they like or we do not get gigs.  This in effect stifles the classical guitarists creative thinking because we spend so much time studying and preparing these standard works.</p>
<p><a href="20001025.html"><strong>Improvisation in Classical Music Part II</strong></a><br />
<em>by Logan L. Gabriel (25 Oct 2000)</em><br />
This week we look at some ways you can build your "improvisatory vocabulary." Leave no chord unturned.  And do not buy a chord book.  Chord books are evil because they suppress the analytical thought process.</p>
<p><a href="20001108.html"><strong>Rasgueados</strong></a><br />
<em>by Logan L. Gabriel (08 Nov 2000)</em><br />
Rasgueados serve two very important purposes.  The first being a technical exercise, the second being a way with which to add rythmic variety to a harmonic accompaniment. To perform this technique let's first learn to fight our natural tendencies.</p></td>
