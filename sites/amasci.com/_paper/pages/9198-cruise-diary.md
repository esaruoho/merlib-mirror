<span class="small"><strong><em>Day 1 – Saturday, Mar. 15</em></strong></span><br />
<br />
We finished the packing and went to bed early on Friday night. Dave, who had generously agreed to drive us to the airport, slept on the couch. At the wee small hour of 2:00 am, we got up and ate a quick breakfast, loaded the car, and proceeded to Sea-Tac. Becca was delighted that she got to ride the whole way up front, Dave's car not having a passenger-side airbag.<br />
<br />
At 3:30 in the morning, the airport is at its lowest ebb. Not even the coffee places opened until four, let alone the check-in desks. We said good-bye to Dave and settled in to wait in front of the desk until we could get our tickets and drop off our luggage. Then it was more waiting in the terminal, followed by a 6:00 am departure.<br />
<br />
The flight, which had originated from Alaska, was packed full. Turbulence all the way, too. Tim was able to doze but I couldn't get to sleep. I read an entire book – <em>Midnight Whispers</em>, John Saul – on the way. Becca had her Gameboy, one of Santa's better ideas.<br />
<br />
The Houston airport is huge, and when we finally got there we trudged for what felt like miles to baggage claim. There, we met up with Tim's family (his parents Dottie and John, and his sister Kathy). The lot of us piled into a van and were taken to the cruise terminal, where we boarded the <em>Royal Olympia Voyager</em>.<br />
<br />
The ship was fairly small by cruise ship standards, but nice. Seven decks. Our cabins were on deck 4, on the right side of the ship facing forward (I still have never learned which is port and which is starboard). Each cabin had two single beds and a fold-down bunk bed, a decent-sized window, a fridge, a closet of a bathroom, a closet of a closet, a safe, a television, and assorted other bits of furniture. A lot of mirrors. Way too many mirrors. <br />
<br />
And slightly bizarre artwork on the wall, black and white paintings that were more like psychiatrist's inkblots than art. The one on our wall, we decided after much deliberation, was a Dalmatian dog in a beret, ripping a page out of a book with its teeth, underwater, while a narwhal (well, Tim insists it was a bomb, not a narwhal) lurked in the background. Numbered in pencil, #154 out of 202. Couldn't decipher the artist's signature.<br />
<br />
Most of the afternoon went by in a flurry of unpacking and assorted errands – signing Becca up for the kids' program, confirming excursion tickets, etc. We all reported as ordered for the lifeboat drill, standing there crammed in like sardines in bright orange life vests while the ship left Houston. None of that lining-the-rails confetti-tossing <em>Love Boat</em> stuff on this trip.<br />
<br />
Then it was time for dinner. We'd requested first seating, so that we'd at least stand a chance of getting Becca to bed at something approximating a normal hour (got to give us credit for trying, anyway). The food was pretty good, and I had made a deal with myself that I would try at least some new and unusual things on this trip. So, when the appetizer was a sort of seafood mixture, including tiny baby squids, baked in a puff pastry, I ate it. Tentacles and all. Wasn't too bad. Puff pastry, anyway, can excuse a lot. Later in the trip, Tim would reject escargot, puff pastry or not.<br />
<br />
John was brave enough to try the chilled banana soup, which would turn out to be only the first of a series of chilled fruit soups. He said that it was like a milkshake, but the rest of us took his word for it. Becca made a friend of Kostas, our bread guy, and most of her dinners would consist mainly of sesame-seed rolls and butter. I'd told her that she could only eat chicken nuggets one meal a day – this was after going on vacations with her in which she'd have them for lunch and dinner if she could, and even breakfast should we dine at someplace like Denny's. But it turned out that the ship did not serve chicken nuggets as a staple. Indeed, in the entire 9 days, they only put in one appearance on the menu. She now claims that I owe her chicken nuggets for all the extra days.<br />
<br />
We were all pretty exhausted from a long day of traveling, and Becca and I went to bed early as we sailed south into the Gulf of Mexico. The others stayed up a bit later, catching the evening show.<br />
<br />
<br />
<strong><em>Day 2 – Sunday, Mar. 16</em></strong> <br />
<br />
On our first full day at sea, which was also a day of rocking waves, mostly cloudy skies, and a brisk wind, Tim and Becca and I rose at 7:00 am for the morning buffet. We found a nice spot in the deck 6 piano lounge, where we would end up spending a lot of our spare time, and played our cunning little travel version of the terrific board game <em>Settlers of Catan</em> .<br />
<br />
I sneaked over to the ship's library to leave a copy of <em>Curse of the Shadow Beasts</em> on the shelf amid all the other paperbacks – ha, ha, the masked author strikes again! – and picked up copies of that day's crossword and brain teaser. There wasn't a lot in the way of on-board activities, but whenever we were able, we tried to do the puzzles and make it to the afternoon trivia contest. Yiorgos, the assistant cruise director, was one of the highlights of the trip. He was a trim little fellow with sharp good looks and an accent, spoke six languages, and overflowed with energy and charm.<br />
<br />
Now, as anyone who's cruised before may have noticed, median ages on these boats tends to be about 55 or more. A lot of older folks, a scattering of 20's to 40's, and not very many kids. At 10:30, Becca joined some of the other "junior cruisers" for a tour of the bridge, a coloring contest, and crafts. They let her sit in the captain's chair and took a photo, which we were allowed to purchase later for 15 bucks.<br />
<br />
By then, the rest of the family had wakened, and they all went to a presentation on the upcoming excursions. Well, presentation might not be the right term … sales pitch? Anyway, we had already signed up for ours in advance. There was also an ongoing art auction, some guy with a bunch of paintings, animation cels (no gargoyles! not even <em>Fantasia</em>!), and sports card memorabilia. Tim's dad won a painting, free except that he got to shell out for the shipping.<br />
<br />
Becca and I felt a little seasick, but that didn't stop us from trying out the shuffleboard courts and the pool. Actually, I found the pool to be something of a remedy for motion sickness. The water in it, salt by the way, was sloshing crazily all over the place. It was easy to trick the mind that instead of being on a swaying ship, we were in a wave pool. That it was the water moving, and not the ship, if that makes any sense.<br />
<br />
Tim and I attended the trivia session. Teams of four, so we were grouped with a couple of nice ladies named Roxy and Irene. We didn't do too badly, though the questions were definitely aimed more at the target passenger age demographic.<br />
<br />
Dinner that night was the formal affair. We got all spiffed up and dropped in at the captain's cocktail party, then went to the dining room. Lamb, they served a lot of lamb on the ship, it being a Greek ship and all.<br />
<br />
And of course, the desserts. My intention, since I had been hit with the bad news about my recurrence of thyroid cancer two days before we left, was to basically forget about eating right and drown my sorrows in desserts and daiquiris. I only had one daiquiri the entire trip – I have a very low tolerance for alcohol – but substituted the non-alcoholic "strawberry whip." But I did eat many more desserts than I needed over the course of those nine days.<br />
<br />
Tim, John, and Kathy dropped some money at the blackjack tables. I watched, while Becca wheedled Grandma Dottie into buying her a package of Skittles in one of the shipboard shops. Then it was time to call it a night.<br />
<br />
<br />
<em><strong>Day 3 – Monday, Mar. 17</strong></em><br />
<br />
Our first activity this morning was a date in the kids' room with Jacqui, the kids' cruise director. Becca and I were the only ones to show up. We made a craft involving glue, crumpled up bits of colored crepe paper, and cardstock templates shaped like fish.<br />
<br />
Early that afternoon, we arrived at the island of Roatan, in Honduras. Becca, Dottie, Kathy and I had a planned trip to go swimming with dolphins, so we hopped in a taxi and off we went. I had been thinking that my cab ride through New York would be the most unnerving of my life, but that was before I got in the beat-up little car that careened through the narrow, chicken-filled streets. The driver honked his horn more over those 20 minutes than I have ever used the one in my car.<br />
<br />
We got to a resort called Anthony's Key, which has a marine science center with models and diagrams of sea life. After watching a short demonstration of dolphin tricks, we were loaded onto a small motorboat and taken over to another, and much littler, island. There, we got into our swimsuits and waded out into the water.<br />
<br />
Up until that moment, we'd all been hot and sweaty and cross, and second-guessing whether we'd really wanted to do this. But the water, clear and just the perfect temperature over a gentle bed of sand, washed away all doubts. And that was even before we got to meet Gracie!<br />
<br />
Thirteen years old, sleek and deep grey, Gracie was our dolphin. The excursion-goers were divided into four groups of eight, each working with one trainer and one dolphin, while a couple of the younger dolphins swam about and buzzed the groups as they pleased. One of the girls in our group was scared of them, didn't like them, and I think the dolphins could tell. They seemed to make a point of exhaling through their blowholes right at her, spraying her, making her squeal.<br />
<br />
Being right there in the water with them was amazing. They didn't feel like I expected. Their hides were like thick, slick rubber. Almost more like a really good special effect than a living creature. But then, when we got to put our arms under them – at this point, the dolphins would arch their heads and tails out of the water, great photo op – I could feel Gracie's heart thumping, so strong, and it really struck me that this was a live, intelligent creature.<br />
<br />
We had plenty of opportunities to take pictures, and there was also a guy from the resort shooting video. Which, of course, we had plenty of opportunities to buy later when they took us straight to the shop and made us sit through it. We didn't need the video, though I did buy Becca a ceramic toucan and myself a clever little box. I love clever little boxes, this one of different hues of wood with a nice satiny finish and a neat shape.<br />
<br />
While we were having our dolphin adventure, Tim and his dad decided to be risky and just hired a cab for the day. Their driver, Carol, took them all over the island and directed them to a local eatery where they had skewered beef and shrimp that was basically soaked in butter and grilled. They also stopped by a market and came back to the ship each toting a case of cans. Soda for Tim, who goes through it at a pace that would have bankrupted us had he bought his quota on board, and beer for John.<br />
<br />
I took Becca to the kids' room again, where we were once more the only ones to show up for the scheduled event. We played a couple of cards of Bingo. The show that night was a comedian/magician, and we went to that one. He told the joke about the guy who goes to the movie theater with a duck down his pants, the old "this one's eating my popcorn" one, and I couldn't believe that Tim had never heard it.<br />
<br />
We got a surprise that night as we were getting ready for bed. The ship's television had only half a dozen or so channels. TNT, TNT dubbed into Spanish, CNN (which got a lot of watching once the war began), a "mast cam" that showed the exterior of the ship, a computer graphic of our course and location, and a channel that was reserved for documentaries on the Caribbean and Central America. When we turned to the documentary channel, we were treated to the one and only Keith David narrating a show about sea life.<br />
<br />
<br />
<em><strong>Day 4 – Tuesday, Mar. 18</strong></em><br />
<br />
For a vacation, we sure never seemed to get much rest. We had to be up, dressed, fed, and ready to go at the crack of dawn for our drive to Copan. If there's one main complaint I have about the excursions, it's that they were not adequately described in the brochures. The length of the bus rides, the necessary physical fitness required, etc.<br />
<br />
Four hours on a bus. We'd spent four hours on the airplane. This drive was through mainland Honduras, and the drivers were just as daredevil and horn-loving as the taxi driver on Roatan. We would roar down roads and cyclists, people, kids, or dogs would be at the edge of the pavement, and the bus driver would honk like hell to warn them, but never would he slow down or even swerve. It's a wonder we didn't run anybody down.<br />
<br />
The poverty in Honduras was like nothing I'd ever seen before. Dwellings that were hovels in every possible sense of the word, single rooms, roofs of corrugated metal, some even with walls of cardboard. Lines of laundry hanging right by the side of the road in the dust. Naked children loose in fenceless yards. Guys running alongside the bus waving bags of fruit to sell. It was painful to see.<br />
<br />
At last, after a pit stop at a gas station and mini mart, we made it to the site at Copan. This was the best part of the trip for me. Copan was incredible. I'd read some tour books, watched videos, thought I was ready. But what a difference, seeing the ruins in person. The sense of history was awe-inspiring. Not just history, but human history. People had done this. Thousands of years ago. Without metal tools, without the wheel, without any of the modern conveniences that we can't do without, they had built these fabulous structures.<br />
<br />
More, it was a place I could easily imagine people living. The Maya pyramids aren't monuments to death and colossal ego, the way the ones in Egypt are. There was a feeling of life and activity. These buildings had been <em>used</em> , they served a functional living purpose.<br />
<br />
I was also impressed by the sheer stamina that the ancient Maya must have possessed. They were a short people. Four, four and a half feet. But their stairs were a challenge for folks considerably taller. Big steps! And lots of them, too. It was quite a workout. I spent a lot of time herding Becca to make sure she didn't go tumbling down the side.<br />
<br />
The visit to the ruins was followed by an outdoor lunch and a museum that had recreated parts of Copan we weren't allowed to see. The hidden temple of Rosalila, for instance, which had been discovered intact underneath one of the larger structures. We bought a bunch of souvenirs, including some bits of stonework, and took lots of pictures.<br />
<br />
One of the pieces I found the most interesting was Zotz, the leaf-nosed bat god who was depicted as basically a gargoyle. He had arms <em>and</em> bat wings. He also, disturbingly, had a big pointy erection. Tim bought a statuette of Zotz before he noticed that particular part, and we figured that we'd probably better not let Becca take that one to school to show her classmates.<br />
<br />
Hot and dusty and sore from the climbing, we piled back into our bus and had another four-hour ride back to the ship. We missed the dinner seatings but caught a late buffet, and then Tim and I had drinks on the mostly deserted pool deck while we watched the full moon.<br />
<br />
<br />
<em><strong>Day 5 – Wednesday, Mar. 19</strong></em><br />
<br />
I had anticipated that Becca would want a break, so she and I hadn't signed up for any excursions today. The rest of the family left to take a riverboat up the Rio Dulce once we'd docked in Guatemala. Becca and I stayed behind.<br />
<br />
One thing about ships this big is that they have to have big docking facilities, too. Roatan was all right, but not exactly what I'd expected in a tropical port. Honduras and Guatemala were worse, because we were smack in the industrial district. Acres of cargo containers and concrete, cranes whining, lots of trucks. Not terribly exotic or romantic. At least from Guatemala, we could see some jungle-covered hills.<br />
<br />
At this port, we were advised against wandering around outside the gates of the docking area. A line of booths had been set up along the pier, selling handicrafts and other items, and we were told that it'd be wisest, if we weren't on an official excursion, to do any souvenir shopping there. Becca and I took a quick walk down there, very quick because it was blisteringly hot that day, and got a couple of tee shirts.<br />
<br />
The rest of the day, she and I relaxed on the ship. We swam, we caught her up on her homework (her teacher had sent some along, as well as telling her to keep a trip journal of her own and do her regular nightly reading), I wrote and mailed a few postcards, and we went to a close-up magic show by the comedian/magician from the other night. Card tricks, and he was pretty good.<br />
<br />
The others made it back just in time for dinner. It was Becca's first full meal, as she had been living on bread, desserts, hash browns and milk for breakfast, and occasional bites pinched off our plates. But tonight, they served a fried rice dish with chicken and shrimp, and she stuffed herself.<br />
<br />
Tim had missed a few spots with the sunscreen that day, and barbecued part of his leg, his wrist around his watchband, and one side of his face (think Richard Dreyfuss from <em>Close Encounters of the Third Kind</em>).<br />
<br />
The evening's entertainment was another magic show by a Ukranian husband-and-wife team. He was really good, she was so-so, and in between acts we got the Global Dancers in their feathers and skimpy showgirl style outfits.<br />
<br />
<br />
<em><strong>Day 6 – Thursday, Mar. 20</strong></em><br />
<br />
Another early morning, but this time we abandoned the buffet in favor of the dining room. Eggs Benedict and pineapple juice, yum! And watermelon … I think I ate watermelon at least twice a day, maybe more.<br />
<br />
We were anchored about two miles offshore of Belize City, and had to take ferryboats called "tenders" to get to shore. All five of us went on the day's trip, which was one of the most misleading in terms of what we'd been led to expect. It was yet another long bus ride, way out in the middle of nowhere to a place called Jaguar Paw Resort, where we learned just what we would have to go through in order to go cave-tubing.<br />
<br />
Nowhere in the description had it mentioned just how far we'd have to hike through the jungle in our swimsuits, lugging our inner tubes and life vests. Nor did we know we'd have to wade across the river, walk on paths of loose sharp rocks, or any of that. Dottie and John backed out, but the rest of us trudged grimly on.<br />
<br />
I managed to twist both ankles, thanks to wet feet slipping around inside sandals that had never been meant for wading rivers and walking on loose rocks. Becca damn near lost her flipflops, and only the quick action of our guide saved them from being swept downstream. Deirdre, a little girl we'd met on board, did lose one of hers.<br />
<br />
At any rate, we were miserably hot and grumpy by the time we got to the entrance to the cave. The sight did mollify much of our grievances, because it was really pretty awesome, the cavernous tunnel and river leading away into the darkness. We strapped miner's lights on our heads and plunked down butt-first in our inner tubes, and paddled down into the black.<br />
<br />
Becca got a free ride, hanging onto our feet. Some dogs made the caves their home, but the guides had warned us so we weren't too shocked when an animal suddenly swam past. The water was cool and pure (or so they told us; we still didn't drink any), and at several places the river passed caves that opened onto daylight, with hanging green foliage and cool rock formations.<br />
<br />
We had to get out at one point and walk, dripping and lugging our tubes again, across a shallow stretch. At some point in the journey, Tim lost the plastic baggie that had contained his socks and his wallet. Thankfully, he'd had the foresight to take most of the stuff out of his wallet, including his driver's license. He wound up losing one credit card, some cash, and his ship's cabin key (which was also his ticket on and off the boat). It was a pain, but it could have been a lot worse.<br />
<br />
Eventually, we floated out near the resort, and got out of the river. Dottie and John had talked one of the guides into giving them a smaller tour, without so much hiking, and they met us when we emerged. We got dried off and dressed, and ran into a snafu over the lunch thing. We'd been supposed to have lunch at the resort, but weren't actually registered for it, so we had to get back on the bus and return to Belize City.<br />
<br />
There, we spent a little while in what they called Tourist Village, looking in shops, before catching a tender back to the ship. We had missed lunch there, too, but they were serving tea and cookies in the buffet room and pizza at poolside, so we finally got some chow.<br />
<br />
That night was "Greek Night" at dinner, so everyone was expected to wear blue and white like the Greek flag, eat more lamb, and shout "Opa!" a lot (which is confusing to me, because while it means "Huzzah" or something in Greek, in German it's the affectionate term for "Grandpa.").<br />
<br />
After dinner, the Ukranian magicians from the other night were doing a close-up magic show in the kids' room. We took Becca to that, and finally collapsed into bed.<br />
<br />
<br />
<em><strong>Day 7 – Friday, Mar. 21</strong></em><br />
<br />
The big day had arrived, the day of the Spring Equinox! The ship arrived at the port of Playa del Carmen, and this one, in my mind anyway, really <em>looked</em> like a proper tropical Caribbean port. It had gorgeous blue-green water rolling in toward white sand, a bustling dockside with coral-colored hotels, thatch-roof beach cabanas, windsurfers, etc.<br />
<br />
And we didn't get to enjoy it, because they packed us onto a larger tender, marched us quickly through town (past a Subway and a McD's, which made Becca whimper), and loaded us onto buses for another, you guessed it, 4-hour drive!<br />
<br />
I had the bad luck to end up sitting over the rear wheel, so the floor of the bus was sloped and put weird pressure on my knees the whole way. By the time we got back, I would be hobbling. But first, we got to drive through empty and really kind of boring countryside, way out into the middle of nowhere, to Chichen Itza.<br />
<br />
They gave us a nice lunch on a shaded patio at a hotel, where there were gift shops, caged parrots and black birds whose cries sounded like 4th of July bottle rockets, and dancers who could twirl with trays of drinks balanced on their heads. But then, our guide rounded us up to walk out to the site.<br />
<br />
Oh, and it was hot. The hottest yet. And steamy, because the sky was heavily overcast. And crowded, because close to 45, 000 people converged on the site on this day of the year (though we found out that the phenomenon was actually visible for two weeks on either side of the date).<br />
<br />
In ancient times, the Maya had cleared all the trees for a long ways around their city for their farmland. Copan's had regrown, and some trees did surround Chichen Itza, but the bulk of it was wide open, shadeless glaring heat. With ambulances parked around, as if just waiting to see who'd be the first heatstroke of the day.<br />
<br />
The main pyramid was damned impressive. It was enormous. Staggering. But by the time we got to the ball court, and were hearing the rules for the umpteenth time, as well as what we'd already learned about in the books and videos (which was basically that no one knows for sure whether the losing team or the winning team were the ones who'd be sacrificed), I knew I couldn't take much more. Neither could Becca.<br />
<br />
We were hot and cranky, and because they'd dragged us here so early when the phenomenon didn't take place until sunset, we had three more hours to get through before the shadow of the serpent descended the side of the pyramid. If it even did, because it was cloudy.<br />
<br />
So the two of us wimped out. We went back to the shaded patio to wait it out. I bought a coloring book of rain forest animals and a deck of cards, and while this mystic event was going on, we sat and had ice creams and played Crazy Eights. The rest of the Morgans were doggedly determined to stick it out, but Becca and I just couldn't do it.<br />
<br />
And it turned out I was glad we didn't stand around out there and broil, because the clouds did interfere so there wouldn't have been anything to see anyway. They parted for about seven minutes, after most of the crowd had given up, so it turned out that of the five of us, only Kathy, who had lingered to look around, caught any of the show.<br />
<br />
Tim had found more stands selling stuff, and came back with two types of carved wooden jaguar heads. It was a challenge getting them into the suitcases when it was time to pack, but they are neat. I got a map of the area that will prove useful next time I run a game.<br />
<br />
That long, grueling day seemed like it would never end. The 4-hour bus ride back got us to Playa del Carmen well after dark, and in time for the very last tender across to the ship. Of course, <em>all</em> the buses had arrived then, and 500 of the 800 passengers had signed up for Chichen Itza. They dangerously overloaded the tender and took 45 minutes to get us all on, and over, and off again.<br />
<br />
We grabbed some food, just barely making the late buffet before it closed, and collapsed into bed while the ship traveled about 9 nautical miles to the next port.<br />
<br />
<br />
<em><strong>Day 8 – Saturday, Mar. 22</strong></em><br />
<br />
Once again, Becca and I had different plans from the rest of the family. The others had all signed up for a trip to more ruins, at Coba. Boy, were they regretting the prospect of another long bus rise, another hot day, <em>another</em> guide going on about the rules of the ball game. There was some talk of canceling, but in the end they went.<br />
<br />
We were docked at Cozumel, which was almost as nice as Playa del Carmen. The water was the most unbelievable shade of rich turquoise that I have ever seen. It didn't look real, that's how blue it was. More like something out of a retouched travel poster.<br />
<br />
The effect was marred somewhat by the gunships patrolling the harbor, and the guards with automatic weapons stationed along the pier. I don't know if it's always like that, or if they had stepped up security on account of the situation in Iraq.<br />
<br />
Since one of the only channels we got was CNN, and since a fair number of older folks are habitual news junkies, we had seen a lot of coverage of war developments. It seemed at once weirdly distant and alarming. Here we were, on our cruise ship with attentive and friendly crew, visiting all these ports of call, removed from our real lives and the real world … yet there was a war going on. I found it personally rather ironic, given that the last book I finished writing was one about a group of people on a Caribbean island when WWIII broke out …<br />
<br />
But we weren't going to let our worries interfere with our vacation. Becca and I joined about 35 other passengers on the pier – moored near us was a simply gorgeous 4-masted clipper ship, registered in Luxembourg, a fact that irritated Tim no end – waiting for our glass-bottom boat.<br />
<br />
And then the shore excursion director and the tour guide got into a fight. The guide told us that the boat's air conditioning was broken, so would we mind delaying the tour until 11:00? That was two hours away. And Paula, the shore excursion gal, started in on him about how would we know it was going to be ready by eleven, and she wasn't going to let him waste our time. They were nose to nose, yelling at each other, while we all stood around with our mouths open.<br />
<br />
It ended when Paula announced that she was canceling the excursion. She snatched our tickets out of our hands, said we'd all be credited a refund, and stormed up the gangplank. The tour guy made a big angry speech about how "she has spoken!" and stormed off in the other direction.<br />
<br />
Well, there we were, and nobody knew what to do. It was ludicrous. For a minute there, I'd thought they were going to start throwing slaps and punches at each other. Then another mom, who had her toddler and her 6-year-old niece, declared that she was going to see if there were any other companies that had glass-bottom boat tours. Becca and I decided to tag along with her. So did her parents, and another couple.<br />
<br />
It turned out to be a good move. The nine of us hired a boat for 30 bucks a person (15 less than we'd arranged through the cruise ship), and went out. No air conditioning … but it was an open boat! I had to wonder just what the heck kind of boat the original tour would have been on. Air conditioning. What was the point, when we had a gorgeous sea breeze and kicked-up spray from this awesome turquoise water?<br />
<br />
We got a two-hour ride, seeing starfish, a lone manta, sand dollars, sea urchins, coral, and a colorful variety of fish ranging from teeny to four-foot-long parrot fish with big green clown lips. I think we had a better time, only a few of us on a small boat, than we would have in a big group, air conditioning be damned.<br />
<br />
And at last, there in Cozumel, Mexico, Becca finally got her long-awaited chicken nuggets. There was a McD's overlooking the ocean, complete with playplace. The only thing wrong was that their ice cream machine was broken so we couldn't have shakes. After I took her to lunch, we wandered some shops and went back to the ship to do more homework and swimming and playing of Crazy Eights (she kicked my butt six out of seven games, as well as beating both grandparents and Kathy).<br />
<br />
The others got back just in time for dinner again, and afterwards, though they were tired, they wanted to go ashore and check out the shops. I stayed behind this time and did some writing on the aft deck. I managed to get a prologue and first chapter done on the trip, writing longhand in a poor spiral notebook that got waterlogged at one point so its blue lines were all smeary. It seemed kind of strange to be sitting on a cruise ship, a "strawberry whip" at hand, scribbling a horror novel. But it felt pretty good, too.<br />
<br />
<br />
<em><strong>Day 9 – Sunday, Mar. 23</strong></em><br />
<br />
Our final full day, entirely at sea. The ocean as we headed north for Houston was a deep cobalt blue with white froth, with a cool wind that made the pool deck almost too chilly to sit in the shade.<br />
<br />
We slept in (at least, compared to what we'd been doing) and had a nice long lazy breakfast. Tim and I foisted Becca off on Kathy, who took her to the kids' program scavenger hunt. She came in second because she couldn't find a cork and didn't want to sing at the reception desk on deck 5.<br />
<br />
We got online on the ship's computers – Tim had done so briefly a few days ago to try and cancel the credit card lost in the underground river, since the same $21.00 that would buy us 100 minutes of internet time would only get us 8 minutes of ship-to-shore telephone. Moot point, since they wouldn't cancel the card over the internet. He had to wait and call from Houston.<br />
<br />
But anyway, we had a lot of minutes left over. I posted to my LiveJournal, and checked out a few of my usual online haunts. It just figures, the one time in my life I leave the country, I'm suddenly sought after. One of the guys at SJ Games was trying to get in touch with me, and posted a notice to the Pyramid message boards. It's nice to be wanted!<br />
<br />
Becca got in her last swim. By then, her poor bathing suit was so stretched out and worn through and generally falling apart that we tossed it in the garbage. She went to the kids' farewell party while Tim and I packed, and then got our butts totally kicked at the last trivia contest.<br />
<br />
That final dinner featured free champagne and the all-star of cruise ship desserts, the parade of Baked Alaska! Oh, but the look on Becca's face when the waiters streamed in with flaming heaps of meringue-covered ice cream … her eyes lit up, and not just from the reflections of the leaping blue and orange brandy-fueled fire.<br />
<br />
We had gotten everything packed, miracle of miracles, even the jaguar heads and the banana paper book. We had to have our luggage, except for carry-ons, labeled and out in the hall before midnight. That led to some creative planning as we tried to figure out what to do with the stuff we'd need for that night, but we got it all worked out and fell asleep to the rocking of the ship.<br />
<br />
<br />
<em><strong>Day 10 – Monday, Mar. 24</strong></em><br />
<br />
We had to be out of our cabins by 8:00 am, when the ship docked in Houston. We spent two hours waiting around in the piano lounge while the crew organized everyone for clearance by the Immigration officials. Then they disembarked us in groups according to what airline or other travel arrangements we'd made, which was supposed to streamline the luggage-finding process when we got ashore into the terminal.<br />
<br />
It didn't, not much. There was a lot of milling around and confusion. But eventually, we had our bags and were on our bus to the airport. Short bus ride, this time, less than an hour. People remarked on how much cheaper the gas prices were than in their various home towns.<br />
<br />
We got to the airport, managed to slip away from the masses and check our bags, and then have a final family lunch at a restaurant inside. The flight that Tim, Becca and I were on was first, and four hours later we were back in cool, grey, rainy Seattle. Dave picked us up at the airport – great guy! – and drove us home.<br />
<br />
It had been a great vacation, a really good time. I doubt any of us would want to do the same trip again, and there were parts of it – the cave tubing and Chichen Itza in particular – that left something to be desired, but I think we're all glad we did it this once.<br />
