Gary Gullikson \< 

 \> wrote:

I have been having a ball flying a Dare (Pat Tritle design) "Marionette" indoors in a school gym, and outdoors in calm morning air. I particularly like the way it turns in an 8-foot circle without losing altitude.

The plane looks like a simplified Santos-Dumont Demoiselle. I bought the kit at Hobby People for \$30; it consists of a few sticks, some laser-cut ribs, some thread and plans. You add the DXA geardrive, GWS Pico flight pack and two 830 mAh Lithium Ion cells (two 20-minute flights per charge) -- or a 270 mAh NiMH pack (15 minutes).

I had to extend the bottom fuselage slab to get the battery pack forward enough for proper balance. I also added some curved bamboo skewer landing skids. I made wheels out of large balsa discs with small-diameter screen door rubber spline for the tires. The hub is small-diameter plastic tubing to fit the axles.

The plane is supposed to be covered with Coverlite or Litespan, etc. Trying to shrink the single (top) surface covering made my wings bow upwards alarmingly. I recommend not shrinking the Coverlite; just get it as snug as possible when attaching it to the wing frame with Balsarite and sealing iron.

I am using a GWS heat-sink radiator on the DXA motor to keep it cool on long flights.

I'm currently finishing a Guillow 28" span PT-17, using the same GWS gear and blue and yellow Coverlite. Target weight is about 7-8 ounces. If DXA power is insufficient, I will install a new GWS EPS 100C2 (280-size) 7-cell geardrive.

My converted Comet 54" Aeronca Chief, powered by a Speed 400 in Mini-Olympus gearbox, 9-cell 500AR pack, is still flying great after 100 or more flights, mistakes, shoot-downs, and repairs.

Life is good. Gary Gullikson, Garden Grove, California

Frank Weaver \<

One problem with small R/C airplanes is the length of the antennas. A three-foot wire dangling behind just does not look right, and I'm sure has some effect on the flight performance.

The solution is a DEANS base-loaded antenna, which comes in two styles: a stick-on or a bolt-on. After all, the helicopter guys use these antennas with no problems.

I have been using them for years along with a rubber duckie Transmitter antenna, and have never had a glitch! I have flown next to adjacent channels and as far from myself as I could see the model. The great thing about the Deans antennas is you can build them into the model's wing or fuselage.

A quick and easy way to fuel-proof your firewall is to color the bare wood with a felt marker and then, wearing a rubber glove, rub in CA glue with your finger. Put on enough CA so you can rub it around without kicking off. Keep your finger moving and the CA won't stick to it. Three applications seems to work best.

One of our local hobby shops has a supply of Cox parts, props, and glow heads. If you are having problems finding a part, give him a try:

<div class="Joe">

...Frank, I've been using the Deans "Whip Antennas" for years. One thing about the "bolt-on" type: the instructions assume that you have ample access to both sides of the surface you want to mount the antenna to.

I've never been that lucky. Therefore I've used a different technique than Deans recommends. I mount the antenna base to a 1/32" plywood disk or oval, somewhere around 5/8" diameter. (I pre-finish this to match the model before attaching the antenna base to it.) Then I cut a hole in the airplane where I want the antenna to go; large enough so that the antenna base will pass through.

After that, I glue the ply disk (or oval), complete with the installed antenna base, over the access hole in the model. \[JW\]

<div class="Joe">

...Eddie, none of the folks in whose company I've flown have the evil habit of purposeless prop flipping. However, I have heard of a couple of instances where someone got a surprise by idly flipping a model diesel's prop. All I can say on this is: Don't leave your airplanes (diesel-powered or not) where any "uninformed person" can twiddle with it.

On your second question, I DON'T recommend Davis diesel conversions. Yes, they do work. Some of them work rather well, I've been told. BUT, the conversion units are expensive -- often as much as the engine they fit costs, and sometimes even more.

Because of that "cost factor" -- and the fact that glow engines aren't designed with as high stress levels in mind as diesels can provide (especially when operated by a beginner with model diesels) -- it seems to me that if you want to fly with diesel power, it's best to buy an engine that was designed that way from the start.

(BTW, the MP Jets .061 was designed as a diesel; the glow version is the same as the diesel except for having a different head.)

Now for the fuel question. (It seems that we just can't get away from that topic on SMALLnet, even though it's been thoroughly covered -- again and again -- in previous Postings.) Where you're located, Eddie, isn't far from Clover, South Carolina -- home of FHS Supply. They are the makers of Red Max model fuels, and I've bought lots of model diesel fuel from them.

The Red Max facilities are automated and computer-controlled, so that the fuel they make is accurately blended, free from impurities, and at a lower unit cost than any individual can achieve with home-made mixtures.

One problem in buying model fuel by mail order is shipping costs. A "Hazardous Material" surcharge makes mail-order model fuel expensive. That's why I buy my fuel "in person". In recent years that's been direct from Eric Clutton (Doctor Diesel), whose smiling face I see each year at the Little Rock annual SMALL meet. (Only WEEKS away now!)

As for diesel reliability in combat models: they've been used to power control-line combat models in Britain for decades. Those models encounter higher G-loads and more rapid changes of direction than the R/C combat models ever experience. \[JW\]

Oscar Aitken Corral \< 

 \> offers his "Chilean solution" to fresh diesel fuel:

Not having commercial diesel fuel available here in Chile, we found that the only way to be 100% sure of the fuel condition is to mix it at the field. This also solves the problem of slightly different mixes for each diesel engine -- and really takes just a few minutes.

We use a wooden box that can be tightly closed (the "lab") where we carry the ingredients and the "chemistry" mixing items to get an accurate mix. We took the idea from our U-Control speed modelers, who have been flying and competing with diesel power for years.

You must be careful though of that never-missing buddy who comes along asking, "What the hell are you doing?" with a cigarette in his hand just when you are pouring the ether....!! Oscar
