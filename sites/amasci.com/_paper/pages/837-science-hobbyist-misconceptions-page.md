# MORE ON WINGS

On Wed, 14 Mar 2001, Tim C wrote:\

    > One point I find disturbing is the author's repeated reference to the
    > fact that airfoils cause a downward MOTION of air which via Newtons 3rd
    > law explains the force of lift on an aircraft.  In fact the airfoil
    > causes a downward ACCELERATION of air.  It is the acceleration NOT
    > motion of air that allows for a Newtonian explanation.  After all, Force
    > equal mass times acceleration (not velocity).

You're very correct, and the lifting force is coupled with vertical ACCELERATION of the air, not vertical motion. However, I intentionally say "motion" because there is another problem here, and I wanted to force the issue.\

The aircraft is flying into initially unmoving air, agreed? The wing experiences lift, so the wing is accelerated upwards and the air accelerates downwards. However, if the air was initially unmoving, and THEN it accelerated downwards, then it HAS to be moving downwards afterwards, no?\

After lots of discussion and thought I see that this is correct, and initially-unmoving air can only provide a lifting force if the air remains moving downwards after the wing has gone past. The situation is similar to that with a hovering rocket: the exhaust mass was initially unmoving (as fuel), then it was accelerated downwards in the rocket engine, and as a result the exhaust remains moving down and the rocket can balance out gravity's downward force. Beware, there are other explanations of lift involving bouyancy, or involving sound waves and instantaneous contact forces with the ground, but they are wrong. Aerodynamic equations don't involve bouyancy or acoustic waves, yet they describe lift. The real answer has been in aerodynamics textbooks all along.\

    > This may seem like another picky detail since a downward acceleration
    > must surely result in a downward motion. (Note: when I refer to 'down',
    > I mean 'in a direction opposite to lift' - another fine point).  But the
    > distinction between acceleration and velocity is important because
    > velocity can be misinterpreted to mean that air is being moved downward.
    > In fact this is not true.  AIR IS NOT BEING MOVED DOWNWARD.

\

You are right... and wrong. That's the key to the whole issue. In a tank of fluid, the fluid can only flow in closed loops, therefore whenever fluid is moving downwards, an equal amount of fluid must move upwards someplace else. Therefore fluid can never move downwards on average. And if the airplane flies by reaction, mass MUST move downwards on average. A paradox? No, and the key to understanding this is in the difference between moving mass versus closed-loop flow.\

There is a situation that "violates" the requirement that net amounts of air never move downwards. I say "violates" because there is no violation and the rules are followed, yet parcels of air do move downwards and carry momentum as they go. How?\

Suppose I throw a waterballoon at you. You feel a force during impact, and we can agree that this sphere of water is a massive object which carries momentum. However, what if we move to an underwater environment? I can still throw the waterballoon at you, and surprisingly enough it will keep moving for awhile, and if it strikes you it will deliver its momentum. However, NO WATER MOVED on average, so how can you have received any momentum. For every parcel of water that moved in one direction, there was another parcel moving opposite. The balloon may appear to be the only mass moving forward, but it is surrounded by rearward moving water which causes the net transport of water to be zero.\

Then something weird happens. The net transport of *momentum* is not zero.\

As the water-filled balloon moves forward underwater, it meets water ahead of it. It accelerates the initially-still water backwards, but then that water decellerates again after it has passed. No momentum is lost, yet the balloon has moved forward. It's as if the balloon has exchanged position with the water ahead of it, but then recovered all of its original momentum in the process, so the water behind it remains unmoving. What's going on? The balloon becomes surrounded by a travelling ring-vortex! The ring-vortex moves forward, and it carries momentum like a forward-moving massive object, but the net forward transport of water is zero. THAT'S where the 'paradox' is solved. Ring vortices can move forwards while the net forwards motion of water is zero. Ring vortices can carry mass and momentum. Also vortex-pairs can move *downwards* behind an aircraft, but they are entirely composed of closed streamline loops, so zero volume is transported even though a net amount of mass moves downwards with a net velocity. Very weird, no?\

Now get rid of the balloon, and just throw a ring-vortex. If it strikes you, it will be just as if a massive object impacted your surface and delivered momentum to you. Put some colored dye in the ring-vortex and you can even see that it can even transport matter within its pattern. Yet the net motion of volumes of fluid is zero, even though a pattern of mass has NON-zero momentum.\

It turns out that textbooks on aerodynamics have entire sections devoted to vortex-oriented analyses of flight. Besides the usual "Bernoulli" explanation, there is the Newtonian explanation, the streamline-curvature explanation, and the vortex-based explanation. Books discuss the vorticity of the wing surface and the singularity which trails like a sheet from the wing's trailing edge, and they look at the line-like wingtip vortices that bound this "sheet." One thing that gets lost is the fact that the whole vortex-complex moves downwards after the aircraft has passed. Another thing rarely mentioned is that the entire vortex-pattern stores a finite amount of downward momentum, and when the vortex moves downwards, it satisfies Newtons laws' requirement that moving mass be ejected downwards by the wing. But it also satisfies the rule that the net downwards transport of fluid volume be zero!\

    > If with every passing plane, air were to move downward, we would be
    > living in a much denser pool of air.  For the downward moving air must
    > either move back up or collect near the surface of the earth. 

\

No, the moving air can become part of a closed-loop pattern of moving momentum as seen in the above animations. The loops are all closed, yet the pattern moves downwards. It has inertia, and functions just like the exhaust from a rocket engine.\

    > Likewise, if one assumes the downward motion of air creates lift, what
    > happens when it goes back up?  Is there an anti-lift effect created
    > somewhere.  

It doesn't have to go back up because the volumes of air follow closed-loop paths, yet the vortex-pattern and its entrained mass moves downwards. It almost seems like cheating!\

    > The fact is Newton's Law does not apply to motion but "net"
    > acceleration.  What really happens is hard to envision.

\

Not really. I came up with that "disk balloons" analogy very early on in this whole business. I knew that mass MUST move downwards, and that any objections MUST be ground to dust under the unstoppable laws of Newton. Over the years I've concluded that others miss this simple solution because they were taught the misconceptions that Dr. Weltner attacks, and I was not. Outsiders to aerodynamics might be ignorant, but if the field is infested with seductive misconceptions, then even a little kid will see what the experts cannot.\

    >  For every
    > molecule of air that moves down there is a molecule that moves up, but
    > the downward moving molecules change speed more abruptly than the upward
    > moving molecules.  This allows a net downward acceleration to occur
    > without a net downward motion of air. 

\

I suspect that this is wrong. If this was right, then I could build an antigravity machine by abruptly accelerating a metal mass in one direction, then smoothly accelerating it backwards at the other end of its trajectory. In fact, the accelerations integrated over time are equal, and the net force is zero.\

    > Yes, it is true, right behind a wing the air will be moving downward,
    > but an equal number of air molecules must go up to offset. 

Yes, and there is downwards momentum, but it is not cancelled by equal and opposite upwards momentum.

    > And the fact
    > is, the upward moving air is ocurring at the same time as the downward
    > moving air, so overall direction of motion is zero; and therefore,
    > downward motion is not a proper explaination. 

\

Have confidence in Newton: initially still air MUST be moving downwards if it was accelerated downwards. Fluid must move in closed loops? Well, in a collision between these two requirement, Newton's Laws win, and the closed-loop requirement amazingly is sidestepped.\

    > One way to imagine the downward acceleration without downward motion
    > phenomenon is to think of a wave in the ocean.  If the wave truly
    > represents moving water then every wave would have to climb up on to the
    > beach and hike inland to make way for the waves behind them (and
    > additional mass of water).  But water does not collect in great masses
    > on the beach.  If you were to put a cork in the water you would find
    > that cork remains relatively motionless when compared to the wave
    > passing underneath.  So, the water molecules are not moving with the
    > wave, but the change in acceleration of those molecules causes the wave
    > to move in a net direction.  Its also that net acceleration in a
    > particular direction that helps to explain beach erosion (but that is
    > another subject). 
    > 
    > Therefore it is the transfer of acceleration of molecules of air in a
    > downward direction that provides a Newtonian explanation for lift, not
    > the downward motion itself.  Another term for this transfer of
    > acceleration is 'a pressure wave'. 

\

Ah, but you need to cross-check with reality. Another term for this is "acoustic wave." Sound. In other words, the wing is being kept aloft by reaction from radiation pressure from downwards-beamed sound-waves. Unfortunately this is unphysical: there is no huge pressure-wave being radiated downwards by a flying 3D aircraft. However, in a two-dimensional world there IS such a wave, and this fact has screwed up aerodynamics students since the founding of the field. In a two-dimensional wind tunnel a pressure-mediated force-pair develops between the wing and the "ground", and it is always there no matter how far from the "ground" the wing may be. Look at wind tunnels and they'll teach you that wings are "pushing off" against the Earth. Look at real 3D airplanes and you'll find no such pressure wave during normal flight.\

Your 'pressure wave' does have bearing on the 3D world: it explains what happens during "ground-effect mode" flying, when the aircraft is flying within one wingspan of the earth. In that case the vortex pattern stops moving downwards, and the force-pair is between the aircraft and the Earth, not between the aircraft an a hunk of air alone. Ground-effect flight uses something like zero-frequency sound waves. Normal flight does not.\

    > Some of the e-mail responses have pondered: "what makes the molecules of
    > air travel faster over the top of the wing?".  This is another question
    > that can make sane minds go mad.  The fact is from the molecules
    > perspective, it is merely being pushed out of the way by the wing, which
    > does nothing to explain why pressure drops when this happens.

\

This also is hard to understand, but it is explained in books on aerodynamics under the section on "kutta condition." The air flows faster off the top of the wing because the air must flow STRAIGHT off the tilted trailing edge, yet the air does NOT need to flow straight up to the leading edge.\

The asymmetry is caused by the inertia of air (this also is the reason that "blowing" through a hole will create a long jet, yet "sucking" will create only a radial inflowing motion.)\

"Kutta Condition"\
Because air has inertia, air must flow straight off the tilted trailing edge. If the air flows straight off the tilted trailing edge, a pocket of vacuum would open up behind the wing. But air flows so as to maintain constant density. Therefore the air rushes over the top of the wing in order to fill the vacuum-pocket before the air density can even begin to drop. But doesn't all of this require that the wing be tilted to a positive angle of attack? No, it only requires that the TRAILING edge be tilted. In wings having positive camber, the trailing edge is tilted downwards in this way, even though the wing as a whole is not. This fact is not mentioned in low-level textbooks. Their authors perhaps are blinded by this effect:\

> Tilt the airfoil, then pretend it never happened\
> [/wing/airfoil2.gif](/wing/airfoil2.gif)

\

To sum up: wings fly because they are tilted, which involks the Kutta Condition which applies only to the trailing edge air flow and not to the flow at the leading edge, with the end result that air accelerates net downwards, air flows fast over the upper wing surface, the wing experiences a lifting force, and a vortex-pattern is launched downwards which carries mass downwards but while transporting zero net fluid.\

Not simple. But it forms an interlocking network of concepts, each of which is simple, and each of which is under direct control of Newton's laws. The big problem is that well-informed people have learned an incorrect alternate description. An uninformed person can learn the above stuff rapidly, while someone with a little expertise will fight against this description every step of the way. That's the trouble with seductive misconceptions: they get stuck in our "throats" and we cannot make any progress until we pry them out again.\

    >  The wing
    > cannot transfer energy to the molecules of air because the wing would
    > then have to constantly lose energy.  But that energy to dodge the wing
    > must come from somewhere and in-fact it does.  The energy comes from the
    > molecule itself (and its neighbor molecules) in the form of lost
    > pressure. 

\

Not true. The wing DOES constantly lose energy. It really does, and it's not explained by viscous friction. This energy loss is discussed as "induced drag", and it is very similar to the energy lost by a hovering rocket ship: the kinetic energy stored in the downwards-moving stream of exhaust gasses. Because a wing must accelerate the oncoming air, it needs an engine, or it needs to fly in a dive. If not, the process of level flying will slow the plane to a stop.\

    > Now as for the author's assertion that Newton should at least be used in
    > conjunction with Bernoulli to explain lift, I agree -- with the caveat
    > that students must NOT be taught that two different effects are being
    > combined.  These are two different ways of looking at the same
    > phenomenon (I believe the author has agreed with this point). 

\

Definitely. However, the "Bernoulli" explanation loses all its power when we confront the fact that the upper and lower air streams are not in a race to meet each other at the trailing edge. And when we learn that the real reason why the upper air moves so fast is that the back of the wing is tilted downwards, then the "Bernoulli" explanation seems like it was wrongly elevated to its contemporary stature on false grounds. Perhaps it should become a facet of the Newton-based explanation, rather than being an alternate explanation which competes for space in the textbooks.\
