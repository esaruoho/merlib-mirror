SMALLsters, two weeks after the Maumelle SMALLmeet: on June 16th, the Shreveport Area Radio Kontrol Society (SHARKS) is going to have their first annual SMALL fun-fly. It'll be at our field 6 miles north of Shreveport in northwest Louisiana.

Though we have no illusions of our first being as large as Maumelle, we hope to have visits from those flyers who weren't able to make it to Maumelle, or if they did, just want to fly some more.

There are several attractions in the area which will make the trip even more interesting. Barksdale AFB, across the Red River, is the home of the Eighth Air Force Museum, with a sizeable collection of vintage aircraft, as well as being a major B-52 and A-10 Warthog base.

Landing fee is \$10 and gets you a ticket to a raffle for a bunch of neat stuff, and pilot's choice awards. RV parking is plentiful, but no hook-ups are available. Control-liners are welcome; though since this is our start-up meet, we've made no provisions for indoor flying this year.

However, in order to appease the weather gods, our club's president burned many pieces of dead cow on an altar (OK, he called them burgers, but we know what he was really doing), so that should guarantee that we have clear skies and calm winds. Right.

For any more information such as motels, directions, etc., drop me a personal e-mail. Patrick LeRay

Sam Brauer \< <SBrauerBCC@aol.com> \> reports at length:

I thought I'd tell the gang about my experiences with the Astro 010, since for the electric junkies in the crowd, it's a power- plant that some may be considering. As a recap, this is Astro's brushless answer to the Speed 280BB. Motors in this class are used direct drive for airplanes in the 8-12 ounce category or thereabouts. Airplanes in the 15-17 ounce category should consider Speed 400s or geared motors.

I've flown both the Astro 010 and the Speed 280BB in the same airplane, so I now have a feel as to how the motors compare. For exact specifications, consult Astro's web site: \< <http://www.astroflight.com> \> or Multiplex's: \< <http://www.critterbits.com> \>.

I must admit that I have trouble coming up with useful information from these spec sheets. I keep coming away with the impression that a Speed 280BB should be able to match a Speed 400 in terms of power; yet in real-world applications, it ain't happenin'.

The Speed 280BB and the Astro 010 are physically close in size, weight, and sort of similar in terms of power. Although it's been suggested, I don't think the Astro 010 is really a replacement for a Speed 400 in terms of power, but with different batteries -- who knows?

The Astro motor has previously been limited by its reliance on a relatively low amperage (3 Amp?) on/off controller; but it has now been mated to a Jeti-06 3P which provides a nicely-working throttle and a larger amp range. I wasn't interested in the motor with the on/off controller, but this Jeti combination seemed attractive.

The test bed: a Wren biplane -- overall weight about 10 ounces (depending on batteries, motors etc.); wingspan 18", no struts, flat-bottom wing, aileron and elevator. Converted from a gas kit (the plans called for a Medallion or TD 049 and warned that it was very quick); heavy wood, which I somewhat lightened.

This design is not a good candidate for a geared motor. It needs to fly relatively quickly, and a 6-inch prop looks big on the airplane. All things are relative -- you could fly this airplane in a relatively confined space once you get used to it, in contrast to things like the House of Balsa 1/2A kits. But it's faster than my Herr Cherokee for example -- maybe 1.5x?

Anyhow, here's how I break the motors down:

Overall Construction: Astro wins hands down. My Speed 280BB did not stand up to the rough treatment dished out by the airplane cartwheeling, pancaking, etc. -- and had a shaft which bent relatively easily. (I haven't had to repair the airplane yet -- it's a tough little bugger.) I've heard of others having similar difficulties.

The Astro is built much more ruggedly, and may last for years. The downside of the Astro is that the Jeti controller is much larger than a Pixie 7 which works fine on the Speed 280BB.

Flexibility: Astro wins again. You can swing a larger prop with the Astro, or use a smaller prop and go for higher rpm. The Astro will take more cells as well. The Astro was comfortable on a 5.7 x 3 inch APC prop, and will swing a 5.5 x 4.5 inch prop on cells which can tolerate more current drain. The Speed 280BB complained at anything over a 5-inch prop.

Efficiency: the Astro can wring decent performance out of cells that didn't have enough amps to fly the airplane on a Speed 280BB. When I was comparing batteries, I noticed that a 7-cell 350 mAh nicad pack produced about the same rpm on the Speed 280BB as the 8-cell 720 mAh nickel metal hydride (NiMH) cells. Since the nicads were lighter, I flew with them instead of the NiMH's.

When I went to the Astro 010, I noticed an rpm increase when I changed over to the NiMH cells, and that's what I've flown in the airplane. These are not the newer Sanyo 720 (or whatever amp rating) cells which apparently can tolerate up to 10 amps current: these are the older Gold Peak cells which don't like being pushed much more than 4-5 amps. I've got some of the newer Sanyo cells on order from Hill's R/C (cheaper than Hobby People). I'll see whether they make a difference.

Power/torque: the Astro definitely has more usable power/ torque using lighter-weight batteries than the Speed 280BB. If you put an 8-cell 600 mAh AE pack of nicads on the Speed 280BB, it will scream on a 4.75 inch prop. The problem is that the batteries weigh so much, it just makes more sense to go to a Speed 400 instead -- which has more power and is cheaper. (Yes, it weighs an ounce more.)

Overall flight performance: with the 280BB, the Wren flew, but was a little marginal; whereas with the Astro 010, the airplane flew much better -- not killer-quick, but comfortably. It's not going to tear up the sky on the batteries I have, but it's an enjoyable schoolyard flyer.

I could never get the airplane to loop on the Speed 280BB -- but it did with the Astro. Also the duration is vastly improved, since I went from 350 mAh nicads to 720 mAh NiMH's.

The Astro 010 offers a power system that is significantly lighter than a Speed 400 (5 ounces versus 7 ounces) with roughly proportional usable power. The Astro 010 offers an increase in performance and flexibility over the Speed 280BB. However, if the airplane would not fly on a Speed 280BB, then it is unlikely that it will be more than marginal on the Astro 010.

If the airplane was somewhat marginal on a Speed 280BB, the Astro 010 can make a significant improvement.

Price: well -- you're going to pay the piper somewhere. In round numbers, a power system (motor, adapter, controller, and batteries) based on the Speed 280BB will be about \$75-80. The Astro is essentially double that. Clearly this is an expensive upgrade.

On the other hand, Speed 280BB motors don't last forever (I've already thrown one away) and you don't have to buy too many \$25-30 motors to make up the difference. Brushless motors, on the other hand, apparently do have long lifespans.

The one joker in this performance breakdown is what happens with better batteries? I've heard that the new Sanyo NiMH cells can tolerate the ampere requirements of the Speed 280 BB better than the last generation. This may narrow the performance gap considerably.

Gearing is another option being bandied about for the Astro 010. This to me is silly -- I've got a bunch of Speed 280BBs geared (MTM S-300s) and they work very well. I don't see how the Astro could offer a significant performance upgrade for the extra dollars.

Overall, I'm pleased with this little Astro in the sub-Speed 400 direct drive application. It offers a lot of improvement over the previous options.

Sam Brauer Norwalk, Connecticut

...Al, that Triplane is indeed an early design that pre-dates the wartime "Ott-O-Former" model kits with the cardboard ribs and bulkheads and pine spars & stringers. Not many modelers knew that Joe Ott designed quite a few models -- mostly scale rubber- powered craft -- for various pre-war model kit makers.

The largest of those was the Whitman Publishing Company in Racine, Wisconsin -- still very much in business. They had an extensive line of model airplane kits, sold mostly in "Five-and- Ten-Cent" stores, under the "Ace Whitman" brand name.

So far as I know, Joe Ott designed all of those. I built a lot of Ace Whitman kits during the 1930's, mainly because the line included many attractive scale model types that our local hobby shops (which carried Comet and Megow kits mostly) didn't have.

I still fondly remember my Seversky Amphibian Racer, Vultee V-11GB light bomber, Vultee "Vanguard", and Curtiss SBC-4 dive bomber. They looked good -- and FLEW ! \[JW\]

Robert Gaskin \< <rrgaskin01@sprynet.com> \> submitted:

Reference: Dal Hamlin's servo problem (servos going to full lock for no apparent reason) -- there is another possible cause. I had the same thing happen twice while flying a 1/2A plane on a two-channel Futaba system. Very spectacular crashes! I then examined the Receiver printed circuit board with an 18 power jeweler's loupe. Sure enough, there was a hairline crack in the trace that returns from the servo ground to the power supply. I soldered in a short piece of wire to bridge the gap caused by the crack, and the problem went away permanently.

Reference: Ultracote availability. The "Hobby People" printed catalog still offers Ultracote, but when I visited their web site recently, the product had been withdrawn from the on-line catalog.

I was able to buy Ultracote from the Horizon Hobby on-line catalog, but I'm not thrilled about the pricing. "Easycoat" is not offered at all. The standard opaque Ultracote was \$14.99 per roll. William Adams \< <William.P.Adams@mii.com> \> asked:

I was interested to read in the last posting about getting the Tee Dee 010 to swing a larger prop tractably, by restricting the exhaust. I am interested in doing something similar on a PeeWee 020. I want it to work with a 6x3 prop, as it looks more scale and should be more efficient with a radial engine nosed plane.

Questions arising: I am wondering where to start with the amount of exhaust restriction, and just what are the physics of this process to make the engine work better with larger props in the first place.

Also, does it make these small engines work too hard or hold the possibility of damaging them. Can a 6" prop be made to work on an 020?

...You don't need to make any major changes to a Pee Wee to use a 6-3 on it. I've been flying relatively large models with Pee Wees for over 40 years, starting with my 1/2-size Miss America (published in Model Airplane News in 1960; that model started the ".020 Replica" movement).

I used a 7-2 Tornado Plasticote prop on that, and the model was actually over-powered. Later projects included a single-channel R/C "powered glider" (180 square inches, 14 ounces) which flew best with a 7-2 Plasticote cut down to 6 1/2" diameter -- and my favorite rudder-only model design for the Pee Wee, my all-sheet-balsa "Starling" (165 square inches, 14 ounces) which extensive flight testing showed flew best with a 6-3 Top Flite plain nylon propeller.

The main reason Tee Dee .010's require "exhaust restriction" is that their front rotary induction design isn't amenable to intake restriction. But the reed valve Coxes already have their intakes restricted -- too much in some versions.

I make a few changes to my Pee Wees. (These are all metal-backplate models. I don't care for the plastic-backplate type.) First, I remove the inlet screen and discard it. Then I "carve away" all the "irregularities" on the external side of the air passageway, to smooth the airflow's path around the 90-degree turn it must make.

(I've also tried spacing Pee Wees away from the firewall with washers, for improved intake air access. That worked fine, except that in a short time the washers imbedded into the wood and caused looseness. And the more I tightened the screws to compensate, the deeper the washers sank...)

The point here is to maximize the "static pressure" of the air that the engine breathes. That makes a world of difference in running reliability and needle setting sensitivity.

I also rework the fueling vents. I loathe the original design, and detest the later one. Yes, I know the reasons for these -- but I still hate them.

On my metal Pee Wee backplates I carefully cut away the nipples; then (by hand, working very cautiously) drill through the existing vent holes, "tap size" for 3-48 threads. Then I tap 3-48 through. CAREFULLY ! (Metal Pee Wee tank backs aren't easy to obtain any more.)

Next I partially thread short pieces of 3/32" OD copper "refrigeration tubing". I do that by chucking the tubes firmly in a "pin vise", using that as a grip for threading 3 to 4 "perfect threads" on the tubing. That way, the "partial threads" above the perfect ones act like pipe threads when I screw these new vent tubes tightly into place in the backplate.

But before I do THAT, I sweat-solder about a 1/4" length of brass tubing onto the unthreaded end of each "refrigeration tubing" vent. That's for easier (and less sloppy) fueling. Then I bend each of the new vent tubes, so that they will face partly into the airstream when installed. The bend angle isn't sharp -- about 45 degrees is as far as I go.

...The trick to using "large props" (to me the sizes I use seem NORMAL) on glow engines is to prevent pre-ignition. If combustion takes place too soon, while the piston is still going up, that causes excessive strain and heat -- and loses power.

On Pee Wees you have no choice of glow heads. But you CAN stop pre-ignition (if that's a problem) by adding an extra head gasket or two.

I haven't needed to do that. I prevent pre-ignition with good fuel. Cox engines REQUIRE all-castor-oil fuel. The best I've found for Pee Wees is 18% nitro, 24 % castor oil. That's what the original Cox "Sport Fuel" blend was.

(Without adequate castor oil in the fuel, the ball joint at the top of the conrod overheats, galls, and FAILS. This is true for ALL Cox engines.)

The old "Sport Fuel" isn't available any more. (Castor oil evidently costs too much for the present Cox/Estes management's tastes.) However, I can make my own equivalent fuel rather easily.

I buy a quart of Byron's 25% nitro, all-castor-lubed glow fuel and add 4 to 5 fluid ounces of Sig's castor oil to that.

One more point. Propellers with the same diameter and pitch, but from different manufacturers, can produce much different results. The ONLY way to find the optimum propeller (diameter, pitch, and maker) is by flight testing. The SAME engine, used on a DIFFERENT model, can require a different prop for best flight performance.

In the world of aeronautics, just about everything is inter-dependent. \[JW\]
