<td data-valign="TOP" width="73%"><p>In 1962 Seattle was the location for a big world’s fair, officially called the Century 21 Exposition, but commonly known as the Seattle World’s Fair. Two landmarks that built for the fair were the Space Needle and the monorail, both of which are still famous Seattle landmarks.</p>
<p>The monorail was designed and built by a German company, Alweg, now out of business. It is a little over one mile long and connects Downtown Seattle with the fair site, now known as Seattle Center, and the monorail is now called the Seattle Center Monorail.</p>
<p>Several years ago, a Seattle cab driver, Dick Falkenbury, thought it would be a great idea if the monorail could be extended to serve a much larger area. He successfully organized a campaign that resulted in the formation of the Elevated Transportation Company (ETC) to study the feasibility of a monorail system and to place a design on the ballot for voter approval.</p>
<p>The plan developed by ETC was for five monorail lines in Seattle, three running north and south and two running east and west. Patterned after the custom in many subway systems, each line was named after a color. The one selected to be the initial route was the Green Line, which would connect Downtown Seattle with West Seattle in the southwest part of the city and the Ballard and Crown Hill districts in the northwest part of the city.</p>
<p>In the November 2002 election the citizens of Seattle narrowly approved the ballot measure to build the Green Line and to begin initial design for a second line.</p>
<p>As a result of the November election, an agency was formed, known as the Seattle Popular Monorail Authority (SPMA), to administer the design and construction of the Green Line, and to begin planning for a second line. The SPMA is now operating under the simpler name, The Seattle Monorail Project, and that is the name now used on <a href="http://www.elevated.org/">their web site</a>.</p>
<p>The Green Line will be about 14 miles long and will serve, among other places, the West Seattle business district (Alaska Junction), Seahawks Stadium, Safeco Field, King Street Station (Amtrak and Sound Transit “Sounder” commuter rail), Downtown Seattle, the Seattle Center, the Interbay district, the Ballard district, Ballard High School, and Crown Hill district. The Green Line design provides for the possibility of extending it in the future south to the Fauntleroy Ferry Dock and from Crown Hill it may be extended north and east to Northgate and/or Lake City. For my opinions about the Northgate Extension, <a href="greenext.html">click here</a>.</p>
<p>The project includes two water crossings — the Duwamish Waterway on the way to West Seattle and Salmon Bay between Interbay and Ballard. Current plans are to strengthen the West Seattle Freeway Bridge and run the monorail guideway along the median of that bridge to cross the Duwamish Waterway. The Salmon Bay crossing would be accomplished by construction of a new monorail-only bridge parallel to the existing Ballard Bridge. The monorail bridge must be high enough over the navigational channel to permit ships to safely pass under the bridge.</p>
<p>On June 3, 2005, agreement was reached between the Seattle Monorail Project and Cascadia Monorail Company for a contract to design, buyild, maintain, and operate the Green Line. Later in the month details of the contract proposal were revealed, including a financing plan that would have cost about $9 billion in interest over fifty years. The financing plan resulted in fierce opposition and the financing plan was killed. Now many people and organizations are calling for killing the monorail project, but others are trying to find ways to solve the problems and move ahead with the project.</p>
<p>For later news on the project, <a href="greenline.html">click here</a>. For the best coverage of news on the monorail, go to the <a href="http://www.elevated.org/newsroom/headlines.asp">Seattle Monorail Project’s News links site</a>.</p></td>
