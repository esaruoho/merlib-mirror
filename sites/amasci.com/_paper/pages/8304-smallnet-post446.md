The selection of good balsa becoming less and less, no Sigment in stock and no effort to restock; no rubber stock; no good supply of stick-and-tissue kits (oh sure, a couple of inexpensive Guillow kits), and on and on. Andy Woitowicz \< 

 \> comments on Norvel 1/2A engine break-in:

When new, Norvel engines can be nearly impossible to turn over by hand. Soaking the cylinder in oil and hand cranking a hundred turns can help by using the abrasiveness of the anodized (aluminum oxide) cylinder to "hone" down the piston to a better fit.

My concern is for the wear and tear on the rod and crank bearings while this is done, as fresh lube is not being provided to these areas as it would be during a normal break in. One solution is to load up the crankcase with oil, do the hundred turns, and then let it drain out overnight. You don't want to flush it out with any sort of solvent. (See below.)

Even after the 100 turns, and lube in the pores of the cylinder, you can still get into trouble with any amount of excess prime. This prime can easily wash out the lube in the pores and return your engine to a locked up state if you're not VERY careful to follow through with your flip.

The ultimate solution, I think, to the Norvel's unique break in needs is to simply heat up the cylinder with a heat gun as hot as you can get it. Even when new and without the hand cranks, a heated engine will flip over easily when hot, but lock up when cold. The expansion rate of the cylinder seems to be greater than the piston. Or, it could just be that the unique combination of the abrasive, anodized cylinder and nickel-plated piston simply requires that much more care when breaking in.

Even ABC type engines can benefit from the heat gun treatment as Dave Gierke has recommended this idea in one of his columns.

Another point in the care of the small Norvels. From the .074 on down, the wrist pin is staked in place instead of using the usual circlip retention. It's a good idea and solves the issue of pin retention on such small pistons. Certainly better than a ball/socket. However, occasionally the pin migrates past the crimp and starts to grind away at your lovely, anodized cylinder.

This produces some very fine, abrasive, aluminum oxide dust to take out all of your piston fit and compression. I routinely restake every new engine or piston-cylinder assembly.

Just use the shank of a 1/8th inch drill bit. Lay the piston on its side, apply the shank to the piston hole, roll the piston forward slightly and tap it with a SMALL hammer. Tap it lightly, don't whack it. Rock the piston back the other way and repeat. Do the same to the other piston hole.

You can use the same technique to replace a connecting rod. Tap out the pin just far enough to let the rod fall out. Install a new rod and carefully tap the pin back in position. Re-stake the side that the pin was forced through and you're done. This can usually be done only once; so if you keep track of which side you did, you can repeat the process with the other piston hole and effect two rod replacements on the same piston.

Amazingly, the plating on the piston holds up very well. Some of my pistons have had up to three rods replaced and held up well. A fourth try causes the plating to flake off on one side. I now limit the process to two tries per piston. Much cheaper to replace a rod than the entire piston/rod/cylinder assembly.

On the subject of a laser-cut Ugly Stik: Please, how can we get hold of that fellow in Apex?

On the subject of ARFs: I kind of agree with Randy, except that the "easy" way to flying models can, and often does, attract a "certain type of individual". Not that all ARF flyers fit into this category. It's just that the care, dedication, discipline and love of the hobby that in the past was a prime requisite, tended to weed out that "certain type of individual".

More than one club has been split by unfortunate behavior and a certain attitude, and that's a real shame.

Another shame is the truly neat and interesting models being offered as ARFs, but at an ARF price. We really would like to build them and perhaps add our own touches. But we're given no opportunity to do that, as these designs aren't available as kits or plans.

I'd suggest to the industry that they're missing out on a potentially significant number of sales if they would offer kits for their ARFs. Andy

<div class="Joe">

...Len, there's nothing "unstable" or inherently dangerous in model diesel fuel. It's just ether, kerosene, and castor oil. Yes, the ether will evaporate if given half a chance -- or even 1/18 of a chance ! And its fumes are easily ignited, if the air-to-ether proportion is right.

However, just because you can smell ether fumes doesn't mean a dangerous situation is present. In the late 1940's when I got my first diesel -- a Drone .29 -- I bought ether for its fuel at a local drugstore. That was a long, narrow store with the pharmaceutical supplies way at the back.

I would wait up front at the cash register for the druggist to fill a pint bottle with ether for me. And as soon as he opened up his "carboy" of ether there in the back, I could smell the fumes -- 50 feet or more away.

Your bulged can of diesel fuel has evidently gotten hot enough for considerable expansion to occur. That seldom happens in a full or nearly-full can: liquid doesn't expand as much as a gaseous vapor does. I keep my diesel fuel supply in my backyard shop. In the summertime its interior temperature can get as high as a hundred degrees F. But none of my diesel fuel cans have ever bulged...

The only thing that causes model diesel fuel to "deteriorate" is evaporation of the ether. If that becomes excessive and your engine won't start, it's only necessary to add new ether -- John Deere "starting fluid" is 80% ether and works as well as the pure stuff for replenishing old diesel fuel.

Eric Clutton says that the exact ether percentage isn't critical. Of course, the smaller the engine, the more ether it usually needs for easy starting -- especially in cool weather. \[JW\]

Larry Marshall \< 

 \> clarifies a point on achieving lightweight paint coverage:

In reply to the query, "Why pour off anything? Just add thinner to the stock paint -- that's what the model railroaders do !" -- the reason for pouring off everything but the pigment and then adding solvent is (was?) weight savings. Much of the weight of a coat of paint is due to the binders contained in the paint.

The Floquil lacquers that the guys are buying from train stores and using on FF scale models are designed for plastics and metals, and so there's a lot of binder in them. But they also have the finest pigment particles of any model paint I've seen.

When spraying tissue, however, you don't need binders, as the paint readily attaches to the surface. Thus, you just use some pigment (What's ideal is Floquil Old Silver), mix it with acetone as a solvent and spray with an airbrush and you can lay down the prettiest, thinnest coat of silver you ever want to see. Opaque and very light.

Rich Miller showed me a "trick" that he uses with good effect. Rather than spraying white tissue with silver, he uses blue tissue. This somewhat darker blue tissue really improves the opacity of thin silver paint layers.
