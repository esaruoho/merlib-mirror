Sam Brauer wrote about the poor performance of his HiLine Micro 4-powered Nieuport (a Bill Hannan-designed 22-inch scale model of a 1911 French monoplane).

Sam, you're at the upper end of the model size range for the Micro 4, and hauling larger cells than are normally used by Micro 4 flyers. I suspect you'll have better success if you get some 50 mAh cells, maybe adding a third one to this model. If you're flying the HiLine 3" propeller, the motor can handle 3 cells.

They don't make the best peanuts in terms of indoor duration, but these will easily do 40 seconds in a typical gym without any special effort.

True, the Goodyear racers seem to fly well. I believe it was Chris Starleaf who published a revised Old Tiger in MaxFax. He too changed the airfoil.

Sam used Crayola modeling clay for a forming mold. In the art stores around here \[Quebec, Canada\] you can buy Fimo clay. It's a harder modeling clay than Crayola, and designed for doing small sculptures. It would be ideal for your cheek cowl idea. I've used blue foam, painted with a slurry of filler to make molds like this.

Mike Swanson asked about measuring wing incidence on small models. I published a solution in Model Airplane News when I reviewed the Great Planes incidence meter. This was some time during the last half of '99 I think.

I just made some stands and pointers to mount the meter on, so that the wing wasn't supporting its weight. While JW's method works, it's surely a lot more work than letting my laser just tell me the angle.

Ron Fikes \< <srfikes@earthlink.net> \> shares replies he has e-mailed to modelers with problems:

To Tom Arcoleo:

When I first used sandwich wrap (Reynolds "Crystal Wrap" -- which comes in colors), I thought I would lose my mind! The dang stuff stuck to itself everywhere. Then I took a deep breath, and swore that it wouldn't get the best of me. I still recommend NOT using it if you are in a tense mood.

Now to the gluing. I found that the "UHUstic" colored glue stick works well. (Buy it a stationery store.) It is colored purple as you apply it, then it turns clear as it dries -- the color lets you see where you have applied it (or not).

I am now using the clear plastic material ("StretchTite" from Costco's) on all my light stuff -- it seems thinner. I color the model's framework for visibility.

I have found that making a frame to hold the covering works well. Glue up a frame from 1/8" x 1/4" balsa, a little larger than the surface to be covered. Glue the wrap to the frame, reasonably tight. This allows the covering to be handled easily.

Apply the glue to your plane and lower the frame/covering onto it. Cut away the frame and press the covering down, going around and gluing the whole perimeter. Trim the edges with a new X-acto \#11 blade, and press down. (You can work out the wrinkles for a long time after applying.)

I have only used this covering on single-surface wings, but if you do both sides, allow the first surface to dry a while. Then do the other side, overlapping like regular coverings. You will have to apply more glue to the overlap (if you need a small amount of glue on something, use a small piece of balsa to scrape some glue from the glue stick, and spread where needed).

Allow the glue to dry overnight, then shrink -- BE CAREFUL ! Don't get the heatgun near the covering: the covering will disappear! Hold the heat gun up above the surface and let the warm air FALL on it. Or put the plane in a warm car. The covering doesn't seem to warp the structure; it shrinks until snug, then stops shrinking.

To Bob Baxter, who had trouble balancing a low-wing model:

You are having trouble with the balancing 'cuz you MUST balance a low-wing plane upside down! Most of the mass of your electric plane is above the wing -- just turn it over and put your fingers under the wing, in the same place. Remember -- balancing a low- winger from underneath is like trying to balance a broom on your finger. It's much easier with the mass hanging below.

Alan Porter \< <aporter@snapappliances.com> \> reports:

I own both the Miller R/C 1/2A starter and the Lite Machines starter, and have a few comments about them. I bought the Miller first, and then purchased the Lite Machines unit out of curiosity, and because I like to support companies developing small equipment.

My needs with these starters are almost exclusively for .049 and .020 engines. The differences between the two starters are considerable. Both cost about the same, but the Lite Machine unit is much more powerful. I'm sure it would spin a .25 engine easily.

The Miller is much more modest in its torque, but it is VERY smooth; and its rubber cone easily presses up against an .020 propeller (without spinner). I routinely start my Fokker D-7's (.049 & .020) with the Miller, and none of them have spinners.

The Lite Machine unit suffers from vibration when it is not centered on a large spinner, so I find it unsuitable for my .020 stuff, and barely suitable for my .049 models. It also jumps a bit when you hit the switch because of its powerful torque.

The real problem with the Lite Machine unit is that their silicone insert is just too long, creating a long unbalanced moment on the spinning shaft. Interestingly, if I insert the Miller cone (which is much shorter) into the Lite Machine unit (with a little tape wrapped around it to make up the space within the aluminum cylinder), it works VERY smoothly. I have e-mailed a suggestion to the Lite Machines folks for them to review their cone design.

In sum, I find the Miller a superior 1/2A (and smaller) engine starter, and the Lite Machine to be better for larger engines. (It is a fabulous .15 engine starter.)

Both devices are well made, and should last for many years.

Alan Porter

Put me down as someone who would like to know why there are no commercially available incidence meters designed for SMALL airplanes. This makes no sense to me, as the demand is there, and the technology is no big hurdle.

I have one of those clunky Robart incidence meters, which is practically useless on anything smaller than sporty-forty models. I use it for setting up my huge scale sailplanes (4 to 5 meter wingspans), and find it very useful for not only setting the proper decalage (stabilizer incidence relative to wing, but for adjusting each wing root incidence relative to the other where they butt up against the fuselage. I also use it to check the washout along the wing span. It works well for large, robust structure.

But if you place it on, say, the horizontal tail of a small model, its very weight will cause the elevator to sag under the load, giving a false reading. So, c'mon, Robart, or Great Planes, or whoever, how hard is it to design a good incidence meter that weighs less than forty grams or so? Precision instruments such as this are commonplace in the industrial world. I'd buy one!

Patrick LeRay \< <PLeray@aol.com> \> demurs a little:

To Mike Swanson, regarding JW's explanation of how to determine incidence: Mike, we gotta remember that JW's an engineer by profession, and only an engineer would reach for a "scientific calculator" to handle a problem as simple as this one is in the small scale we work in.

First, the idea of using a straight edge in line with the stabilizer is the right start. And also to measure the LE and TE distances from that line. Then, like JW said, find the difference between your two measurements.

Fine so far. But now the easy way is this: Pick up a cheap drugstore protractor (if you don't already have one for measuring other angles on our aireoplaneos), then draw a straight line on a piece of paper, and mark off a section the width of the chord.

At one end, make a tiny dot above the line at the distance you got for the difference between the LE and TE. Now connect the dot with the other end of the chord line, put the protractor center on that point and read what the angle is. Easy breezy. Just measure accurately.

BTW, last week I found that Office Depot has some dandy steel rules in 6", 12", and 18" sizes. Made by Fiskars, with really fine markings on them, including the first inch in 1/32" gradations, and millimeters on the opposite edge. Downright reasonable prices, too. I bought two of the 6" since I have a habit of laying them down in odd places around the workshop.

Patrick LeRay

Sam Brauer \< <SBrauerBCC@aol.com> \> relates:

I thought I'd tell people about some of my experiences with my Herr Cherokee. It shows that playing with electrics can require lots of experimentation, and a bunch of stuff to play with.

Anyhow, I built this Cherokee with an MTM S-300 motor with a 5:1 gearbox. The kit is very well done, and goes together easily. It's tough to figure out how to shave weight, and I didn't bother. The wood selection was also nice and light. I agree with your comments that modern high-revving engines don't match relaxing flying well, so I opted to save the Norvel for another project -- probably one of the House of Balsa designs, which are much sturdier than the all-built-up Herr designs.

I knew that I'd need more power out of the S-300 than what I've been flying in my Megowcoupe, so I opted for an extra cell -- going to a 9-cell, 720 mAh nickel metal hydride (NiMH) pack from an 8-cell pack.

Mike Gross (MTM) suggested an 8 x 5 Graupner prop for this airplane. My highly unscientific living room test (throw the throttle open: if the plane jerks forward a couple of feet in 0.5 seconds, you've got enough power) suggested that this combination might be problematic.

Somewhat dubious, I hauled the plane out to the field.

Even though it's freezing, my obligation to investigate this combination for my fellow SMALLsters knows no bounds! First attempts handchucking were very short-lived -- too much down and noseweight. Next attempts were on a large frozen puddle -- no joy with an 8 x 3.8 inch APC slow flyer prop. Wouldn't even hop. Insert a series of expletives here.

Take the airplane on a somewhat warmer day to my patch of pavement which is shared by the folks who drive around the park. (Occasionally akin to a racetrack, which has the unfortunate consequence of making parents wary of letting their kids go play.)

Trying the suggested prop, after a several hundred foot takeoff run, the Cherokee staggers into the air. Since I'm not going to clear obstructions, I cut the power and plop it back down.

I try a Rev Up 8 x 6 -- still no joy. If I try to increase the cell count, I've got to go to a different speed controller, I'm already pushing the Pixie 7 with 9 cells. I decide to try a 9 x 4.7 inch APC slow flyer prop. Not much more yank, and it seems like the revs are down.

I have a 7-cell 600 mAh nicad pack handy, so I plug that in. Lo and behold, more power! Clearly, the current draw of the motor was too high with the larger prop for the NiMH cells. With an 8-inch prop, the NiMH cells did better than the nicads. In defense of MTM, he recommends nicads with this motor.

Weight of the airplane is now up to 22 ounces -- about 3 ounces over the glow version. Sandpapering the prop tips on the pavement, I try again. Climb is a marginally improved over the 8-inch prop, so I plonk it down again.

Next try is a 9 x 7 Rev Up -- handchucks only, though. Ahaa -- success!

The airplane flies like well, a Cherokee -- although it's stand- way-off-and-squint scale. It's slow, needs a dive to loop, requires tons of rudder, but does a reasonable slow fly. In a 10-12 knot breeze, it wasn't making a lot of headway at full bore.

I do question the utility of this gearbox on a more high- performance airplane. MTM has been pushing this motor on a warbird, and I'm scratching my head with that one.

For comparison, I've also been flying a Twister with the stock setup -- direct drive 6-Volt Speed 400, 7-cell 600 AE pack, 5.5 x 4.5 inch props or 4.75 x 4.75 inch props, and an all-up weight of about 15 ounces. (Note that the geared Speed 280BB in the S-300 weighs about the same as the direct drive Speed 400 motor.) Either prop works well, and the airplane has sparkling performance. It won't do vertical rolls out of sight, but it does nice round loops, eights, and flies inverted well too.

The airplane tracks well and flies much better than I do. Slowing down does take some work given the clean airframe, but the airplane doesn't fall out of the sky. Overall, I like this airplane and I think it's a good value. I'm toying with the idea of going to a direct drive Speed 280 and an 8-cell NiMH pack to see what happens a couple of ounces lighter.

Anyhow, I'm headed to the WRAMS show this weekend -- I suspect I won't be the only SMALLster there.

Sam Brauer Norwalk, Connecticut

Nic Hallett \< <swiftweasel@webtv.net> \> inquires:

I haven't built and flown a model airplane since well before the current popularity of electric and CO2 power. My question is this. How to equate power output between glow engines, electric motors, and CO2 motors.

In other words, how to decide if a particular model is suitable for a given CO2 motor. Or, if a kit or plan will result in a model to be powered by a particular size glow engine, then what size electric or CO2 power plant will be suitable.

Thanks in advance, Nic

...Nic, define "suitable"! Does your concept imply having a given model airplane (FF, C/L, or R/C) that you could equip with interchangeable power providers: a "glow package", a CO2 unit, and an electric-power system -- and the model would fly about the same way with any of those three energy sources ? That's MOST unlikely!

Also, it depends on how you want your model to fly. CO2 is out of the question for wild aerobatics -- in fact I've never seen a successful CO2-powered control-line model. (The lines don't stay tight.)

The best way I know to determine the suitability of a model for a certain form of power is to look around (on the flying field or in the magazines or Internet forums) and see what kind of design performs as you want yours to, using your choice of power.

Then you have a standard of comparison. It's a limited one, but USABLE. A more "generalized" standard might appear to cover more territory, but it would probably be too broad to do you much good.

Let's take an example. Suppose you like the way an e-powered Megowcoupe flies. (That model was designed in 1981 as a 2-channel R/C schoolyard scale flyer, to be powered with a Cox Golden Bee.)

You check the specifications and "hardware list", and find out the motor, gearbox, battery, and propeller being used. You also learn that the model has 350 square inches of wing area, and weighs 17 ounces.

Now you know that a similar-sized Interstate Cadet or Aeronca Chief or Stinson Station Wagon would fly about the same, using the same equipment.

There's nothing cut and dried here, though! Read through Sam's "Cherokee adventure" above. Sam's no beginner; yet even he has to do considerable cut and try work to get a given model to perform as he wants it to.

There are hot-performing glow and electric planes. In those a different set of criteria apply. Even though the sizes may be similar, the equipment varies greatly: propellers; weights; number of cells for the electric model and amount of nitro- methane in the glow model's fuel...

You specifically mentioned CO2. The new Gasparins put all the earlier types to shame for efficiency and long running time. They all turn from 3000 to 2000 rpm, spinning "rubber-power" type propellers. Each Gasparin comes with a set of instructions, performance data, and a list of suggested model sizes for all the various motors.

Those spin 5-inch props (for the smallest of the new Gasparins) on up to 7-inchers (for the largest).

For the G300BBRV motor (which I consider the most practical for 3-channel R/C flying) Stefan Gasparin recommends a model size between 26" and 32" span, weighing around 4 to 5 ounces. (The motor, full tank, and propeller together come to about 40 grams; slightly under an ounce and a half.)

Just about any of the "Earl Stahl" scale models could be adapted, scaled to a wing area of 120 to 140 square inches.

If you want a reasonable guarantee of success, start out with an established and well-proven design for the kind of power you want to use -- and go on from there. \[JW\]
