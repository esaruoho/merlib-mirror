# Chinese Finger Trap Part 4

*Source page: `~steelsil/glcc/chifin4.html`*

Adult Children of Alcoholics (ACOA): If you grew up in a family in which a parent was alcoholic, you are an ACOA. This experience typically has the effect of making you feel over-responsible, reflecting your experience that you felt as though you were the only responsible person in your family. Alcoholic families are tense, angry and uncommunicative. They do not resolve personal problems well, and often totter on the brink of financial ruin.

The explosive, unpredictable, irresponsible, and uncommunicative behavior found in alcoholic families deprives the children of the opportunity to learn many skills for relating emotionally, relaxing in a constructive way, and managing life in general. The result is that ACOAs tend to be either grasshoppers (like the alcoholic) or ants, i.e., the over-responsible workaholic who holds the family together. In alcoholic families, a child may be inappropriately cast in the role of being his parents' parent. This is also a cause of workaholic behavior in ACOAs, as being responsible for others is a parental function. The workaholic behavior also enables the ACOA to gain a sense of control and security in what he has found to be an unstable and unreliable world. It also enables him to avoid communication and intimacy. This is a relief for him, as he does not know how to cope with communication and intimacy.

In addition, being a workaholic enables him to avoid the shame, guilt, low self-esteem, anger and insecurity which ACOAs usually feel as a result of their experience. If you are an ACOA, getting in touch with these feelings, learning to communicate and relate, and learning to find a balance between being irresponsible and over-responsible are challenges that you may need to grapple with.

On the other hand, being an ACOA may lead to being an alcoholic oneself. If this is the case for you, or if you tend to seek out relationships with alcoholics, then the section on alcoholism and drug abuse is relevant for you. The headings "Dependency" and "Dysfunctional Families" in this section of this book contain relevant information for the ACOA, as people who form relationships with alcoholics and drug abusers are "co-dependent" and are from dysfunctional families.

"After the Tears", by Jane Middelton-Moz & Laurie Dwinell is a good book on the subject. Adult Life Stages: Our adult lives are naturally composed of stages which are determined by the natural course of human development. Starting in early adulthood, we must accept separation from our family. We must develop intimate peer relationships and launch a career. Then we must form a marriage or partnership of our own. This requires that our friends and relatives come to accept our new spouse or partner. We acquire children by birth, adoption or marriage to a parent, and must learn to raise our children.

Then we must adjust to the fact that our growing children will begin to grow apart from us and leave us. We must begin to accept the need to care for our aging parents and to adjust to the inevitability of their deaths. We must come to terms with our career choices. Women must come to terms with menopause.

As our children begin to leave the home, we must begin to adjust to a new intimacy with our spouses. We must come to terms with the fact that our children are now independent adults, and begin to accept their spouses, partners, and children. Finally, we must face our own aging, our loss of our jobs due to retirement, our own mortality and limitations, and perhaps deal with the death of our spouse or partner.

We may have difficulty accepting any of these natural events for a wide variety of reasons. If we defend ourselves against the feelings which any of these stages arouse, then we are stuck in the finger trap or in the jar. Our own nature requires that we face the feelings involved and do the tasks which our innate tendencies require of us. Anger: Anger occurs as a result of hurt or fear. Threat to or harm to our self-esteem, to our feelings of being loved and belonging, to our sense of being in control of our lives, or to anything or anyone else important to us can cause hurt or fear and thus anger. As a result of our attempts to defend ourselves against feelings of hurt, fear, humiliation, etc., we may lose track of what threat or hurt originally caused our feelings of anger. Later events may "punch our buttons", and we blame all of our anger on that subsequent event. This would be an example of transference or displacement.

If you have chronic anger, sometimes called "a temper", then you are transferring your anger from some hurt or threat in the past, the awareness of which you have repressed (forgotten to avoid the pain or fear), and over-reacting to later events. If your "temper" gets you into trouble or is counter-productive for you, then you need to learn anger- management. The techniques for anger-management are outlined in chapter 7 of this book, "Coping with Your Newly Discovered Feelings". Following the program in this book should enable you to uncover and deal with the underlying cause of your "temper". A good book on the subject of anger management is "Learning to Live Without Violence", by D. Sonkin and M. Durphy. Compulsive Behavior: Alcoholism and drug addiction; compulsive gambling, eating disorders such as overeating, anorexia and bulimia; compulsive sexual promiscuity; compulsive spending, etc. are all examples of compulsive behavior. Compulsive behaviors are often treated in treatment programs that include 12-step programs. Twelve- step programs usually have names ending in anonymous, such as Alcoholics Anonymous.

Such treatment programs focus first on controlling the behavior, second on improving the client's current health and environment and third on improving communication and relaxation skills. Only towards the end of treatment do such programs begin to deal with the underlying issues rooted in the client's childhood and current family relationships. The reason for deferring an exploration of the underlying feelings is that under the stress of exploration of these overwhelming feelings, there is a danger that the client will return to the self-damaging compulsive behavior instead of progressing in treatment.

Relevant headings in this section of this book are, "Alcoholism and Drug Dependency", "Dependency", "Dysfunctional Families", "Eating Disorders", and "Adult Children of Alcoholics" Dependency: People who have difficulty in being alone, or who have difficulty in asserting themselves without being angry, frequently have a pattern of being in dependent relationships. The dependency can be with one's parents, with one's spouse or boyfriend or girlfriend, or less frequently, with employers, neighbors or friends. Dependent people often have difficulty ending relationships that are bad for them, such as relationships with alcoholics, drug addicts, abusive partners, etc.

They may either be unable to assert themselves in relationships or they may have rocky relationships in which they start out idealizing their partner and end up feeling that their partners are terrible people wholly devoid of redeeming virtues. People who fit this description usually come from families in which independence and belonging were not both supported.

Parents who do not support their children emotionally cause dependency in their children by throwing them onto their own emotional resources too early in life, with the result that they become anxious and insecure. Such families are referred to as "disengaged".

On the other hand, parents who seem to need for their children to be over-dependent also cause dependency in their children, by making their children fear that their parents will reject them if they try to establish a reasonable degree of independence.

Parents like this are usually driven to this behavior either by their own fears of being alone or by their own low self-esteem. If your parents needed for you to be dependent, you probably were cast into one of the following roles in your family: Security blanket; scapegoat; parent's parent; status symbol; audience; servant; possession; or rival for one or both of your parents. Families in which this is the case are referred to as "enmeshed".

To learn to become independent and self-confident, children need parents who support their efforts to reach out and become increasingly capable and self-reliant. Children need for parents to show approval, acceptance and pleasure in their children's growing abilities in becoming independent, capable and self-reliant. Families that do this are said to support "separation and individuation".

Separation and individuation is an issue that usually is only apparent to therapists. If you have difficulties with dependency, this is probably the first time that you have realized it. If you did not receive support for separation and individuation, a therapist can help to make up for your family's deficiency by supporting and encouraging your progress in becoming independent and self-confident.

It must be emphasized, the biggest problem for a dependent person is not getting in touch with the feelings of depression and fear of abandonment. It is not in understanding why they feel these feelings. It is not in remembering the events which made them fear independence and self-assertion. Rather, the biggest challenge for a dependent person lies in acting independent, capable, assertive and self-reliant despite feeling frightened and depressed.

See the heading "Dysfunctional Families" in this section of this book for more relevant information. Depression: As I have written in various places, depression is caused either by anger turned inward against oneself or is caused by hopelessness and despair about not being given love, approval and support at an early age. In either case, depression ties in with your whole life, and each part of this book is relevant to the fundamental issue of what is making you depressed. Especially, see the heading of "Dependency" in this part of this book. Disciplining Children: One may discipline children either too much or too little. The optimum approach to discipline is to allow and encourage children to express their needs, ideas, feelings, etc. in a civil fashion, but to require them to obey directives regarding their behavior. As a child grows older, external discipline becomes less and less necessary, and reasoning and negotiation become more appropriate. There should, however, never be any question that the parents are in charge in the family home.

With regard to discipline, the parents or other adults jointly responsible for caring for a child should privately negotiate their differences of opinion and then form a united front or coalition in the presence of the children. The exception to this rule is divorced parents, who should manage their households separately.

The question of how much punishment should be given is answered simply: Enough to achieve the desired behavior is the right amount. Less is inadequate, more is excessive. Children should not be disciplined by hurting them physically or by insulting them. Isolating them physically or withdrawing customary pleasures until compliance is achieved should be adequate. It is important to understand that not all problems with children's behavior are discipline problems, and so a disciplinary approach may be counterproductive on some occasions.

If this is not how you were raised, then you are probably angry about the way you were treated regarding discipline matters. You are at risk for passing these feelings onto your children, if you have not resolved your feelings about this issue. If you have difficulty in being moderate when you discipline your children, you are probably displacing or transferring your anger from someone else onto them. You may also not have had any exposure to more moderate ways of being in charge, as you imitate an excessively strict parent.

If you have difficulty being firm with your children, then you may have a dependency/assertiveness issue to look into. Other possibilities are: Your parents were too strict, and you don't want to be like them; your parents were too strict, and you want for your children to rebel for you; you want an excuse to be angry and disapproving with your children, because you are angry with someone, feel guilty, etc.; or you are angry with your spouse and like having the children annoy him. Dysfunctional Families: Families that encourage and support separation and individuation are healthy and functional. Dysfunctional families do not fit into this category. Families that have members who are alcoholic, drug-addicted, have eating disorders, are severely depressed, mentally ill, are sexual abusers, etc., are dysfunctional and produce members who have difficulty in being confident, happy and emotionally involved without being over-dependent.

Even when such families appear normal and happy on the surface, there is a great deal of anxiety, fear, depression, anger, low self-esteem, etc. behind the facade. Sometimes some members of such families are aware of this, but sometimes they are all fooling themselves into believing that everything except the "symptom" is just fine. In such families, usually everyone is dependent and afraid of abandonment, though some members would deny this.

In dysfunctional families of every type, it is common for the mothers to have an over-dependent, over-involved, or over-possessive relationship with the children, and for the fathers to be remote, uninvolved and disengaged from the family.

Sometimes such families produce "scapegoats". The scapegoat is a family member who is considered to be a bad person and the source of all trouble in the family. He is considered to be a rebel, a "black sheep", an ungrateful child, etc. He can do no right, whereas other family members can do no wrong. Sometimes, families will reject the scapegoat altogether. This is analogous to the biblical phenomenon of scapegoating, in which all of the sins of the town were ceremonially loaded onto a goat. The goat, called a scapegoat, was driven forth from the town, thus cleansing and purifying the inhabitants of sin.

In families in which this practice occurs, family members are using the scapegoat to displace anger in a way that is safe for them. They are also ridding themselves of other unpleasant emotions such as low self-esteem and guilt, by "putting them into" (projective identification) the scapegoat. Conflicts which are lying dormant between other family members have their feelings discharged in conflicts with the scapegoat. Naturally, being the scapegoat is a deeply painful, angering and disturbing experience.

The scapegoat often acquires a self-image of being a rebel in a hypocritical and unjust world, and manages to get himself rejected again and again in life. Though he usually won't admit it, the scapegoat is very anxious, has low self- esteem, fears abandonment and seeks out dependent relationships. He frequently engages in some variety of self-destructive behavior in an attempt to please his family, who he unconsciously understands to wish him harm.

If you are a scapegoat, then you need to understand that your family may or may not be willing to change. You, on the other hand, can throw off that role and learn to be accepted and approved by others who are not in your family. Then your anger, hurt, despair, disillusionment, and self-defeating behavior can be replaced by happiness and a more positive and constructive life.

Another role for children that is sometimes found in dysfunctional families is the "family hero", also called the "parentified child". If you are the family hero, or your parents' parent, then you were expected to be very good, and excessively responsible. My comments about workaholics under Adult Children of Alcoholics in this section apply to you. Eating Disorders: Anorexia, bulimia, and compulsive overeating are referred to as eating disorders. Anorexia might be described as self-starvation. Usually, the anorexic denies that he or she is excessively underweight. In bulimia, the victim alternates between compulsive eating ("binging") and "purging". In purging, the bulimic diets, induces vomiting, takes enemas, and/or exercises excessively, all in order to lose the weight put on in binging.

Anorexics usually come from enmeshed, perfectionistic families in which they are over-controlled, in which too much is demanded of them, and too little emotional support is given to them. The anorexia may simultaneously represent a number of motives. They typically are: A need to exert control over one's own life and body; A need to punish and frighten one's family; An opposite need to please one's family by becoming "perfectly" thin; A turning of anger against the family against oneself; A cry for help; An identification with the family, which is emotionally "starving" the anorexic; A wish to avoid sexual maturity; A wish to die.

The compulsive over-eater is "empty" inside, and eats to fill this emptiness. This emptiness is a way of experiencing feelings of being unloved, un-lovable, lonely, depressed, feelings of low-self esteem, anxiety, feelings of being abandoned, etc. The over-eating is a defense against these feelings. Over-eating may also represent a wish to avoid being sexually attractive or a desire to please a family that has a wish for the over-eater to be overweight.

Bulimia represents a successive alternation between the two states of mind that have been described, that of the over-eater and that of the anorexic. It must be emphasized that eating disorders are extremely dangerous to the suffer's physical health and may be fatal if untreated. If you suffer from anorexia, bulimia or compulsive over-eating, I urge you to seek treatment from a treatment program (see the heading "Compulsive Behaviors" in this section of this book) or from a therapist who understands your disorder.

For a description of the typical family history of someone with anorexia, bulimia or over-eating, look under the headings of "Dependency" and "Dysfunctional Families" in this section of this book. Grief and Loss: People feel grief as a result of the loss of anything or anyone that they are deeply attached to. Thus, it is not only bereavement as a result of a death in the family that gives rise to this feeling, and initiates the mourning process, but also the end of a marriage, the loss of a deep friendship, the surrender of a deeply cherished hope or wish, the loss of some aspect of physical functioning, an important event which causes major damage to one's self- esteem, etc. Losses tend to cumulative, as any loss tends to reactivate the feelings caused by previous losses.

Natural reactions to losses include feelings of anger, guilt, self-reproach, sadness, temporary depression, anxiety, loneliness, fatigue, feeling helpless, shock, yearning, numbness, and sometimes relief and pleasure at being free.

One may temporarily experience: Disbelief; confusion; preoccupation with the loss; sensing the presence of the person lost; temporary hallucinations; sleep disturbances; appetite disturbances; absent-mindedness; social withdrawal; dreams of the person lost; avoidance of reminders of the person lost; searching or calling out for the person lost; restless over activity; sighing and crying; visiting places or carrying objects connected to the person lost; and treasuring possessions of the lost person.

The tasks of mourning are: To accept the reality of the loss; to experience the pain of grief; to adjust to an environment in which the person or thing lost is not present; and to withdraw emotional energy in the person or thing lost and to reinvest it elsewhere.

Problems may arise in the process of mourning when: You are ambivalent about the person whom you have lost; when you have previous un-mourned losses; you do not feel permission to grieve a loss; you refuse to accept the reality of the loss; you have no one who is sympathetic to your feelings of loss; or you are excessively dependent on the person or thing lost.

Successful mourning requires that you complete the tasks of mourning outlined above. You must first accept the reality of the loss. Then, you must allow yourself to experience the pain of grief. After that, you must begin to adjust to an environment in which the lost person or thing is missing. Finally, you must begin to let go of who or what you have lost and begin to find other people and things to care about and to enjoy. Guilt: Guilt is a more complicated subject than it might first appear. Guilt is the voice of your conscience. Your conscience is primarily the internalized attitudes of your parents, and to a lesser extent, other authority figures and your peer group. In addition, frustrations give rise to a desire for revenge, which in turn inspires a fear of loss or retaliation. This also can cause guilt. Guilt can also be disguised anger, in which anger with someone else is turned against the self, as in one type of depression.

Naturally, everyone should have a conscience, but some people have internalized attitudes that are not healthy for them. Parentified children have internalized attitudes that they should not satisfy their own needs, but should rather place the needs of others above their own. As a result, they feel an inappropriate and counterproductive guilt any time that they attempt to grow or assert themselves. Children who have been abused or exploited internalize the attitude that this is the appropriate role in life for them, and feel guilt for not wanting to be abused or exploited.

Some people have consciences which are too harsh. Their consciences punish them instead of motivating them to change their behavior. This is counterproductive. A useful, functional conscience is successful at motivating people to meet desirable goals, such as meeting their obligations to others, and making amends when they have failed to do so.

A harsh conscience which does not motivate a change of behavior reflects early experiences in which a parent or other caretaker was harsh and attacking, but did not take steps to insure the desired change in behavior. This type of parental behavior is typically the result of scapegoating, discussed under the heading "Dysfunctional Families" in this section of this book. (See also Projective identification and Reversal of self and object in the appendix on defenses.)

People may internalize guilt about natural functions such as sexual desire or the desire to separate and individuate. Guilt about natural, inherent, growth-oriented urges is counterproductive, and exposing it as such and tracing its origins is good for your happiness, growth and development. You can explore what makes you feel guilty, how you learned to feel guilty, and examine your fundamental values to see if what makes you feel guilty is really compatible with your fundamental values and beliefs. Guilt concerning natural, innate urges can be let go of through this exploratory process, to the ultimate benefit of yourself and your loved ones. Jealousy: Jealousy is intimately connected to past experiences in which you felt displaced by a rival. It springs from feelings of low self-esteem, especially feelings of low self-esteem about your physical appearance, charm, suavity, or anything else that might make you especially attractive in a sexual or personal way. Uncovering memories of past experiences in which you felt displaced by a rival, such as in the "Oedipal Complex", can be very helpful in enabling you to overcome feelings of jealousy. Discovering the memories and challenging the beliefs which give rise to your low self-esteem can be very useful in helping you to deal with feelings of jealousy, as well. Marital Problems: The core of marital problems is usually transference. You think that you are reacting to your spouse, but you are actually reacting to your father or mother. Other defenses can interfere with a marriage, as well. Here are some examples: If you have low self-esteem, you may project it onto your partner, and incorrectly perceive him as putting you down. If you are over-dependent, you may alternately feel that your partner is over-controlling or invasive, and then you may feel that he is about to abandon you. This would be an example of transference.

You may have found someone like one of your parents, you may induce your spouse to behave like one of your parents, or you may mis-perceive your spouse as being more like one of your parents than they are. If the marriage was a mistake because your spouse is unsuitable, you probably picked someone who is like one of your parents, which would be an example of repetition compulsion. If you induce your partner to behave as one of your parents did, this would be an example of projective identification. If you mis-perceive your spouse as being more like one of your parents than he is, then that is an example of projection. Your spouse may do each of these things with you, as well.

The list of possibilities of ways in which your and your spouse's defenses can hurt your marriage is endless. Following the program outlined in this book is the only real solution. "Intimate Partners", by Maggie Scarf, is a good book on the subject. Mental Illness: The term mental illness is usually reserved for schizophrenics, paranoid people, and is sometimes used to refer to manic-depressives and the extremely depressed. Put simply, a schizophrenic is someone who sees, hears, or otherwise perceives and responds to things or people which are not there, or has obviously false beliefs (hallucinations and delusions). They become withdrawn and incapable of caring for themselves. Their behavior, thought, and dress may become odd or bizarre.

As you probably know, a paranoid person has the unshakable but untrue belief that there is a conspiracy against him. A manic-depressive person alternates between depressed and manic states. A manic state is one in which the manic person acts unusually energetic, active, confident, cheerful and powerful. In extreme cases, manic states include bizarre or erratic behavior and delusions or hallucinations.

There are a variety of beliefs among medical professionals, counselors and psychotherapists concerning the cause or causes of the various types of mental illness. I personally believe that the parents of the mentally ill do certain things that cause their children to become and perhaps to remain mentally ill. The following reflects my personal views regarding a controversial subject.

In schizophrenia, the schizophrenic has usually had his parents intrude into his thoughts and perceptions in such a way as make him feel that his choices are to entirely disbelieve in his family or in himself. He is told that his perceptions and thoughts are false when they are not, and that his family's inconsistent behavior is consistent. As a result, his ability to tell the difference between reality and fantasy is undeveloped. In addition, the schizophrenic has been discouraged for his attempts to grow in independent and self-reliant functioning. These factors give the schizophrenic person the characteristics of a dependent person as well as the obvious schizophrenic symptoms.

Paranoia is a variety of schizophrenia, and has similar causes. The paranoid person projects his anger or guilt outward, and experiences these feelings in the form of specific delusions about being persecuted. In my view, people who are angry have usually been given reason to be angry, so their view that they have enemies is not entirely without foundation.

I feel that there is usually a core of disguised truth about inner and/or outer reality to be found in delusions and hallucinations, as there is in dreams. Thus, a delusion or hallucination may be a wish fulfillment fantasy, a projection, a disguised perception of a dangerous truth about another person, etc.

I believe that manic-depressive and severely depressed persons are depressed about a lack of support, love or encouragement at an early age. In effect, it is a dependency problem, and my remarks under the heading "Dependency" in this section apply. I believe that manic states are a defense against underlying severe depression. They constitute a fantasy of rising above and conquering the depression, anxiety, helplessness, vulnerability, etc. associated with the depression. The defense fails sooner or later, and the underlying depression is revealed. The cycle continues when the manic fantasy is reinstated.

The mentally ill person needs to be given permission and encouragement to see, accept and express his feelings and perceptions without the necessity of disguise. If he is withdrawn, he needs encouragement to relate to others. If he has difficulty differentiating between fantasy and reality, he needs to be given help in doing this. Eventually, he will need to be given permission and support for acting in increasingly self-reliant and independent ways, despite the anxiety and depression that this will cause.

In doing these things, the mentally ill person will usually be working in opposition to his parents and perhaps to other family members, despite protestations to the contrary. People exist in relationships, or in the memory of relationships. When someone is frightened, dependent or confused, there is often someone with a need for him to feel that way. It is well to remember, however, that transference and projection can make it difficult to be certain who that person or persons is or are.

"Sanity, Madness and the Family", by R.D. Laing & A. Esterson is a good book on the subject of schizophrenia. Self-Esteem: The psychoanalytic term for self-esteem is narcissism. In general usage, narcissism is used only to refer to excessive self-esteem, which is in fact a cover for inadequate self-esteem. Self-esteem problems usually have their roots in the way that people were treated at an early age. If your parents didn't seem to want you, respect you, or if they actively humiliated you, then you probably have problems with self-esteem.

Later in your childhood, events may have made you feel that you were not as admired, respected, or as liked as your same-gendered parent or a sibling. This is connected to "Oedipal" or "sibling rivalry" issues. Thus, a girl might feel inadequate or inferior to her mother, and jealous of her mother's possession of her father. An older sibling might feel similar feelings towards a younger sibling who displaced him as the cherished "baby" of the family, etc.

Low self-esteem can be a family "hot potato", as people in a family try to get rid of feelings of low self-esteem by making other family members feel low-self esteem. This can be done by acting dismissive, contemptuous, indifferent, superior, judgemental, competitive, etc. This would be an example of the defense of projective identification.

People with low self-esteem often project their low self-esteem onto others, and wrongly believe that others have a low opinion of them. In addition, the may defend against it in other ways, including repression, denial, reaction- formation (the appearance of excessive self-esteem mentioned above), etc.

Some people have a "narcissistic" personality. Narcissistic persons make poor parents, as they are inclined to be cold, competitive, possessive, un-empathic, un- supportive, arrogant, intolerant of differences of opinion, perfectionistic, and incapable of deep concern for others or emotional commitment to others. They tend to treat others as audiences, resources for favors, possessions, rivals, or as status symbols, rather than as people to be cared about and related to.

If you have a narcissistic mother, you probably have low self-esteem, depression problems, dependent relationships, rebellious feelings, and are prone to feel that others are cold, unjust, controlling or contemptuous of you. In addition, you probably have a hunger for approval which is quite at odds with your rebellious feelings. If you are a woman with a narcissistic father, you probably have difficulty communicating with men, feel inadequate as a woman, and are attracted to narcissistic men.

An excellent movie about a narcissistic mother and dependent daughter is "Autumn Sonata" by Ingmar Bergman, available on VHS videocasette. An excellent book on the subject is "The Drama of the Gifted Child", by Alice Miller. Sex: The most common cause of sexual difficulties, in couples who have had satisfactory sexual experiences with each other previously, is hidden and avoided anger between the partners. This anger may have transference roots and/or may be well grounded in actual conflicts in the marriage which have not been addressed and resolved successfully. There are a number of other possible reasons why someone might not wish to experience sexual intimacy with a particular partner.

Persons who have been sexually abused and who are just beginning to get in touch with the feelings which this has caused may also develop a dislike of or desire to avoid sex, as a part of their recovery process. They are experiencing, in transference with their sexual partner, the feelings which they had repressed which they felt when they were sexually abused.

People who have never had satisfactory sexual experiences commonly are suffering from the effects of negative attitudes about sex which they have internalized. They may feel guilt, shame, low self-esteem, disgust, etc., about their sexual desires as a result of internalized parental attitudes.

Men may experience performance anxiety, as a result of self-esteem difficulties or negative sexual attitudes. (See the sections on "Self-Esteem" and "Guilt" in this part of this book.) It is also possible to have physically caused sexual difficulties, sometimes as a side effect of drug or alcohol abuse.

If you have a sexual difficulty, then ask yourself when it started. If you have always had it, chances are that you are responding to negative parental attitudes about sex, or to sexual abuse. Otherwise, it is probably connected to relationship problems and is not specifically sexual in nature, unless you are just beginning to get in touch with memories of having been sexually abused. To explore the possibility of a physical cause, you should consult your physician.

Once you have dealt with these possibilities, techniques which are specifically sexual in orientation can be brought to bear. "Human Sexual Inadequacy", by Masters and Johnson, and "Becoming Orgasmic", by Heiman and LoPiccolo are good books on the subject of sexual therapy. Suicide: It is not unusual to think about committing suicide. At least half of the population has thought about committing suicide at some point in their lives. In spite of this, the average suicide rate in the United States is about 13 per 100,000 annually. If you have suicidal thoughts or feelings, you should talk to someone about it. Most cities have crisis lines modeled after the Seattle Crisis Clinic. The volunteers at your local crisis line care about your problems and want to help you.

You should remember that suicide is a permanent solution to a temporary problem. If you feel overwhelmed by problems, it can help to separate each problem and consider each in turn. If you are angry, there must surely be better ways to express your anger. If you are hopeless, take heart. Many people have been as hopeless and miserable as you feel right now, and have gone on to recover from these feelings and lead happy and fulfilled lives.

A Glossary of Defenses</span>
-------------------------------------

Defenses, as I have often stated in the text, are ways in which people conceal the reality of their own feelings, perceptions, and thoughts from themselves. People defend themselves against painful or frightening feelings, perceptions, and thoughts in much the same way that ostriches are said to put their heads in the sand to hide from wolves.

Defenses can be a useful way to pace the rate in which we absorb overwhelming input. Unfortunately, defended against feelings and perceptions have a way of tormenting us in ways of which we are unaware, and they can produce very self-defeating behavior. As a result, it is important to re- discover what we have hidden from ourselves. To that end, I think that a detailed knowledge of the ways in which we conceal things from ourselves can be very useful.

I have made this book as free from technical jargon as possible, as my intent in to convey practical information useful for everyday use in people's lives. Nevertheless, it seems simpler to use technical words when discussing defenses that it does to invent my own terms or to use clumsy circumlocutions. For your convenience, I have made this glossary of the technical terms for defenses which are used and explained in this book. This appendix is redundant, following the principle that redundancy is the key to clear communication. Acting out--behavior which dissipates feelings, rather than fully experiencing those feelings which motivate it; one may avoid remembering painful experiences by acting out. An example would be: An event reminds you of something that hurt you. You repress awareness of the memory, and pour all of your anger and hurt into the current incident. Then you do something counterproductive, such as end a valuable relationship. Avoidance--to be aware of a wish or feeling, but to avoid it. Changing the subject is a typical example of avoidance. In therapy, incessant chattering, or bringing up unimportant topics, or red-herrings, may be signs of avoidance. Conversion, body armor--converting a conflict, resistance, or repressed feeling into a physical symptom or tension. An example: A man I know has a daughter who did an entire series of outrageous things. He cut off all contact with her, and developed asthma immediately afterwards. His asthma is a constriction of his bronchial tubes, which lie next to his heart. He has converted his grief and anger with his daughter into a physical symptom. He has "choked his heart". He says that he is unaware of having any feelings about his daughter whatsoever. This is because he has converted them into a physical sensation. Denial-- denying that one feels, or wishes something which one actually does feel. Displacement-- experiencing a feeling or wish which one has towards someone as being towards someone else instead. It is an isolated feeling, wish or perception rather than a wholesale set of feelings, wishes and perceptions which is displaced. Wholesale displacement is called transference. Devaluation--having contempt for someone, feeling that someone is irrelevant, etc., in order to defend against some painful, frightening or humiliating perception, thought or feeling. The parable of the fox who couldn't get the grapes that he wanted, and so decided that they must be sour, is a good example. Identification with the aggressor--You identify with, that is feel that you are like, identical to, or a part of someone who does something very frightening or painful to you. The famous instance in which Patty Hurst joined the Simbionese Liberation Army in their bank robberies after being kidnapped by them is a well known example. Idealization--a reaction formation against anger. Isolation--to intellectualize; act objective and detached when discussing what should be emotionally meaningful. Omnipotence, grandiosity, manic flight--having feelings of elation, invulnerability, power, et cetera, as a reaction formation against feelings of depression, helplessness, and low self-esteem . Projection--denying that one feels or wishes something, but feeling that someone else feels and wishes it instead. Projective identification--experiencing an impulse, image, or emotion that is in oneself as being in someone else, either rather than or in addition to being in oneself, combined with the attempt to control and manipulate that other person into feelings and behavior consonant with the projected image, emotion, or impulse.

Projective identification is a complex concept. The four types of projective identification that I have focused on are: 1) Putting a specific feeling into someone else, such as a momentary anger, shame, etc.; 2) putting a whole side of your nature into someone else, such as ongoing feelings of dependency or helplessness; 3) reversal of self and object, in which you put an entire period of your life into someone else, in order to rid yourself of it; 4) projection of the object, in which you induce someone to feel and behave as an important person in your past felt and behaved. Reaction formation--feeling the opposite of what one really feels underneath. As Shakespeare put it, "Methinks the lady doth protest too much". Regression--to return to developmentally earlier modes of functioning, such as acting helpless and dependent. This is quite striking when it occurs, as it is always by definition out of character. Repetition compulsion--repeatedly acting out the same unfulfilling pattern of behavior. Seen most often in cases in which someone repeatedly makes relationships that don't work out with the same type of person. Repression--motivated forgetting. Reversal--turning anger originally directed towards someone else against oneself (sometimes depression or guilt is caused by the reversal of anger). Reversal of self and object--Behaving towards someone else as someone once behaved towards you. The effect is to make that second person feel as you felt, and so to rid yourself of those feelings by the process of projective identification. Splitting--experiencing and behaving as though people were all good or all bad; sometimes having such feelings towards one person, in alternation, without integrating these attitudes. Transference--experiencing and behaving towards someone as though they were someone else. Feelings and wishes felt towards one's parents are often directed in total towards one's therapist or spouse, for example. Undoing--behave in ways opposite to previous behavior, in the hope of magically undoing it.

Appendix IV. Psychological Theory

There are a number of psychological theories. The two that I have found most useful are psychoanalytic theory and family systems theory. They are described as theories, but to me, they are more than mere theories. To me, they are observations which contain the essence of important truths.

Psychoanalytic theory has three main branches. They are classical Freudian theory, ego psychology, and object- relations theory. Family systems theory is a loose body of work by a number of psychologists, psychiatrists, and other social scientists. Almost the only thing that the different schools of family systems theory have in common is that they all look at the ways in which one's family members are currently affecting one another.

Psychoanalytic thinking has, in the past, tended to focus on the individual. Family systems theorists focus on the family as a whole, and tend to regard individuals as merely a part of a larger family system. In actuality, people are both, and so I feel that a whole view of human difficulties requires paying attention to both. In recent years, there have been theorists who have worked to combine the two approaches. Most of them have been psychoanalytic family therapists.

The following summary will fail to do justice to a very complex subject, and will necessarily contain drastic oversimplifications. However, I hope that I will do no violence to the essence of the ideas presented.

Psychoanalysis

Psychoanalysis is the brain child of Sigmund Freud. Freud invented three models for describing the human mind. These are the core of classical psychoanalysis. They are the topographical, the economic, and the structural. In the topographical model he described his discovery that people are aware of only a part of themselves. What they are entirely unaware of, he called the unconscious. What they at times become aware of, he called the preconscious. What they are quite aware of, he called the conscious mind.

In the economic model, Freud described his discovery that people seem to maintain a constant level of psychological/emotional energy, until the tension is discharged. If some feeling, wish or need seems to disappear without discharge, it actually does not. Rather, it is converted into something else, or becomes hidden. This discovery is useful because it enables one to discover wishes which have been disguised or hidden, and convert them into something accessible and thus find an acceptable means of discharge for them.

In the structural model, Freud described the way in which people appear to be divided into three parts. The first part is the basic, innate, instinctive part of the person, which supplies the raw energy which motivates people. The second is the conscience, which opposes some of the individual's instinctive wishes. The third is the rational, problem solving part of the individual, which strives to find solutions for one's problems.

The Psychosexual Theory

In Freud's view, the main human drive was the drive towards bodily pleasure. Other motivations are considered to be modifications of this drive. He described this drive as being psychosexual, but he meant rather more by the term sexual than is usual. He considered bodily pleasures, such as the pleasure of sucking, as performed by an infant and pleasure taken in the act of defecating to be psychosexual pleasures.

He felt that there was a natural progression of concentration on one bodily zone to the next, as a part of the maturational process. Thus, we have the oral, anal and phallic stages of human development, corresponding to the main zone of pleasure in the developing child. All human wishes and needs were considered to be more or less disguised attempts to stimulate these pleasure zones, and all human problems were felt to stem from problems reconciling these wishes with reality and with one's conscience.

(It was only towards the end of his career that Freud came to feel that there was also a basic drive towards aggression, which opposed the drive towards bodily pleasure. This drive had destruction as its goal.)

In the psychosexual theory, all dependency needs are considered to derive from wishes to receive oral pleasure. All urges to spoil, be messy, or to be obsessively neat derive from wishes to receive anal pleasure, or desires to please reality or one's conscience by resisting desires to receive anal pleasure. Desires to relate to the opposite sex are considered to be wishes to receive phallic (genital) pleasure.

The Oedipal conflict stems from a desire to have the opposite-sexed parent at one's exclusive disposal for gratifying the wish to receive genital pleasure. Since that wish requires disposing of one's rival, the opposite-sexed parent, it leads to a fear of retaliation. In boys, Freud considered this to be a fear of castration by one's father. In girls, this was felt to be connected to a belief that they had been castrated.

Psychological and emotional problems are felt to be determined by the impact of these wishes and fantasies on the human mind, which was described in terms of the three models outlined above. Thus, one's conscience might disapprove of one's wish to have sexual intercourse with one's parent. The wish would be repressed, but the conflict would result in various symptoms.

Some of these symptoms would be problematic, and others would be innocuous. Dreams are symptoms of such conflicts, and contain important clues about repressed psychosexual conflicts. Less benign are symptoms such as hysteria, which is the term used for conversion symptoms such as psychologically caused paralysis.

The wish is repressed, or forced into the unconscious. This is the work of the ego, as are the other defenses. The wish does not go away, but rather the psychological energy invested in it remains constant, until the wish is discharged. Thus, we have the three models (the topographic, structural and economic) represented.

Ego Psychology

Psychoanalysts found Freud's theory invaluable, but began to modify it encompass experiences which classical, psychosexual theory did not seem adequate to explain. They began to feel that the rational, goal-directed part of man, the ego, was more than a mere buffer between the instincts (the id) and the conscience (the super-ego). They began to suspect that the urge to know, to strive, to discover, to adapt, etc., was the result of a drive separate from the drive towards bodily pleasure. In addition, the function of the ego in adapting to reality began to lead to an increasing interest among psychoanalysts regarding the environment to which the individual had to adapt. Perhaps the most famous name in ego-psychology development is Heinz Hartmann.

Object-Relations Theory

An alternate perspective sprang up in psychoanalytic circles. It was called object-relations theory, or the middle school. Seen from this point of view, human nature is centered on the drive towards relationships, and human development is a journey from a position of total dependency and on the mother to one of relative independence.

The child is seen as starting out as being psychologically immersed in the mother, and then as becoming aware of itself as a separate being. This causes anxiety, which is allayed by the development of a comforting sense of the mother's love, care and support. In effect, the awareness of the mother's feelings of love becomes a separate part of the individual, and is carried around inside of the individual as a fantasy of the mother's loving and approving presence.

Object relations theorists discovered that interest in the father develops as the child becomes aware of himself, and that this interest serves to draw the child into the world. The father's example and approval inspires the child in continuing his development as a separate individual from the mother.

If the mother fails to approve of the child's exercise of his independence, a frightening conflict springs up within the child. The child will fail to develop feelings of pleasure, comfort and security in the exercise of its individual capacities, and will instead remain overly dependent. The child will be filled with rage, depression and terror as a result of this thwarting of his natural instinct to develop as a self-confident, independent person. As a result, he will be filled with these negative feelings, and will form dependent relationships or addictions in order to defend himself against these feelings. Two of the most famous theorists who developed this line of thinking are Margaret Mahler and James Masterson.

Aside from the focus on separation and individuation, object relations theorists focused on the development of self-esteem and also on the ways in which people form relationships. They explored the ways in which self-esteem is developed as a result of the acceptance and approval of the child by his parents, and how a failure in this regard could be humiliating and disorganizing. The theorist most associated with this line of thought is Heinz Kohut.

They also began to explore the ways in which people sought out others, how they chose them, and why they stayed with them. They saw that people tended to seek out people who were like their parents. They also saw that the desire to be like their parents, and to be attached to their parents seemed to be a drive in its own right. Many theorists have contributed to this line of thought, starting with Freud. One notable contributor is W.R.D. Fairbairn.

Other object relations theorists developed an interest in exploring the instinct towards aggression that Freud posited late in his career. They saw that this instinct was aroused by the frustrations which life necessarily brought. The arousal of the desire to destroy what frustrates him necessarily places the child in conflict with his need of and attachment to his mother, as his mother necessarily fails to completely gratify his every wish. This conflict begins to extend to others, for the same reason.

Fear of the consequences of his own rage is frightening to the child, and so the child defends himself against awareness of his destructive wishes. As a consequence, he disassociates himself from his destructive wishes, and comes to feel that they are outside of himself. He is frightened by these "monsters", and feels persecuted by them. He projects them upon others, and so fears and hates those upon whom he projects his own fears and hates.

If he does not integrate these destructive feelings with his loving feelings, then he will always be subject to a set of polarized passions which drive him from loving idealization to hatred, fear and destructiveness and back again. If he does come to terms with them and integrates them, then he will be able to adopt a balanced and moderate view of himself and others. Two famous names in the investigation of the ramifications of this line of thought are Melanie Klein and Otto Kernberg.

Yet another focus of some object relations theorists was on the ways in which the mother or caretaker soothes and comforts the anxious or overwhelmed child, and enables the child to learn to do this for himself. Donald Winnicott and W.R. Bion are notable contributors to this line of exploration.

The awareness of all of these additional facets of human nature and development led to an increasing awareness of the impact of adaptation of the client to the client's real family, and an increasing awareness of the importance of the ways in which the differences between people's actual experiences impacted their feelings and development.

Modern Psychoanalytic Theory

Modern psychoanalytic theory integrates all of these developments. It includes Freud's discoveries regarding conscience, adaptation to society's demands, the desire for physical and sexual pleasure, and the way in which people defend themselves from awareness of unacceptable wishes. It also includes awareness of the natural desire of people to adapt, function and strive, develop feelings of positive self-esteem, cope with anxiety and stress, relate emotionally to other people, manage conflicts between love and hate, and manage conflicts between the desire for security and the desire to be a self-confident and independent person. It has become aware of how real differences between families and relevant real world events impacts individuals.

Modern psychoanalytic thought has grown into a theoretical edifice of vast complexity, far beyond the bounds of what simplifications and popularizations of Freud's original theories have led the public to regard as psychoanalysis. Psychoanalytic techniques have been elaborated to the point where the original approach, in which a patient lies on an analyst's couch and talks about his fantasies about the analyst, is only one of many approaches.

Other psychoanalytic approaches are often called psychoanalytic psychotherapy. They include an approach in which the client sits and faces the therapist, and in which the focus is on an active discussion of the client's real relationships, using an active and systematic exploration of the client's family history. This is called family-of-origin therapy. Variations on psychoanalytic psychotherapy are also done with groups of clients, or with the entire family at one time.

Family Systems Theory

Psychoanalysis began its development shortly before the turn of the century. Family systems theory is much more recent. It began to become apparent as a movement or viewpoint in the late 1950's.

Some psychologists and psychiatrists began to focus on the ways in which the actual, current family effects the individual in problematic ways. They came to see individual feelings and behavior as part of a system of interacting people. One might almost say that some of them came to see the individual as no more than a cog in a larger machine, which they called the family system.

From this viewpoint, the concept of the individual and the hold of the past on the individual was almost a myth. What was important was the ways in which the individuals in the family system currently interacted or behaved. This, they felt, was the source of human suffering and counterproductive behavior. They felt that people in a family interacted in ways that were healthy or unhealthy, and that changing family rules, communication styles, or interaction patterns would solve all psychological, behavioral and emotional problems. They developed an approach to psychotherapy in which the therapist met with the whole family at one time.

There sprang up a number of sub-schools in family systems thinking. One school focused exclusively on the ways in which current rewards and punishments affect behavior. This school, behaviorism, was descended from a school of academic psychology which was founded on observations based on the training of rats.

Another school was focused on the ways in which people communicate, and attempted to teach people to communicate in more logical, positive and realistic ways. Notable here is the work of Virginia Satir.

Another school focused on the amount of time that people in a family spend with each other, the degree to which they tolerate differences of opinion and conflict, and how they resolve conflicts. Salvador Minuchin's contribution is outstanding in this regard.

Yet another school combined psychoanalytic theory with an acknowledgement of the importance of current mutual response and adaptation within the family. This book is written from the perspective of this latter point of view. An outstanding contributor to this train of thought is Thomas Slip.
