Chuck Kriete \< <dotchuck@epix.net> \> submitted:

Ref: John Rossetti's comments on flying rudder-only with a Livewire Trainer. I am now on my fourth model of the Livewire Kitten. The first weighed in at 13 ounces, was made of balsa and blue foam, powered with an MG1 on six 550 cells, and flew great. So great it flew away in a thermal.

The second was a dud. Mostly of pink and blue foam, it was two ounces heavier, and I never did get it trimmed out well. It also flew with the same equipment as the first, but crashed often enough so that the front end gave up.

The third was a jewel -- is a jewel, as it is still flying after many repairs added two plus ounces to its original weight of 7. It flew first with a GWS A motor and 9/4 prop, a nine volt lithium battery from Ralph Weaver, and managed over 9 hours of air time. Necessary repairs (due to poor judgment of landing area) changed the motor to a GWS S-1, with startling improve- ment in climb rate and duration on the lithium.

When that wore out (both motor and battery) I shifted back to the GWS A motor, and this time a 9/7 prop with a 7-cell NiMH from Cloud 9. Flight times are shorter, and the climb is not as sprightly as with the S-1, but the fun is still there. It is stable, predictable, can loop after a dive, and thermals like crazy.

The new Kitten that is almost finished will go back to the GWS S-1, a two-cell lithium from Don Matlack, and is coming in at just about 7 ounces. There is great learning that results from building several models of the same design, and much fun in rudder-only.

The one I am still flying has well over 15 hours in the air so far, and the nose has just been rebuilt for the sixth time. I am a slow learner.

Chuck Kriete

...Robert, I think you've misremembered the "Kicking Duck". That was a method of getting 2 control functions from a single channel that the late Howard McEntee devised. The name came from its working principle, which made use of the "inductive kick" from the receiver relay -- this was, of course, some time before transistors became common in R/C equipment. \[JW\]

...Otto, during my investigation of present-day model dopes, one thing I learned was that neither Sig nor Brodak actually manu- factures the dope they sell. Brodak's dope is made by Randolph, who've been in the aircraft dope business for 83 years.

Aircraft dope MUST be formulated for resistance to sunlight and for long-lasting flexibility. Aircraft dope mist also protect its underlying fabric from harm due to ultraviolet light exposure.

One way Randolph dope achieves fabric protection is by using opaque pigments: coloring agents that block light transmission.

On the other hand, Sig's dopes are produced by a company whose primary line of business is in automotive finishes. These commonly use translucent pigments, so as to produce a look of "depth" to the finish. Light can penetrate these pigments -- and that causes two detrimental effects insofar as model airplane use is concerned.

First, more coats of dope are needed to achieve a "scale-like" finish. That adds weight, and increases both the cost of the finish and the time it takes to apply it.

Second, ultraviolet light can penetrate the translucent pigments and damage the covering material (tissue, Silkspan, or silk -- even polyester isn't immune to U-V exposure). U-V penetration is of no concern in car finishes, because those are applied to metal.

Insofar as pinholing is concerned, that's often due to over- thinning. Adding acetone in small quantities probably won't hurt a dope's final finish. But years ago I found that a slow- evaporating solvent called "Retarder" works best for thinning dope. Using that and a soft brush, I can achieve almost a sprayed-finish look. \[JW\]

Dr. Mike Hawkins \< <mikeh@samart.co.th> \> says:

I am glad that the gentleman who needed an SBD canopy managed to get one, but I suggest that the next time he need one, or a B-17 nose cone for that matter, he moulds it out of an old coke/ lemonade bottle.

This really is very easy. Carve a male mould out of balsa. Fill the surface and sand. An epoxy filler is better than cellulose as it is more heat-resistant. Cut the bottom off the bottle and jam in the mould with scrap wood underneath.

Apply your covering heat gun on "hot" and in seconds you have your canopy. Apply the heat to the open end of the bottle first; otherwise it shoots the mould out like a cork from a pop-gun.

If you get it wrong the first time, drink another quart of your favourite beverage and try again. Takes only a few minutes !

Austin \< <themouse7@attbi.com> \> wrote:

Here's a note for the guy boiling the engines. A secret I learned from the C-L crowd is to use a crock pot (got mine at a thrift store for next to nothing) and put 50/50 water and antifreeze in that. I let my engines cook overnight.

After about 24 hours even the filthiest engines will just wipe off. I usually use a soft rag, and a toothbrush to get the goop out of the fins; no scrubbing, just a little help to get in the cracks. If you have to scrub, you probably didn't let the motor cook long enough.

I then use my air compressor to blow out all of the water. Some guys put their motors in an oven to dry. I suppose that would work too. I then oil everything with Marvel Air Tool oil for storage. If I'm not going to store a motor, I usually run it on a test stand with a fuel with plenty of oil, to get it lubed back up. (I also oil my engines as I reassemble them; I forgot to mention that -- and clean everything really well. I think that is very important.). My FP's got black as tar, but after I was told about this cleaning method, they look like new!

Austin

Andy Woitowicz \< <ajw@ntl.sympatico.ca> \> contributed:

I've never built a foam wing. As Dave Platt says, air is lighter than foam. However, there is a foam that I've worked with recently that has got me interested in the stuff. The Pibros is a foam delta glider that uses a thin, sheet foam material called Depron.

This is only available in Europe (so far) but you can make your own from posterboard. Posterboard is the same stuff, but with a layer of paper laminated to both sides. It comes in 3/16" and 1/8" thickness. All you need to do is use some coarse sandpaper on both sides, just enough to break any water seal -- and simply soak the material in your bathtub overnight. You'll need to weight it down with something. I use old soda bottles filled with water. After that, the paper peels right off, for the most part.

There will still be a lot of paper stuck to the surfaces but an hour or so of rubbing this off with a sponge or the palm of your hand takes care of that. The result is beautiful foam sheets that can be used for wings and tails.

My first Pibros has had a Norvel .061 stuck to the nose with a bit of fuselage structure in the middle. Balsa fins were added along with being covered with Towerkote in a starburst pattern. All-up weight with two servos is 14 ounces. Not bad for 200 square inches. With my Norvel converted to rear exhaust and a tuned pipe doing 22K on a 6 X 3, I expect to do unlimited verticals.

The Pibros was originally conceived as a simple, cheap slope soarer, so my second, just finished, was done up this way. The original uses laminated center pieces to build up the top and bottom surfaces into an airfoil. Most Pibroses, or Pibri, come in at 8 to 10 ounces. At 200 squares this is quite good.

However, still addicted to balsa, I used ribs to build up the airfoil, and the completed airframe came in at 3.5 ounces. With two ounces of radio, and all-up at 5.5 ounces, I hope to take advantage of the small hills we have in our area.

Check out \< <http://www.rc-soar.com/pibros/> \>

Dave Larkin \< <dslarkin@blvl.igs.net> \> states:

I haven't seen Covertite -- or is it Coverlite? -- but on the supposition that it is just 're-badged' Airspan or Litespan, the covering technique is all-important, and one has to unlearn some old techniques.

First of all, iron temperature is critical, so it's best to use a 21st Century iron, though a 'spring-type' temperature gage can be used with an ordinary iron, if that's all you have available.

I suggest using Balsaloc as an adhesive, though you can also use UHU purple stick glue. Covergrip can also be used, but is rather powerful. Apply the adhesive to the structure, allow to dry; then position the tissue and tack it down, trying to get it reasonably taut. Go round the outline again, applying tension to get it even tauter. Do not shrink the tissue until both sides of the surface are covered. It's possible that you may get better results on rounded wingtips by using a separate section for these.

When the covering process is complete you can run the iron over the surface to tauten it. Be careful not to exceed the specified temperatures, and stay away from the outlines or the tissue may loosen and move.

Add your trim, cut from iron-on tissue which has had the adhesive applied and allowed to dry before cutting out. If the use of dope is recommended, as it is with Airspan, you can apply it now.

I normally use a couple of 50% thinned coats on open-framework flying surfaces. Over sheet surfaces you may need to add more. But on open areas, more coats of dope will lead to a brittle condition.

Regarding PAW throttles, if the idle is high then someone has disturbed the spraybar orientation. That's easily done when setting up the linkage. Remove the carb, look up it from below. The spraybar has 2 holes drilled about 150 degrees apart. With the barrel wide open, turn the spraybar until you can just see both holes from below. Hold this position (having set the arm in the desired position) and tighten up. You should then easily get a 4K rpm idle.

With the bigger PAWs, with the Varijet throttle, an idle of 1500 rpm or lower is obtainable.

Best regards, Dave Larkin
