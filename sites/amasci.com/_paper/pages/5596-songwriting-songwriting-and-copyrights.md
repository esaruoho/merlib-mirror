<td width="100%"><h1 id="songwriting-copyrights">SONGWRITING &amp; COPYRIGHTS</h1>
<p>Songwriting is an art that you can develop through years of painstaking work, but the learning curve can also be shortened if you have some help along the way. With this column, we will attempt to help you learn the basics and some of the more advanced skills. Whether you want to be the next Bowie, or just sing an original ballad below women's balconies, there is something for you.</p>

<strong>Songwriting # 35</strong><br />
<em>by A-J Charron (15 Feb 2002)</em><br />
A lot of bands have a lead vocalist who also plays an instrument. You listen to a live album from them and you hear the instrument performed to perfection and wonder how they do it. You've tried to sing while playing your guitar and have discovered that it's not an easy thing to do.<br />

<td colspan="2"><p><a href="20020129.html"><strong>Charts</strong></a><br />
<strong>Songwriting # 34</strong><br />
<em>by A-J Charron (29 Jan 2002)</em><br />
I found that when I bring a new song to my band mates, it can be a long learning curve for the other guys. Now I put everything into charts and give these to the guys a week or so before meeting them with our guns ready. Works a lot better.</p>
<p><a href="../topics/buyingequipment.html"><img src="../topics/images/equipment_small.gif" data-border="0" width="176" height="32" alt="How To Buy Equipment" /></a><br />
<a href="20010725.html"><strong>Where To Find Great Prices</strong></a><br />
<strong>Songwriting # 33</strong><br />
<em>by A-J Charron (25 Jul 2001)</em><br />
“The pawn shop is a musician’s best friend.” I’ve heard that phrase many times before. The worst part of it is, it’s true. Many musicians end up pawning old instruments and old equipment there. They get out of the game or they simply change their instruments or equipment for something newer. This is where you can profit from their losses.</p>
<p><a href="20010501.html"><strong>Best-Kept Secret</strong></a><em><br />
</em> <strong>Songwriting # 32</strong><em><br />
by A-J Charron (01 May 2001)</em><br />
Following the last column, this week we’ll be looking at our last option of what to do with your songs. This option applies to both performers and non-performers alike. The best-kept secret in the music business is The Publisher.<br />
</p>
<p><a href="20010406.html"><strong>Where Do I Go From Here?</strong><br />
</a> <strong>Songwriting # 31</strong><em><br />
by A-J Charron (06 Apr 2001)</em><br />
What <em>do</em> you do with your songs? That's a common enough question among songwriters. There are three avenues you can take. Today, we'll be looking at the first two.<br />
</p>
<p><a href="20010323.html"><strong>Arrangements</strong><br />
</a> <strong>Songwriting # 30</strong><em><br />
by A-J Charron (23 Mar 2001)</em><br />
Some songwriters get stuck at the “unplugged” part of writing songs. They have no idea how to arrange their songs. If that’s your case, then that’s OK. It doesn’t mean you won’t make it. It just means that you’ll need someone else to arrange your songs. If you can’t find a particular arrangement for a song, it might be a good idea to try and enlist someone else’s aid.<br />

<td colspan="2"><p><a href="../columns/20010126.html"><strong>Putting Things Together</strong></a><br />
<em>by David Hodge (26 Jan 2001)</em><br />
A lead is simply the guitar playing a melody of sorts. Over the next few months we’re going to be examining how melodies and chords work together. I’ve called these columns “workbooks” because not only will we look at specific examples from all kinds of songs, we’ll have exercises to try out original ideas as well.<br />
[from <a href="../columns/index.html">Guitar Columns</a>]</p>
<p><a href="../columns/20010213.html"><strong>Home On Your Range</strong></a><br />
<em>by David Hodge (13 Feb 2001)</em><br />
Whether or not you realize it, you yourself may be one of your biggest obstacles when it comes to songwriting. Think about how you go about writing - especially if you’re one of those “let’s strum around and see what comes up” sort of folks. Chances are you pick up your guitar and the first chord you play will be an E, A, C, D or G. Unless, of course, you happen to be in a mad, sad or bad frame of mind and then you pound out an Em or an Am.<br />
