Ed Stevens \< <ed@norvel.com> \> responded:

Thank you for your email explaining your issues with the low end of the NORVEL engine. There are several possible explanations for your problems.

First, we can look at fuel contamination. NORVEL engines are the coolest-running engines around. They run about 50 degrees F cooler than even Cox engines. As a result, contaminates in the fuel which may burn up in other engines, including Cox engines, may not burn up in a NORVEL. When these contaminates do not burn up, they tend to foul the plug gradually, causing poor idling among other problems.

The most common contaminate is dissolved rubber. Use of a rubber squeeze bulb or a syringe with a black rubber/neoprene stopper is 100% guaranteed to cause problems with your NORVEL .049 - .061 engine. Nitromethane dissolves rubber, and dissolved rubber cannot be filtered out by any common fuel filter. This rubber builds up on the plug, causing it to act colder.

Sometimes the build-up happens in one flight, and sometimes over the course of many flights. It depends on several factors (age of bulb/syringe, defueling or not back into bottle, etc.). Even if you change glow plugs, you may experience problems the next flight.

Also, some fuels have additives which do not burn up in the NORVEL's cooler combustion. Omega fuel has always been good to us, so that isn't the problem. If you have used other fuels, it's hard to say.

Contamination of the plug is fairly easy to identify with a magnifying glass or keen eye. Compare the coil to a new plug's coil. If the coil has a white or grey build-up on it (where the new plug's coil is shiny silver), the coil is already fouled. Of course, if it is black or brown, then the plug is fouled, too. There is no way to remedy a fouled plug.

Second, you may need to add head shims to lower compression. The NORVEL engine should have 3-4 gaskets under the head if you are not happy with the idle. Lower compression improves idle characteristics with relatively little rpm loss.

Third, you can add an exhaust extension to the muffler, or restrict the output diameter of the muffler. This will raise the engine's temperature and improve idle.

Finally, it is important to get a tachometer reading. If your engine is idling at 4,500-6,000 rpm (normal for a properly tuned NORVEL engine with a 6x3 prop and 45-60 minutes of run time), then there would be no reason why the airplane shouldn't come down and taxi around like a champ.

We have several AquaStars in our club, and nobody has idling prob- lems. Of course, they have me as mother figure reminding them that the rubber bulb or syringe just won't work. It's amazing how many modelers dismiss this piece of technical advice as poppycock.

I had a guy from Florida call me one time with a technical problem very similar to yours. He and five friends all bought AquaStars and BigMig .061's. One of them lived on the water in the Florida Keys. They had a bunch of problems with idle, and one representative called me. He couldn't believe NORVEL engines could be so bad, although his other friends had already given up.

It turns out that three of them were using one guy's rubber bulb (a ten-year-old bulb, no less), and the other two guys were using a fuel we don't recommend. After some new fuel and plugs, they all got up and flying.

So, I suspect your problems are in the same area.

I should say that it is extremely difficult to troubleshoot via email, as the medium is a very slow way to do question and answer problem solving. So, if you don't think I'm on the mark, we can try again. I would need to know what your engine is reading on the tachometer (high and low end), number of shims under the head, running time, fuel you use now, fuels you have used, type of transfer device from fuel bottle to fuel tank, years of experience with R/C modelling, average weather conditions when flying (if relevant), and size/brand of propeller.

Or, if you have the resources to call, our technical support number is (330) 425-3631. I look forward to hearing from you soon.

Best regards, Ed Stevens NORVEL, Ltd.

In response to Sam Brauer's impressions of the Horst gearbox, that gearbox is notorious for binding (usually resulting in a fried speed controller).

I bought a Nora (that comes with a stock 280 and Horst 4:1 ratio gearbox). I did my research on the E-Flight Archive, and the fix to this problem is to drill out the shaft ever so slightly to accommodate the heat expansion that occurs while the motor is in use. Not wanting to drill a new gearbox, I tried coating the shaft with graphite powder, but the binding still occurred (in my house, after a 30- second motor run).

So, I drilled it out, flew the maiden voyage and haven't had a problem with it since. (FWIW, I think my Cox Dragonfly .049-powered, Randy Randolph designed, 3-channel BEETWEEN flies more slowly than the Nora!)

Roy Clough \< <royce6@juno.com> \> contributed:

CO2 Musings: When I first got an OK CO2 motor back in the 40s, I tried it in planes, boats and cars. But learning something from slopping water into the boat, I made a small plastic tank to hold the cartridge in the plane.

I'd fill the tank with warm water -- maybe an ounce or so, and get somewhat longer runs. Other guys at that time tried little woolen soaker sleeves. I recall seeing one setup where small washers had been slipped over the feed line and soldered in place to absorb atmospheric heat.

My CO2-powered boat, with a fairly heavy flywheel, worked okay, but the race car, with a direct wheel drive, just spun its drive wheel to little effect. About this time, at the urging of Howard G. McEntee, I converted an OK CO2 to run as a diesel. Not great, but I was quite happy to discover, as I had with my earlier conversion of a sub-piston Atom, that the compression could be varied by screwing the cylinder in and out of the case. When I got it to work, I simply tightened the lock nut and ran it fixed compression. What bugs me today is that while I used a rear disk intake valve, mainly to fill up crankcase volume, I can't remember for the life of me how I bypassed the piston.

Somewhere one of the magazines has a picture of this thing, not as a construction article, but as a curiosity.

CO2: In the industrial scene we used chunk dry ice to tumble molded rubber products, like oil seals, to embrittle the mold flash and knock it off. I used to think at the time that it would be no great exercise to make a small tubular tank with a quick-sealing cap, and a punch to cut a slug of dry ice that would just fit. This would be a job for gloved fingers, but technically I could see nothing wrong with the idea. The tank would then contain a slug of dry ice rapidly turning to liquid, and a small amount of air that should blow out when the motor was started.

I pass the idea along now, for argument, amazement, or actual trial by anybody interested. Chunk dry ice was, and I guess is, quite generally available. Something I only thought of now, was that it might be a convenient way to chill little Gasparin tanks before filling, but the stuff is cold, probably colder than the liquid the tank holds, and I'd beware of making tank or feed line brittle enough to break.

Back in the 30's there was an expansion engine called the Imp. It came in two versions, as an in-line twin and as a Vee 4. I had the Vee 4. It had a tank for chunks of dry ice and carbide powder, plus a hopper on top for a small quantity of water which could be dumped into the tank to start the carbide evolving acetylene gas and heat which encouraged the dry ice to vaporize. These things were supposed to be relatively powerful (for the time) but I thought the process too messy and used my Imp with a compressed air tank.

Roy C.
