I have a seven-year-old free flight model, and its covering is creeping at the trailing edges in spots. Re-ironing won't hold in the hot summer sun.

Any suggestions as to what to use to stop the creep? CA maybe?

Frank Hodson

...Frank, peeling back the loose areas and coating both the wood and the underside of the covering with Sig's "Stix-It" -- then re-ironing down the edges after the Stix-It dries -- ought to do the job. My son David taught me to use this technique to anchor the "wrapped-around" edges of PolySpan (another instance of model covering tending to pull away from the structure). \[JW\]

Sam Brauer \< <SBrauerBCC@aol.com> \> contributed:

It seems like there is some interest in inexpensive gliders for youngsters. Recently I was faced with this dilemma (i.e. poor flying characteristics of most of these gliders) while visiting some friends who have 2 boys, aged 5 and 7.

My friends are fortunate enough to live near Lansdale, Pennsylvania, home of Penn Valley Hobby Center -- which has the largest selection of free flight model kits of any hobby shop I've ever seen.

It turns out that Guillow's is not the only game in town. North Pacific is still selling simple, inexpensive gliders and rubber- powered airplanes. I think the A-J stuff is still available, but it's been renamed -- I remember seeing some folding wing catapult airplane. Check Penn Valley's website.

I'll date myself- I clearly remember buying Sleek Streek's for \$0.29 and Skeeter's for \$0.19. I wouldn't be surprised if the first time I bought these planes they were even cheaper. Anyhow, the North Pacific stuff was a lot lighter and consequently flew better than Guillow's, which was always more expensive anyhow. North Pacific still makes all-wood Sleek Streeks, Star Flyers, etc. -- but now they can also be found with molded foam wings.

Although I generally prefer balsa for building, I think foam in this case works like a charm. It's light, tough, and easier for kids to handle. The worst problem is that the foam wings and tail need to be glued in, especially to the plastic piece wing mount; but RC-56 or a similar adhesive works fine if you allow enough set-up time.

North Pacific also makes glider versions using these foam wings. There are two small gliders, one with a foam fuselage and one with a spruce fuselage. The foam fuselage breaks instantly on impact, and needs reinforcement. I haven't tried giving it to a kid as an indoor glider. The spruce fuselage is great, plus they're cheap.

I've noticed that most young children have trouble dealing with the amount of time it takes to wind a rubber motor, while the dexterity needed to hold a winder is frequently lacking. Plus kids have lots of energy, which means they like to throw things and run.

Unfortunately, accurately throwing a hand-launch glider (i.e. not into the ground) can be hard for a kid too. But kids can use a catapult! A simple notch cut in the spruce fuselage works great, about 1/2" in front of the leading edge. I used a 12" loop of 3/32" rubber. The catapult did much better than either my arm or the kids' arms for throwing the glider -- plus the two kids could team up and launch gliders together.

The catapult also worked for one of the Whitewings series of gliders which are sold in malls. The Whitewings' performance was close to that of the North Pacific gliders, which surprised me. Some of the catapult flights were pushing 20 plus seconds -- enough time for the glider to make several circles, and the kids to try to catch them.

If you're having trouble making an airplane fly with a kid, give catapult gliders a shot. They're cheap enough for anyone to have a ready supply on hand to hook youngsters in playgrounds. You and the kid should have a ball.

Sam Brauer

...Sam, I believe that the folding-wing catapult gliders that you mentioned having seen at Penn Valley are Florio Flyers. These used to be made in John Florio's basement workshop in St. Mary's, Pennsylvania; after John's death I believe they were discontinued.

I hope they're back on the market again, because these little all- balsa craft were real performers! They flew FAR better than the A-J Interceptors, which were spiral-dive prone. I've lost three Florio Flyers (I'm not certain I have the name quite right: John also designed and kitted R/C models with a similar name) out of sight in thermals. I'd probably have lost even more of them if I hadn't stopped flying them soon after John's death -- I have only a couple of these neat-flying craft left.

Actually, these models wouldn't be at all hard to build from scratch. (I think I remember seeing full-size plans in one of the model magazines -- or was it in a club newsletter?) The Florio design didn't have cambered wings (neither did most of the A-J Interceptors, come to think of it) and the wing folding arrange- ment was rather simple. \[JW\]

Returning to the TTOMA newsletter THE THUMB PRINT, the latest issue contains an intriguing article by Fritz Mueller on the "E-Charger" toy planes recently put on the market by Spin Master -- makers of the now-famous Air Hogs compressed-air-powered toy airplanes.

The E-Charger models (which retail for about \$15) are powered by electric motors, energized by a 3.3 Farad capacitor in the airplane. This is charged up with an external 4 AA battery pack.

The E-Charger planes are admittedly "cheap toys" and were never intended for "model-type" performance. However, their power units are challenging, and Fritz explored the possibilities of using one of these in a scratch-built airplane. The article is rather extensive, complete with a performance graph; I'll just summarize Fritz's results.

Fritz doesn't think much of the practicability of "condenser power"; but DOES like the idea of using the motor/gearbox powered by a pair of 50 mAh nicads. The single 3.3 Farad capacitor that comes with each toy weighs 4 grams, and provides maybe 3 to 4 seconds of useful propulsive power, depending on how fresh the AA charging pack is.

Two 50 mAh nicads total 7 grams, but will provide something like 40 seconds of useful power to the E-Charger motor/gearbox/prop combination.

At the BlackSheep meet last month I saw a couple of small boys flying (attempting to, anyway) E-Charger planes. With a little help from adults, one of these toys became capable of about 15-second flights. They weren't consistent, though: these planes are really too small for true stability.

I've examined a couple of these E-Charger thingies. In my opinion they'd do a better job of hauling a model through the air with a propeller such as one of the smallest-sized Williams Brothers CO2 props. And in fact I'm thinking of buying a couple of E-Chargers; throwing away everything except the motor units -- and using those to power a couple or three of my favorite Bill Hannan designs, with 50 mAh nicad power, of course...

But there's a dagnab problem here! What should I build first? A "Stringless Wonder"? A "Yen To Fly"? A "Neighborhood Nieuport"? A "Flying Stained Glass Window"? An "AM-FM"? Gad, that Hannan lad was prolific with his simple-but-fine-flying model airplane designs...! \[JW\]
