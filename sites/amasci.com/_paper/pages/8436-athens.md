# Athens (Αθήνα)

We spent more time in Athens than anywhere else on our vacation - about four days when we first got to Greece, followed by a couple overnight stays between visits to other places and a couple days at the end before flying home.  These pictures are taken from all those times and they're presented generally in chronological order.

<td><a href="images/greece/athens/Lyn%20At%20Panathenaic%20Way.jpg">Lyn At Panathenaic Way<br />
</a>Taken on our first day in Athens.  We had reserved a room the first night at a hotel that looked close to the airport.  The plan was to crash there (since we were arriving at 4am) and head into town the next day to find a room a little closer to the action.
<p>As it turns out, the Hotel Miranda is close to an airport - the old airport.  Athens has recently opened up a new airport all the way on the other side of the city.  So we had quite an exciting high-speed taxi ride to our hotel, at 4am on deserted Athens streets, with a taxi driver who didn't speak much English.  What a way to start a vacation.</p>
<p>After some other adventures (including getting lost at least once) we finally got a room and walked over towards the Acropolis.  It was pretty late so we didn't go in, but instead walked around the vicinity.</p>
<p>The Panathenaic Way was an important road in ancient Greece that led up to the Propylaia, or entrance to the Acropolis.  During the <a href="http://depthome.brooklyn.cuny.edu/classics/dunkle/courses/panfest/panfest.htm">Panathenaic Festival</a> which was held each year to commemorate the birth of Athena, the Panathenaic Procession would follow this road up to the Acropolis.  This procession was a very important event, and in fact one of the friezes on the Parthenon is believed to depict a Panathenaic Procession.</p></td>

</a>You can see the few pieces of sculpture left on the Parthenon.  The horse's head that is left on the Pediment is from the chariot of Helios (the Sun). Reclining next to it is Dionysus.  Most of the surviving sculpture from the pediment is now in the Acropolis Museum and the British Museum.  The <a href="http://www.goddess-athena.org/Museum/Sculptures/Group/Parthenon_East_Pediment_x.htm">East Pediment</a> originally depicted the birth of Athena.  (This is appropriate given that the Parthenon was a temple of Athena and indeed she was the patron goddess of the city of Athens.)
<p>The larger evenly-spaced rectangular holes held a row of Persian shields taken as booty after a battle against the Persians in 334 BC and dedicated on the Acropolis by "Alexander and the Greeks, except the Lacedaemonians."  The small groups of holes between them (directly under each triglyph) at one point held the bronze letters of an inscription in honor of the Roman emperor Nero. </p></td>
