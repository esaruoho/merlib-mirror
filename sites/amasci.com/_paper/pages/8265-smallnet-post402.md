Francis S. Person asked about kerosene substitutes, and JW stated that "Plain old gas station kerosene (NOT truck diesel fuel!) works fine in model diesels."

I am VERY suspicious of kerosene bought at the local gas station, for all of the reasons you mentioned: impurities, water, etc.

<span id="15">Source: Kerosene\
</span> A VERY GOOD source of kerosene for diesel fuel can be found at the local Home Centers: Lowe's, Home Depot, etc. It's usually sold in 5-gallon cans, and is for use in kerosene heaters. It is usually labeled as such, and is often labeled as "K-1" or "1-K", also often referred to as "odorless" although THAT is really funny.

This time of the year is a good time to look for it, particularly in the South, since this is the season when kerosene heaters are more commonly in use. I just saw the display of 5-gallon cans at Lowe's: must have been a hundred of them in the stack! I bought my next year's supply of kerosene and have it stashed away in the shop. Bill Lee

John Carpenter \< <john.carpenter@flightsafety.com> \> asked:

I'd like to know if anybody has used one of the Davis diesel conversion heads on the Norvel .049/.061. If I remember correctly, Norvel uses the traditional wristpin-connecting rod arrangement, and might just stand up to the added stresses.

I have several .061's in control-line and R/C models, and like everything about these little jewels except for one thing. They are LOUD. I'd love to be able to install a bigger prop and slow down a few of them.

...John, Diesel conversions of Cox engines don't fail at the piston-rod juncture. The crankshafts break.

NO Cox reed valve engine has a strong enough crankshaft for diesel use. They are too brittle. That's why the Davis "Diesel Conversion Kit" for Cox motors includes a new, stronger shaft.

However, the Davis conversions have a different weak spot. That's the Teflon-composite plastic diaphragm used as a "contra-piston", to allow compression adjustment.

These little disks can't take the stress, strain, & heat for long. That's why Davis includes more than one in his "conversion kits".

As I've recommended again and again: If you want to fly with a model diesel, buy one that's designed for the purpose.

There are MANY choices available -- and in most cases the whole new engine costs little more than Bob Davis charges for his "conversion kits".

I especially like the MP Jets .061 diesels. Those are as potent as the Norvels, yet FAR quieter. PAW .061's are also exceedingly powerful -- I have one on a 2-meter "powered glider", and despite 620 square inches of wing and an all-up weight of 27 ounces, the PAW hauls it right on up.

PAW's have the smallest mufflers of any model engine -- but they do lack precision in their throttles. I rework mine and that solves the problem; but without metal-cutting machinery you may not be able to do the same. \[JW\]

Sam Brauer \< <sbrauer@bccresearch.com> \> submitted:

It looks like I'm having my 15 minutes of fame. Recently, I completed an electric-powered R/C conversion of the C/L Fierce Arrow 400, and I've been told that pictures are showing up in Electric Flight International, Flying Models, and possibly Model Aviation.

I started this project with the help of SMALLnet, who put me in touch with the late Dick Gleason, and got me kicking stuff around with Bill Netzeband -- the designer of the original ship.

If you don't know the airplane, it's a pretty wild-looking flying wing -- I fell in love with it when I saw it in a magazine when I was a kid.

Originally I was planning on using an old Enya 15 I had lying around, but after running the weights and hemming and hawing, I finally went with a Jeti 15/4 Phasor motor powered by an 8 cell CP 1300 mAh nicad battery pack. I've put a bunch of props on it, but right now it's flying with an 8 x 4 Graupner folder. That doesn't break and flies the airplane respectably.

Overall flying weight is about 31 ounces. Yes, it's a bit heavier than the original C-L version, but not too bad. This isn't exactly the combination I'd use now, but we live and learn. (I'd probably go with a 7 cell CP 1700 battery and swing a 9-inch prop.)

Contrary to what I've read about most flying wings, this one is pretty viceless. I've set the center of gravity where it showed up on the plans -- the one thing I notice is that I carry some reflex in the elevons.

You have to be careful when you go inverted: it needs a fair amount of down. However, inside and outside loops are easy, and it will do vertical rolls before it runs out of steam. I think the CG is pretty close: when the airplane is in a dive it's pretty neutral.

The flight characteristics are smooth and graceful, just like the C-L stunters I've seen at the Nationals. Slow speed handling is also quite good. The plane slows down readily without falling out of the sky, although it can exhibit a little wag. Some folks have described the airplane as fast, but I don't think that's true. The Fierce Arrow is not sedate though, but lots of stuff is clearly quicker. It's also quite sturdy -- don't ask me how I know that.

I dressed the airplane up with graphics from Rockin' Rose Grafix. The guy running the shop is Jeff Bemrose -- he's a bit of a character, but I think he's got a real flair for design that's off the beaten path. ...William, oil leakage from PAW exhaust collector rings is NOT from around the rim, although it looks that way. The culprit is the holes for the cylinder screws. The oil oozes out through the clearance between the screws and their holes in the collector ring; then emerges at the outside edge of the bottom fin, exactly as if it had come through the gasketed junction.

The fix for this is to modify the collector ring with pressed-in aluminum tubes. The holes have to be (CAREFULLY) hand- reamed for a LIGHT press fit, using stock K&S tubing of whatever size is right for the screws in the engine.

It's not necessary or desirable to use a tight press fit, because of the danger of crushing the thin top/bottom walls of the ring. In any case, minor crevices or hairline gaps will soon seal themselves with oxidized castor oil.

I advise making the tubing inserts a bit over-length; tapering the "entering end", and pressing the tube through with 3-in-1 oil to reduce the necessary force. After the three tubes are in place, I trim them close to the collector ring surface, then bring them all flush by draw-filing with a fine-cut mill file. \[JW\]

...Francis, I wonder whether you know that Eric Clutton's "Stunt Tank" is intended to be installed with the wedge on the bottom. I think the "Stunt Tank" name came from the common use of this style of fuel tank in control-line stunt models. In those, the tank is installed with the wedge to the outside of the circle, and the fuel pickup is indeed located at the tank's vertical midpoint. "Centrifugal Force" insures that all the fuel is used during flight.

But in R/C models this sort of tank works quite well when installed with the wedge downwards. That is, if your flight's limited to "positive-G" maneuvers.

If you want to fly inverted for more than a few seconds, or do outside loops, you need a "clunk" tank. \[JW\]

...Otto, your remarks point out the reason for my recommendation to Ed Stevens for Norvel to come out with a "modern OK Cub".

By that I did NOT mean a copy of the old 1/2A Classic, but a motor that uses modern technology to achieve the friendliness of the OK. After all: the OK Cub .049 was the second 1/2A engine on the market, and it was so easy to use that MILLIONS were sold, almost all of them to inexperienced modelers.

And those inexperienced modelers had FUN, flying sport-type free flight and control-line airplanes (1/2A competition hadn't yet reared its ugly head) -- mostly in their own neighborhoods, where their friends could see the action and get interested themselves.

The OK Cub .049 (there were other size Cubs, but the .049 was the "magic motor") stayed on the market for a long time. However, the majority of its 15 million (!) engine sales occurred in the first five years: summer of 1949 through the summer of 1955. And most of those sales were uninfluenced by advertising or contest-winning reputation.

People bought lots of Cubs because all over America they could see (and hear) people having fun with them.

OK Cubs powered the first real "Park Flyers". They made enough noise to be attractive to youngsters; yet not so much that the neighbors complained. Yes, their power was puny compared to today's 1/2A engines. But their sales record far exceeds any presently-available engines of any size (except for C\*x, of course -- but the makers of those have lost interest in, and discontinued support for, the "average modeler")...

Electric power has proved: you don't have to have a shrieking power plant to enjoy model flying. But Cub-power modelers all over America learned that more than 50 years ago. \[JW\]
