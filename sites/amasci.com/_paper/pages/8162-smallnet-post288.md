Well, gang, we've sure received a pile of submissions about razor blades! Tim Goldstein \< 

 \> starts off:

You can find all of the old "snappable" razor blades you want here in the USA. Just take a visit to E-bay and do a search using this query: razor (blade,blades). While you will find many single blades or individual packages selling for too high a price to collectors, you will also find collections and assortments of the real McCoy old carbon blades. I picked up quite a load of them for less per blade than I can buy stainless blades at my local grocery store, and even managed to get 100 of the esteemed Gillette Blue Blades.

I was a little concerned about the sharpness of blades that are 30 + years old, but after having tried a sampling from what I got, I am happy to report they are sharp as razors, and so much better than what you can buy today.

I have tried using the blades from modern disposable razors, but I feel the carbon blades cut nicer and are much stiffer and easier to control.

I just checked on E-bay and there are a number of lots for sale with 25 - 100 blades. Tim \[Denver, Colorado\]

Larry Marshall \< 

 \> amplifies:

Double-edged razor blades are invaluable if you cover with tissue. While it may seem an odd place to buy them, they are available from eBay, the auction site. If you go into the Collectibles area you'll find a section devoted to barber paraphernalia. There are many packages of these blades available. Some seem highly collectible and grab way too many dollars to consider. But it's also the case that there are more common ones being sold in lots for not much money.

Once you have a few packs you don't need any more, as these steel blades are readily sharpened by rubbing them on the inside of a glass.

Cheers --- Larry

Mike Russell \< 

 \> continues:

I saw what was mentioned in the last newsletter about razor blades, and I know of a very good source: Bic razors. They are plentiful, cheap, and usable after shaving. Just carefully pry off the small plastic top, remove the blade, and carefully clean it with alcohol.

Cover the dull side with a piece of folded-over masking tape and you've got it. The blade can also be cut with common scissors. The blades seem to last for quite a long time: I've been doing this for years. Mike Russell

Kirk Kirkham \< 

 \> asserts:

I'm new at this, and the razor blade thing immediately caught my eye. Some 25 years ago, after the black blades disappeared from the market, someone in the midwest cornered Gillette and discovered: yep, they're still being made, but only for the foreign market.

He pressed, and finally they agreed to sell him some; but the mini- mum, as I recall, was 10,000 blades. I ordered a two-lifetimes supply from him -- fortunately -- because it is THE blade for such tools as the SIG razor plane and for my wood stripper.

Rather than going through the corporate-America obstacle course, with today's e-mail communications, it probably would be easier and faster to arrange a swap with a modeler in South Africa.

My wood stripper was made for indoor modelers, but it has many other uses. It has micrometers on each end, so precise stripping of wood is possible. Thus, I was able to taper sand a sheet of 4-pound balsa, then strip it and mould "trumpet" wing tips for my 45-inch Stinson 0-49 (from Model Airplane News, February 1950). Tapering the wood allows moulding a wingtip that flares from quarter-inch square at the leading edge to half-inch wide at the trailing edge, with each lamination going full length.

Nah, the O-49 isn't finished yet, but sometimes procrastination pays. The wings will be mounted with pins sliding into tubes and retained by Rare Earth magnets -- available from Radio Shack -- and the wing struts. Those little magnets are real "grabbers." The tricky landing gear will be tongue-in-box, sort of like some older Nordics, only the tongue will be the gear wire bent into an oval to fit the box. The bird should fall apart in a rough landing.

Both the magnets and the landing gear solution were suggested by Gene Wallock, SAM librarian, down in Lawton, Oklahoma. They're great for cowl retainers.

Back to a wood stripper for a moment: I've laminated a lot of wing tips and tail outlines, but precisely-cut stripped wood is a sheer joy to work with. Using strip wood cut with only a straightedge almost inevitably means at least one piece won't quite go all the way down, leaving a strip of glue to be filled or sanded.

With a wood stripper, if the assembly of strips is level on top, they're level on the bottom.

Oh yeah, the blades. The problem with most blades available in the U.S. is the amount of chromium. High chrome steel takes a very fine edge, but that edge curls all too easily. This is the reason I hate Swiss Army Knives. Anything much tougher than a wet whisker will tend to curl that edge over.

Mark Wayne \<

I live in Rockland County, New York, about 20 miles North of the big city. I have been flying R/C since I was 15 years old (about 27 years). I love small planes, the kind you can transport without removing the wing.

I have some large models, but on the SMALL side, I have eight machines currently flying. First, and probably my favorites, are two Ace Ultimate Biplanes. One is powered by an OS .15 FP. This provides adequate power for short takeoffs and to perform all maneuvers with limited vertical paths. My second Ultimate is powered by an OS .25 SF. Take-off is at part throttle (any more power and the plane torques over on take-off). Vertical flight is unlimited with this version.

I also fly a CAP 231 by Ace R/C. It calls for .049 - .09 power. The plane would not fly with the Norvel .061 R/C, so I put an OS .15 FP in it. It flies like it is on a rail, with enough power to do a victory roll on take-off.

I have a wonderful ARF called a Reno Racer 250 by EZ with an OS .25 SF. As I've become more confident with this rocket, I have removed the baffle from the muffler (the added performance was noticeable) and will probably fit it with a tuned pipe. As it is, the wing has a thin airfoil, making this plane very fast.

Recently, I converted two foamy throw gliders to small R/C. The first is the Robart glider. Featured in an article by Nick Ziroli, Senior, the plane is powered by a Norvel .061 R/C, and uses elevon mixing for control... The other plane is the small Comet glider that I mentioned in my previous contribution.

There are three electrics in my collection. One is an ARF high- wing twin, with a three-channel radio. For indoor fun I have a helium-filled electric Blimp that I fly around in my house. The third model is still unassembled: a New Multiplex "Pico-Jet". A friend of mine has one of these, and it flies great.

Regards, Mark

Curtis Mattikow \< 

 \> reports:

Somebody had been asking about the Flying Styro kits. I got the Me109 (27") when it first hit our shores some five years ago. It flew well on rudder and elevator with a Texaco 049. I just hacked off the nose and put on a firewall. Radio access through the cockpit, held on by screws. Plenty of power, but not terribly fighter-like, maneuverability-wise, with only rudder and elevator.

More recently, I acquired three of these kits, completely pre- painted, with Modela CO2 engines installed, for a song on eBay. There are a lot of them floating around at close-out prices. There are unpainted, unassembled versions, with no motor, beauti- fully hand-painted preassembled versions (the camouflage job must be seen...you would be proud to do as well yourself) and several variations in between.

I slapped one together right away with the CO2 motor for free flight. Have not flown that one yet. The second one I fitted with a Golden Bee 049 R/C and four channels. I spent about three hours figuring out how to get all the gear in and still preserve the paint job; and maybe a little less time than that actually executing it.

I took out almost all of the dihedral. I cut away the flaps and used them as ailerons. I made the wing removeable by cutting with a sharp razor at the scale wing fairing line.

The first flight was awesome. I got the thrustline totally wrong -- the first three seconds involved a spectacular save three inches off the pavement while it dove right and down and tried to kill itself.

Once airborne, it took all the trim available to achieve level flight. BUT, the performance was stellar. Four minutes of high- speed fun, and the scale appearance is incredible, with all the right bumps and scoops that make up an Me109.

The second flight was a bad hand launch straight into the ground. It has since been repaired, but the weather has prevented further testing. I have a third kit awaiting whatever refinements found needed by flying the second one.

I next hope to tackle the little 22" Mustang...maybe a G-Mark 03 and three channels? Anybody tried it?

Curtis Mattikow (Heart of New York City)

John Rimmer \< 

 \> contributed:

Roy Clough mentioned a puzzler about overheating a 1500 mAH nicad pack and dumping it in water to cool.

I think he may be on to something. Nicads have an electrolyte of potassium hydroxide/water paste. When overcharging, the water decomposes and becomes steam if the temperature is high enough -- plus hydrogen and oxygen, depending on the type of cell.

I think that in the instance Roy described, the rapid cooling in water eliminated all vapor, and the vent ingested water, which tended to restore the loss during overcharging. This might increase the capacity of the cells if the original KOH paste was low in water content. I am going to try this using an oven at over 100 degrees C. for the heating -- deliberate overcharging to build up heat could cause an explosion, depending on the cell type.

I will use an ISL 8-936 Chameleon Charger by Schulze (sold in the USA by R/C Direct) to check the capacity and voltage before and after heat treating the pack. Will let you know!

While on this subject, check out the web site: \< <http://ww.cc./columbia.edu/~fuat/cuarc/NiCd.htm> \>. This is one of the best papers on Nicads I have read in a while.

John
