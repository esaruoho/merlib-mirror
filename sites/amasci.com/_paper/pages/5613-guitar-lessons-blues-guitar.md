<td width="100%"><h1 id="blues-guitar-lessons">Blues Guitar Lessons</h1>
<a href="101.html"><strong>Basic Blues: 12-bar Blues Theory</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By Olav Torvund</span><br />
This lesson is for beginners and intermediate players. It takes a look at the basic 12-bar blues form. This form is familiar to almost any guitarist. Every guitarist interested in blues, folk, country or rock should know the 12-bar blues by heart; Just switch on the "auto-pilot" and play.
<p><strong><a href="102.html">The Blues Melody and Blues Scale</a></strong><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By Olav Torvund</span><br />
This blues lesson is for intermediate players. Those who want to play blues, should know the blues scale. Guitar players should know how to finger blues scales all the way up the neck in the so called "box position".</p>
<p><a href="103.html"><strong>Blues Notes and Passing Tones, Bending</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By Olav Torvund</span><br />
If you read my previous lesson on the blues scale, you will remember that the blues-tonality is unstable and unsettled, because you play a minor of scale over major chords. You should also remember that one and a half steps up from the root gives you a minor third, and two steps up from the root gives you a major third. I will also remind you of the VII note, where you have the VIIb note in the blues scale, but a VII note in the major scale and in the chords.</p>

<p> </p>
<p><a href="../../maximum/20020901.html"><strong>Blues Triad Mastery</strong></a><br />
<span class="smauthorname">By Darrin Koltow (01 September 2002)</span><br />
I created BTM because I wanted to learn triads in a way that was fun for both fingers and ears. I wanted something Bluesey. I wanted to play *music* and not a monotonous lesson as boring as cardboard. I couldn't find a lesson like this, so I wrote one.</p>
<p><strong><a href="19950104.html">Blues Feeling</a></strong><br />
This is another intermediate lesson. The goal of this lesson is to show you how to get a good blues feeling. Don't think about it. Just pretend you are Jimi Hendrix and play.<br />
</p>
<p><a href="19950105.html"><strong>One-Chord Booogie Grooves</strong></a><br />
Blues has always been a stylistic sponge, soaking up the sounds of other, more popular musical trends and incorporating them into its timeless framework. Over the years elements of rock, Latin and funk have all been added to the blues vocabulary. Of course, the influence goes in the other direction too, some times in ways that are less obvious.</p>
<p><a href="19950106.html"><strong>Blues Turnarounds</strong></a><br />
These turnarounds are mainly in E, but I've put in a few in other keys. They are just a collection of things I've picked up over the years. I'd be very interested in any others anyone's got.</p>
<p><a href="19950107.html"><strong>Introduction to the Texas Shuffle<br />
</strong></a>You can read theory and play scales all day long, but without hearing (and playing) how they are applied in a practical sense, their value is greatly diminished. This lesson is an introduction to the Texas shuffle, with apologies to SRV.</p>
