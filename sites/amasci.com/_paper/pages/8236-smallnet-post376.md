Sam Brauer \< <SBrauerBCC@aol.com> \> reports:

This past Sunday I went to an indoor meet at Glastonbury High School in Connecticut. Yes, it was a contest, but most people were there flying for fun. Anyhow, I've been fooling around with 2 airplanes that I think may be of interest to SMALLsters.

First- a Hannan 1911 Nieuport. This is the 22" version; plans are available through Peck Polymers for a few bucks. The original was powered by a Cox 010 but it required a throttle restrictor because the little Tee Dee puts out way too much power.

I'm flying one with a HiLine Micro 4, a 4-inch prop, and a 2-cell 110 mAh nicad. So far it hasn't been wildly successful; the plans called for no downthrust, but that hasn't worked for me. On the other hand, if built lightly, I think this combination will have enough power for some gentle flying at low levels. The airplane isn't climbing strongly, but it's got enough to stay airborne.

I've been flying it left, but I may try right -- it has a tendency to spiral. It's certainly suitable for indoor flying; it doesn't do well in wind.

The second plane is a peanut: a Walt Mooney-designed Ole Tiger. I have a confession to make: I built this airplane to compete in the FAC Goodyear event. However, it's one of the best-flying peanuts I've got, and is making other people take notice.

I'm one of those folks who hates flying peanuts. Some people have the knack of making the small stuff fly; well, I'm not one of them. These airplanes are wonderfully appealing: minimal building space requirements, materials, and they're so easy to transport.

But making them fly is something else again. After many years it's finally dawned on me that the standard advice of what are easy airplanes to fly doesn't work well. Translation: Piper Cubs don't make good peanuts. Yes, I know, Fikes and Lacey's do, but they're too ugly and they can be hard to trim: no dihedral.

Anyhow, most of the problem with peanuts is in the wing. In the small sizes of a peanut \[13" maximum wingspan\], the relatively high aspect ratio of a Cub doesn't work well -- but a somewhat lower aspect ratio does. I have a Stinson 125 from the Micro-X kit that flies fine -- although it's a little fast and jerky for my taste.

In contrast to the standard advice to build a Cub or similar, the Goodyear racers have very low aspect ratios which work well for "peanut" flying. Everybody who built one was surprised at how well they flew. However, Walt Mooney used what must have been a scale (or close to it) airfoil on the Ole Tiger and I decided that wasn't exactly what I was looking for. So I used an ILAR (it looks about right) airfoil that's flat bottom and about three times as thick instead.

I'm flying the airplane with a Peck silver 4.5 inch prop unmodified (FAC rules) and the motor peg is located under the leading edge of the stabilizer. These airplanes have long noses and plenty of weight up front, so a peg further forward needed tailweight.

The airplane flies very well. My best flight so far was 40 seconds which was a trim flight without full turns. My next flight, where I put some more turns in, was very rudely interrupted by a basket- ball backboard. In the ensuing argument the backboard won, and I had some rebuilding to do -- but I'll bet this airplane will push a minute.

Furthermore, Ole Tiger flies in a stable realistic manner. Goodyear racers aren't exactly slow, but this one's not too fast. With that small prop up front, the tail surfaces shown on the plans work, and its been very easy to trim.

One of the annoying things about Goodyear racers are the cheek cowls which give the airplanes a lot of character. I came up with a quick and dirty method of making them.

I started with some Crayola modeling clay. This clay comes in lots of colors, and is easy to form right out of the package. After you form it into a shape and let it harden for a day or so, you end up with a mold that you can use. Bear in mind that this stuff is pretty elastic, so the shapes are going to be approximate; but I build for fun, not with a ruler.

Anyhow, once your form is hardened, cover the surface with Reynolds wrap or some other plastic wrap. Then I used some medium-weight Silkspan soaked in Titebond thinned with water, and wrapped two layers over the form. When dry, I took some DHP filler, applied it, and sanded it smooth. It's reasonably light- weight (I suspect it's lighter than balsa) and easier than trying to carve and hollow out blocks. It's also pretty rugged -- the "molded Silkspan" dents but doesn't shatter.

<span id="153">Source: Plans, Peanuts\
</span> Anyhow, if you've been frustrated flying peanuts before, go out and try one of these racers. Dave Livesay's site: \< <http://members.nbci.com/dlivesay/plans.html> \> has lots of plans to download.

Sam Brauer Norwalk, Connecticut

Linda \< <alk@esper.com> \> inquires:

Very interested in learning how to use the balloon tanks mentioned. The idea crossed my mind years ago but I figured that it wouldn't work. How do they hook up, and specifically what type of balloons are used -- and who sells them?

Is this method used for both glow and diesel? Thanks for any information on this! Linda

...Linda, in recent Postings a rather complete description of balloon tank use appeared, mostly from Roy Clough and Larry Renger. Here's a brief summary.

The balloons are "helium quality", available at party supply dealers & the like. They must be rinsed out inside before use as model fuel tanks, because they contain a talcum powder coating to prevent the balloons from sticking together internally.

Balloons can ONLY be used with glow fuel. Diesel fuel rots them, although one SMALLster recently claimed to have flown for quite a while with a diesel and a balloon tank.

You can use a stopper from a Sullivan tank as an attachment for the balloon. Only ONE tube passes through. The fuel feed line should be soft plastic (to avoid damaging the balloon, of course) and must have a V notch cut in the feed end, to prevent the balloon material from sealing off flow to the tube.

Attach the balloon to the stopper with several turns of nylon or other strong heavy-duty thread. Not much pressure is needed -- just enough to insure a leakproof seal. (Lots of balloon tank users tie their balloon necks with rubber bands. I don't -- those have an evil habit of breaking...)

To fuel the tank the first time, you need to suck out all the air from inside. One way is to partly fill the tank with fuel; then hold it so that the tubing end is in the air bubble; and suck out the air by pulling back on the fueling syringe.

Yes, you MUST use a fueling syringe! Otherwise, you lack an accurate idea of how much fuel goes into the tank. (I have to admit that for quite a while I pumped up my balloon tanks "by eye" -- and got away with it MOST of the time...) \[JW\]

Mike Swanson \< <mike635@webtv.net> \> wrote in a "personal query" that his Great Planes Laser Incidence meter causes warps on small models when he tries checking those with it. In case this topic is of interest to other SMALLsters, here's an edited copy of my reply:

Thanks for your query. You're right: nothing on incidence meters has ever been published in SMALLnet. That may be because they're not in common use. I know that they seem like a good idea -- but I've been building & flying small model airplanes since 1937, and have NEVER used an incidence meter.

That's not because incidence lacks importance; it's just that there's another way to establish it.

With very few exceptions, all MODEL AIRPLANES are constructed from plans. (I capitalized those two words to indicate that I'm not talking about mass-produced flying toys.) Anyway, the plans provide an easy way of measuring incidence. By the way, the fuselage centerline has no necessary importance here. The "zero incidence datum line" is the centerline of the STABILIZER. (Just wanted to clarify that point, if it needed clarification.)

Now, if you're concerned with the incidence of "flying toys" (which can be a lot of fun to fly, I admit -- many's the happy afternoon I've spent tossing some of Jim Walker's famous "A-J 74" all-balsa gliders) -- again we don't need incidence meters. We establish "optimum incidence" when necessary, by trial and error adjustment.

That is, when the incidence is NOT optimum, it isn't far off; and watching the flight path tells us where change is advisable. Of course, that takes experience. But obtaining that experience is part of the challenge of model flight...

Having said all that, there IS a way to check the incidence of a small model's wing. Fundamentally it requires placing a "reference straightedge" (such as a straight, stiff balsa stick) on the stabilizer, parallel in top view with the fuselage centerline. Then you gingerly measure (with something like a steel 6" or 12" machinist's scale) the height of the CENTER of the wing's leading and trailing edges above this "reference straightedge".

To shorten the mathematical formula, let's call the measured heights HL and HT, for leading and trailing edge respectively. The wing CHORD across the locations where the measurements were taken = C.

Using a scientific calculator, enter HL; subtract HT; divide by C. The result is equal to both the sine and tangent (alike at small angles) of the incidence angle. Use the "sin-1" or "tan-1" function of the calculator to display that angle.

"Real Life Example": On my "Redstart" biplane, the top wing measurements are HL = 1.44; HT = 1.24; C = 2.63. (Yes, it's a mighty small airplane.)

1.44 - 1.24 = 0.20; dividing that by 2.63 = .076 -- which is the sine of 4.36 degrees. (Or 4 degrees 21 minutes.)

Now for the BOTTOM wing. That requires measuring "downward", but I cheated by putting the airplane upside down. HL =1.14; HT = 1.31; C is still 2.63. Aha! Now HT is MORE than HL, because I'm measuring downward instead of upwards. (This is what you'd do on a low-wing monoplane, also.)

That doesn't change the calculations, though, because the difference in height is what matters. 1.14 - 1.31 = - 0.17. Dividing by 2.63 again, we get the sine of the lower wing's incidence angle, which is .0646 -- (changing from minus to plus doesn't matter here) -- and that's the sine of 3.7 degrees.

OK: my little Redstart has its top wing at + 4.36 degrees and the lower wing at - 3.7 degrees. That means there's only about 0.7 degrees angle of incidence DIFFERENCE (called "decalage") between the two wings. That's rather low for a biplane with staggered wings -- but they're only staggered slightly; less than half a chord. And that's what it took to make the Redstart fly the way I wanted it to.

By the way, all these measurements had no relation whatever to the "fuselage centerline". On the Redstart that appears to be noticeably "downhill" with respect to the stabilizer. That's because I designed the model (it's a rubber-powered free flight) with built-in downthrust...

We model designers have a BAGFUL of tricks, even if some of us don't own an incidence meter... \[JW\]
