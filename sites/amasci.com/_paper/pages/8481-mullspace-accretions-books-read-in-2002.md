## 2002 Books

\
\

The following is a list of the books I read in 2002. Short comments are included where I felt them worthwhile. Since I am posting it long after the fact, many of the books have comments written much later, rather than as I read them. Many books are not in chronological order, but clustered with other books by the same author, since that jogged my memory, better. I also read several magazines and books on writing, HTML, CSS, crypto, screenwriting, game development, graphics, dictionaries, thesari, art books and other stuff, but I've forgotten which ones or decided not to include them for various reasons. Sorry.

\
\

### Completed

*The Jehova Contract*, by Victor Koman. Hard-boiled political-social-religious Science-Fiction/Fantasy? Loopy Anarchism.

*Neuromancer*, by William Gibson. **The** book which established cyberpunk. Gibson is a god, what more to say?

*Last Call*, by Tim Powers. Excellent and strange tale of Poker and the Fisher King. Kind of a theme of Powers' but wonderfully weird, even where it nearly becomes repulsive. Powers has a great touch. First Powers I ever read, recommended by my friend Jason Wood.

*Hit List* by Lawrence Block. Loved it. I adore Keller! Funny, twisted, good fun. I call it the "anti-noir" because it really *ought* to be dark and moody, but it's more fun than that. The sort of light-touch oddity Block does so very well. Second of two, but I read this one, first.

*The Drawing of the Dark*, by Tim Powers. Excellent historical and weapons research and a genuinely good, urban fantasy with the best-written sword fight ever. Wonderful.

The *His Dark Materials Trilogy*, by Philip Pullman. Interesting, thought-provoking, but, uneven and occasionally preachy YA series.

*Johnny and the Bomb*, by Terry Pratchett. For middle-school and "transitional" readers, but laugh-your-ass-off funny for almost any age. I love the Johnny books, though they are hard to get, here, for a reasonable price.

*Mort*, by Terry Pratchett. Umm... discworld. There y'go.

Two Anne Perry William Monk books (*A Breach of Promise* and *Twisted Root*). Series almost lost me with these two. Far too much emphasis on the secondary characters and not enough on Monk, also, too much of Perry's favorite motives and devices. Saved by a good twist in *Breach*, but predictable and annoying with *Root*.

*Eleven Days*, by Donald Harstad. Police procedural, first in an excellent series. Enjoyable and I've continued to follow Harstad's books ever since. One of the many wonderful rec.arts.mystery writers.

*Uncharted Territory*, by Connie Willis. Fun, Novella-length, light Science Fiction by another of my personal writing-gods. I love Willis.

*Passage*, by Connie Willis. What did I tell you? Excellently-written and occasionally disturbing novel about a researcher of Near Death Experiences. Terrific stuff, though it's not my favorite CW book and the end wasn't as much of a nailer for me as some of her previous books.

*Dog Land*, by Will Shetterly. Odd "transitional" book about a boy coming to grips with racism and personal identity in the South. Touches of strange magic and wandering gods. Not bad, but not what I was expecting.

*The Difference Engine*, by William Gibson and Bruce Sterling. (yuck) Really disappointing. Waded through it and didn't enjoy it. Not a big fan of Sterling and there wasn't enough of Gibson in evidence for my taste. Disjointed and messy.

*The Grand Ellipse*, by Poula Volsky. Strange, but I really liked it. Planning to read more Volsky when I have the chance.

*American Gods*, by Neal Gaiman. Flawed, but mostly enjoyable. Wandered a bit, but mostly good. Gaiman seems to be still feeling his way a bit.

*Cryptonomicon*, by Neil Stephenson (re-read)

*The Tipping Point*, by Malcolm Gladwell. Great non-fiction about what causes social trends. Enjoyable and interesting.

*Partners in Crime* and *A Cast of Killers*, by Gallagher Grey (pseudonym of Katy Munger). Not bad, but not as good as her later books. Nothing special, alas.

*Out of Time*, *Money to Burn* and *Bad to the Bone*, by Katy Munger. Second, third and, alas, last of the Casey Jones series. Fun, well-paced and enjoyable "tart-noir". I miss Casey already.

*To Visit the Queen*, by Diane Duane. Cat wizards!

*Flu: The Story of the Great Influenza Pandemic of 1918 and the Search for the Virus That Caused It*, by Gina Bari Kolata. Interesting scientific stuff and reasonably written, though with a few flaws and dated by the time I got to it.

*Blindsighted*, by Karin Slaughter. Sometimes-gruesome forensic thriller which kept me engaged the whole time. I generally find forensic books dull and talky, but this one nailed me. I *love* this book, even thought it's really grim in parts. Another of the wonderful rec.arts.mystery writers. (No, I don't recommend these guys because I know them. They are just really good and nice folks, too.)

*Slaves of Obsession*, by Anne Perry. Except for some decent research into the American Civil War (though a little too much revisionism, here and there), this book almost put the end to my reading this series. Thin plotting and a lot of pretty, but unnecessary padding.

*Sir Apropos of Nothing* by Peter David. Disappointing. Should have been funny and mostly just missed the mark. Too cute, too punny and yet, often grim and unattractive without really getting anywhere worth going.

*From the Dust Returned*, by Ray Bradbury. Wonderful. A perfect Halloween book from a master writer.

*Only You Can Save Mankind!*, by Terry Pratchett. The first of the Johnny books, a very funny and a surprising anti-war story. Fun and touching and a really fast read.

*Known Dead*, by Donald Harstad. Good but not as good as *Eleven Days*. Still, that's like saying "well, triple chocolate cake is pretty good, if you like that sort of thing."

*Hit Man*, by Lawrence Block. First of the two Keller books. Not as smooth or as cohesive as *Hit List*, since it was built up from a collection of short stories and bridges, but still, very entertaining tales of Keller, the thoughtful assassin (and his unfaithful dog!).

*The Last Good Kiss*, by James Crumley. Old-fashioned, hard-boiled detective noir from the early 70s. Harsh, weird, sometimes bizarre and drug-soaked, but damned good stuff if you're a noir-fan (like me!)

*The Discovery of Chocolate*, by James Runcie. Odd and sensual tale mixing star-crossed love, history, adventure and chocolate. Tasty nibbling and a quick read.

*Bridge of Birds*, by Barry Hughart. Wonderful, fairy-tale quality Fantasy of "an ancient China which never was, but should have been". Delightful. Everything Fantasy ought to be and very, very satisfying. (Must find more of his stuff, but the guy only writes one book every 8 years or so.)

\

### Started and stopped

*Unnatural Fire*, by Fidelis Morgan (not in the right "head" for it, got to it later, see 2003.)

*Damned Lies and Statistics*, by Joel Best (Still picking a bit at a time. Math overwhelms me, once in a while, though it is not a heavy-math book, really.)

*The Pleasure of Finding Things Out*, by Richard Feynman (nibbling a very small bite at a time: very dense with ideas and complex concepts.)
