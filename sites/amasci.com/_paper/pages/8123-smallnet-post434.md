<div class="Joe">

...Quite right, Dan: Not much is obvious in propeller aerodynamics. For example, the fundamental thrust equations involve diameter and rpm -- but NOT the number of blades.

Check the SMALLnet web site for my article on propellers.

3- and 4-bladed propellers are used on full-scale aircraft to absorb the power of the engine without having to make the landing gear excessively long. The more blades, the lower the propeller's efficiency -- but a net gain in thrust usually results because of the added power.

For your purposes, if you choose a 3-bladed propeller with the same pitch as the 2-blader that flies your airplane best, and allows the engine to turn the same rpm at maximum throttle as it does with the 2-blade prop, your model should fly acceptably. The propulsive efficiency will be less, but model planes are usually grossly overpowered anyway. \[JW\]

My Astro 110D pot went bad, again. The first one was replaced by AstroFlite, free -- but I wanted to fix this one myself. Removed the original pot from the circuit board, soldered three color-coded wires to the board; fished them out through the original pot hole; reassembled the charger; and soldered on a metal, wire-wound 5K pot (1 turn linear); and stuck the pot to the face of the charger with servo tape for a test run - SMOOTH AS SILK!

The original pots are inexpensive carbon ones that don't hold up to constant adjustments. The metal wire-wound ones should last forever. I got mine at a local electronics surplus shop, but they are available at Fry's or Radio Shack (the RS \# is 271-1714 5K 1/2W).

There is room on the face plate (at the bottom) to drill a new hole for the pot. Just make the wires long enough to reach.

Get that old charger out; it is still good...

Ron Fikes, Palo Alto, California

I thought I'd tell the gang about a new airplane that I just flew: a FunX-E. For those folks who lament the passing of the Ace Pacer, may I suggest the FunX-E? This airplane is basically a 1/2A-size Phoenix 7/8 (for those of us who remember Don Lowe's design).

The kit is made by J.M. Glascraft \< <http://www.jmglascraft.com> \> and is very nicely done. I got mine with skinned wings, and the fuselage is a great lay-up with kevlar thread for beef. It's very light and had next to no pinholes.

I just got done with my first flight, but it's clear this bird is a keeper. Specifications: wing area around 250 squares - my wings are a little longer than shown on the plans per John Fotiu's (the designer) suggestion. All-up weight is 23 ounces. (Airframe weight sans battery, motor/ESC and prop is 11 ounces.)

I decided to try a Mega 16/15/5 in it, spinning a Cam 7.5 x 4 folder powered by a 10-cell 1100 mAh HE Cell (HE Cell is a manufacturer) NiMH (nickel metal hydride) pack. This setup pulled 15.5 amps for 150 watts hot off the charger -- no cycling as yet. (A touch over the magic 100 watts/pound -- might need more though -- I'll probably start fiddling soon.) These HE cells have begun replacing the 600 AE nicad -- only a nudge heavier, and slightly more power with 2 times the duration. (Of course, the lithium polymer batteries now coming out seem to blow these out of the water. Now is not a good time to invest in chargers or batteries, the technology is changing too fast.)

Note that my setup is fairly far away from the designer's recommendation: a Speed 480BB or Hacker B40, 7- or 8-cell CP 1300, and a 4.75 x 4.75 prop. (This setup is at least 3.5 ounces heavier, although with 20%+ more power.)

I suspect I'm going to screw around with props. The airplane could probably use a few mph more top end. However, it tracks like it's on rails -- a nice smooth-flying airplane. At 23 ounces it doesn't feel heavy at all.

Landing is something of a challenge. This model is basically a slope-soarer with that folding prop, and it just doesn't slow down. Overshoots are going to be common. I was a little worried about launching; but with a gentle push, it was up and climbing with no strain.

For those of you who miss a Pacer: (I never had one, but flew a buddy's many years ago) I think this airplane fills the void well. I think it will be a better aerobat than most of the existing e-plane designs -- already the rolls are wonderfully axial.

Downsides:

1\. There are no instructions. It's clear this airplane is intended for folks who have a bunch under their belt.

2\. You get to cut out the wood parts. The wood included is very high quality, though.

3\. You'll need one of those laser-cut 400/480 motor mounts.

Summary: Even with the above comments, I still found this model an easy build. I suspect I'm going to fly it a lot. It doesn't look like all the shoulder-wing e-birds out there, such as a Twister, Embat, or E-streak; and I suspect that it's a better pattern airplane -- especially in the right hands (which unfor- tunately don't happen to be mine.)

Sam Brauer Stamford, Connecticut

I have had several questions and comments about lithium cells.

I was at the NEAT fair (U.S. largest electric plane show) in New York. There was lots of talk and information there about lithium batteries.

I had purchased the 800 mAh Qualcoms I mentioned earlier from \< <http://www.allelectronics.com> \>.

Talking with the people at NEAT, I found that All Electronics had quickly sold out of their stock of these batteries, and no one has found a source for them elsewhere. However, I also use their 1200 mAh CAT# BTE-1200 cells at \$3.50 each. These cells when made up into a pack weigh 86 grams -- about the same as a 7-cell nicad 270 pack.

So they will work "OK", but not great, with the with LiteStick-size planes. I use them in mild 280-size applications. With more than 3 ampere loads I never get much more than 800 mAh out of them when testing with an Astro Flight Watt meter. But, for \$7.00 a pack it is a good deal. My Tipsy will fly more than 1 hour with this pack.

However, if you want to spend more, a dramatically better Lithium is on the way.

When at NEAT, I purchased a few Lithium polymer cells. They will soon be available from FMA and Radical R/C. Tiny 150 mAh cells for under 1 ounce. Electrics up to cells of 3,000 mAh that will supply enough amps to fly Speed 400 + size planes.

These cells are both lighter, and supply more amps, than Lithium Ion or Lithium Metal cells.

My new two 560 mAh cells weigh just 26 grams. (Total \$24.00) This is lighter than an equivalent-voltage 50 mAh nicad or 110 mAh NiMH pack!

The cells are flat and could be easily built into a wing. They will put out a sustained 1.5 to 2.0 amps: plenty for the typical GWS Lite Stick type motor.

I also bought 4 1 Amp cells; total weight 80 grams. (Total cost \$52.00) They will be connected 2 cells parallel, and then these 2 in series, for a total of an 8.0-8.4 volt 2,000 mAh pack.

I saw Dave Thacker from Radical RC hovering and doing all kinds of 3D aerobatics for more than 20 minutes using these cells in his Mini-Edge 540 (Speed 300 size).

If you purchase any Lithium cells, remember: you MUST have the proper charger designed for Lithium cells. Your regular Nicad/NiMH charger will quickly destroy Lithium batteries. FMA stated that they will soon have Lithium Ion/polymer chargers available.

An inexpensive charger is also available now from Hobby Lobby and Batteries America. I have also seen Lithium chargers at Best Buy. Also, do not charge much over 1 C.

This is the future of small electrics. Tim Hamel

Frank Weaver \<

I make all my fuel tanks for small applications such as .010 to .061's from K&S brass sheet. They have no moving parts and are great for inverted flight.

The trick is to build a wedge style tank, running the pick-up back into the centre of the wedge, through a baffle. Solder in the baffle to the top and bottom, but just shy of the sides.

The working force here is, I think, a centrifugal effect when the model is in flight. Anyway, it works. Also, you can build custom tanks for any application.

Fly SMALL, Have A BALL, Frank Weaver

AnneMarie Cross's request (for Great Planes) for information on old Top Flite kits reminded me of how angry I got when G-P, on their way to buying out every available model company, gobbled up Top Flite. Gone were all the Flite Streaks, Noblers, Rascals, et al.

I had hoped that all those good old Top Flite kit designs would have been saved for posterity, but no such luck. (My special thanks, though, to all the "cottage industries" that have been helping to keep alive the Top Flite heritage.)

The G-P takeover of Top Flite took place in 1992, I believe. In February 1993 I wrote to George Aldrich, as I had heard that all the original drawings for his Top Flite designs were gone.

Following is George's reply, verbatim:

"You are correct about Great Planes destroying my original drawings of the Nobler, Flite Streak, etc.!! I called them last year trying to get those drawings for the AMA Museum. They didn't know me from a pumpkin - or anything about Control Line! If they decide to get out of the model R/C business, they'll probably open a hock shop in NYC! Same mentality!

"Frank Garcher of Midwest was down at T.F. when G.P. was taking over, and they were walking around on the drawings - which were just thrown on the floor!"

That was the last nail in the coffin for me and R/C. I now fly FF and occasionally C-L, and I keep it SMALL!!

By the bye, does anyone out there see this new crop of electric (ugh) Park Flyers as potential Sport FF models with small "wet motors"? Kudos to the lad who put a throttled Cox .010 in his small R/C thing. Just think: no batteries to lug aloft; no waiting to charge; and no weird, mysterious electric charge-em-up thingie to worry over -- plus some really nice aromas to go along with the affair.

What a shame he didn't go diesel; then he could have gotten rid of batteries altogether!!!

Think SMALL, fly free, and as Don Myers would say: "Stinkers forever!"\
Jim Smith
