<td data-bgcolor="#FFF9F4"><strong>A</strong>n amendment to the U.S. Constitution has been presented to Congress, which states:
<div data-align="center">
<table width="90%" data-cellpadding="7" data-bgcolor="#F3F3EB">
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>“Marriage in the United States shall consist only of the union of a man and a woman. Neither this Constitution or the constitution of any State, nor state or federal law, shall be construed to require that marital status or the legal incidents thereof be conferred upon unmarried couples or groups.”</td>
</tr>
</tbody>

If this becomes law, it would duplicate part of the 1996 “DoMA” law [See our article: <a href="doma.html">DoMA</a>].
<p>Because it is so broadly worded, this amendment could also pave the way for dismantling all other contracts between same- as well for non-married, opposite-sex couples, such as domestic partnership status for workplace benefits, hospital visitations, Civil Unions [See: <a href="d-p-verm.html"><strong>Vermont: Civil Union</strong></a>], etc.</p>
<p>With the term “legal incidents” of marriage, the amendment could destroy a wide range of rights, including state and local civil rights laws prohibiting discrimination based on marital status, protecting elderly couples who refrain from marrying in order to retain pensions, and even laws allowing a person, in the absence of a spouse, to oppose the autopsy of a close friend because of the deceased person’s religious beliefs.</p>
<p>The amendment could prohibit states from expanding civil rights. It could have the effect of forbidding states from serving their traditional role as testing grounds for stronger civil rights laws.</p>
<p>The bill was first presented in 2002 by Rep. Bob Schaffer (R-CO) and Rep. Tom Tancredo (R-CO). Rep. Marilyn Musgrave (R-CO), re-introduced the bill on May 21, 2003. It is now H.J. Res. 56, and known as the “Federal Marriage Amendment” (FMA). As of November 2003, it has 100 co-sponsors. No hearings have been held on the measure.</p>
<p>On November 25, 2003, an amendment bill was introduced in the Senate by Sen. Wayne Allard (Colorado-R), and four Republican co-sponsors.</p>
<p>Until DoMA, the Federal system had never defined who is eligible for legal marriage, that right has always been with the states. The FMA would again place the definition of marriage in the domain of federal law. That shift in governmental power, from the states to the federal government, flies in the face of the right wing’s historical, constant, rallying cry for a smaller, less intrusive government.</p>
<p>The only other Constitutional amendment that took American rights — rather than defending our freedoms — was that of the anti-alcohol law of the Prohibition. That was the 18th Amendment, which was ratified in 1919 and repealed 14 years later by the 21st Amendment in 1933.</p>
<p>No amendment has ever been introduced that took away rights from an entire class of American citizens.</p>

<p>While right-wing extremists have worked for decades to dismantle the rights of same-sex couples to such rights as job benefits and custody for their children, this year’s push for a an anti-marriage Constitutional amendment has been supported by larger numbers than before.</p>
<p>Spurred by the U.S. Supreme Court’s ruling in September 2003 that gay and lesbian Americans shouldn’t be thrown in jail simply because of who they are, people like Jerry Falwell, Senate Majority leader Bill Frist (R-Tennessee), and Senator Rick Santorum (R-Pennsylvania have reacted with the terror that Americans might not share their intolerance. They require an amendment to Constitutionally codify their personal, religious beliefs, and deny civil rights and equal benefits to millions of people.</p>
<p>To amend the Constitution, a bill must be passed by two-thirds of the U.S. House and Senate, as well as being ratified by three-fourths of the state legislatures.</p>
<p>Historically, it has been extremely difficult to pass an amendment. However, anti-marriage laws have already been passed in many state legislatures. [See our <a href="t-line-2.html"><strong>Legislative Reactions to Legal Marriage</strong></a>] And right-wing radicals continue to paint gay and lesbian citizens as being non-human, and not deserving of equal treatment.</p>
<p>While it would be an uphill battle to get an amendment passed, it is not clear how the final vote would run.</p>

<p>Lately, there has been a lot of Web-based petitions, surveys, and polls on this issue. They are not considered scientific data-collecting methods, and are usually not taken seriously by social scientists or politicians. A major reason for this is that the participants cannot be verified as valid individuals.</p>
<p>Web petitions simply do not meet the barest of scientific replication standards. Politicians usually rely on direct communication, such as letters, from constituents, as well as on polls managed by professionals who use telephone and other personal interview techniques.</p>
<p>Internet surveys and petitions are usually designed with the goal of bringing traffic to their owner’s Web site, in order to sell ad space.</p>
<p>Because of these reasons, we encourage the writing of letters to our congressional representatives, rather than try to stuff the Web site survey boxes. And, because of the current germ paranoia, a fax may be better than sending an envelope.</p>
