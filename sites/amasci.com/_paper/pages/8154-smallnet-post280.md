Dr. Mike Hawkins \< 

 \> starts tonight's Posting:

Recent postings queried the use of a pressurised tank with the HB 21 four cycle. The way to do this is to put a brass tube T piece in the fuel tubing between the exhaust manifold and the cylinder head. Take the extra tube to the tank. The vent from the lower crankcase goes overboard. This worked fine for me.

There has been correspondence on the use of small polythene tanks for the Norvel etc. I tried this in the Aquastar and the engine would not run well. On measuring it was 5 inches from the spraybar to the pickup at the back of the tank and it was 3/4 inch lower than the spraybar. A bit much for a small engine I feel. I therefore replaced the polythene tank with a metal tank, 35 mm x 35 mm x 40 mm, made from an old fruit juice can.

Punch holes for two brass tubes before soldering up the tank, one for fuel to the spraybar and for filling from the rear lower corner of the tank; the other for venting to the nipple on the Norvel muffler from the front top of the tank.

Mount the tank on the Aquastar's pylon so the mid line of the tank is level with the spraybar. The engines seem to appreciate it and run well. Successful flight off water today !

Regards, Mike

MVVS AND SMALL DIESELS

Sam Brauer \< 

 \> wrote that he had bought an MVVS engine from the UK. I wonder if the price was any cheaper than buying it from MVVS of America. They have two websites: \< <http://www.mvvs.com> \> and \< <http://www.morrishobbies.com> \>.

In small sizes they have only the .15 diesel, a well-regarded engine that can be selected for side or rear exhaust. The MVVS 60 is also available as a diesel. The head is sold as a separate item, and could be retrofitted, though I would think that it would be better to run an engine exclusively as a diesel if that is its intended destiny since the running clearances would likely be different if it were broken in on hotter-burning glow fuel. (Any opinions from the experts?)

When Czechoslovakia was under Communist control, engines were made with both MVVS and Modela names. MVVS engines were .15 and larger; the two small diesels, .09 and .12, carried the Modela name, though I believe that they were made by MVVS. MVVS of America inquired about obtaining the smaller diesels, but production had ended with the privatization of the MVVS company.

The molds for the .12 (2cc) were modified to enable fitting ball bearings and the exhaust outlet was changed from rectangular to round. There may have been other changes as well. This engine is now available only as a glow. MVVS of America can supply it.

Ed Carlson is the best possibility for obtaining the .09 and .12 in the USA. I believe that a UK supplier also bought up a number of them. I have a couple each of the Modela 2cc (.12) glow and diesel engines available for sale if anyone wants to contact me off-line. These are NIB, left over from my days in Japan.

<div class="Joe">

....Steve, it sounds to me like your trouble is excessive cooling. The glow plug gets too cold and quits igniting the fuel-air mixture. One fix would be to add an "on-board glow igniter", i.e. a battery that keeps the glow plug hot during flight.

But an easier fix might work for you: with heavy-duty aluminum foil, cover up the top couple of fin grooves in your Norvel .15 and try flying it. That should keep the combustion chamber warm enough so that the glow plug continues working. \[JW\]

Andy Woitowicz \< 

 \> talks about engine noise:

In regards to Sandy Frank's plea to quiet down .049s: For several years I've been doing my engine test flying at the local park. All of my .049's have mufflers. The best of these are the units provided by VA. Both the older and new generation VA's are rear exhaust and they use a muffler that is bolted directly to the engine. A very neat, secure set-up. These are certainly quieter than the typical open exhaust .049s, mostly Coxes, that we've run for many years. Cox has offered muffler/exhaust throttle systems in the past but at the loss of much-needed power.

The VA's are more powerful, being modern construction, so some loss in exchange for quieter operation is acceptable, especially for the new Mark 2. I made a dual-chamber muffler with a multitude of baffles. A loss of 1000 RPM was noted with not a really substantial decrease in noise. I don't have a sound meter and my observation was purely subjective but that's the way the public perceives our activities anyway.

I will be making up a two stage muffler like you see on the all-out fun-fly aircraft. They typically use an empty mousse can connected with silicon tubing to the conventional muffler. These run very quiet with a minimum loss of power. In my case I'll be using an empty "magic marker" tube which is about right for 1/2A. This will be permanently attached to the aircraft and will be connected to the regular muffler with silicone tubing. Will keep all posted.

Having said all that, a big factor in the annoyance level of small engines is the RPM or frequency of the noise. As related before, in a previous Posting, high pitched sounds are more irritating regardless of how loud they are. This is because we have been conditioned to respond to high pitched sounds, like the wail of a baby. All baby creatures, humans included, have smaller voice boxes and the sounds emitted are higher pitched by default. Same goes for baby (1/2A) engines.

Joe is on the right track in mentioning diesel. These have a deeper exhaust note, run slower and swing bigger props. Having compared, on the bench, one with the other using a diesel conversion head on VAs and Coxes, it's very clear that diesels have the advantage. If a diesel were designed from the ground up to throttle well and utilize a real, conventional muffler, I think we would have something.

Fortunately we do. The MP Jet .061. This engine was, by all appearances, designed first for diesel operation, and then also offered for glow. I have the glow version and I am impressed with the impeccable quality of construction. The Jet is available with ball bearings and as a bushed engine. It has a superb, complex, very effective, true two-needle throttle that works very well.

It is quite a remarkable product considering the price. I have bought these throttles for experimentation on my other engines and paid only 12 dollars each. They are not easy to adapt, but they are very effective in throttling some of my more difficult engines.

And, the whole point of this piece, the MP Jet engine is side exhaust with a conventional muffler. I have not run the engine on diesel, but plan to do so. Is there anyone who has experience with this engine regarding diesel and noise/muffling? What fuel, what props, what RPM???

Regards to all. Andy

....Andy, send for Ed Carlson's catalog. That contains a complete performance report on the MP Jet diesel (ball bearing) -- plus a 2-page instruction sheet on model diesel operation, including fuel recommendations (the MP Jet calls for 40% castor oil for break-in; 35% for flying).

BTW, all PAW's are "designed from the ground up" for diesel operation. PAW makes no glow engines that I know of! Their throttles work just fine for the people I know who fly with them.

My own PAW's, from the .03 up to the biggest I own: .19 -- run smoothly & responsively from about 2500 rpm to maximum. Of course, I fly with bigger props than most do: 6-3 on my .03; 7-3 or 7-3 1/2 on the .06's; and 10-4 or 10-5 on the .19's.

I make only one major change to the PAW throttle: I solder the throttle arm to the barrel. That eliminates the problem of the spraybar rotating out of its proper orientation. \[JW\]

Rick Ramalho \< 

 \> wonders:

Being very new to the R/C Airplane scene, I am curious as to what is the preferred way of configuring the transmitter for 3-channel operation? More specifically, for an airplane without ailerons, do you put the rudder control on the left stick, and fly it as a rudder, or do you place it on the right stick, and pretend it is acting like the ailerons?

Thanks. Rick

<div class="Joe">

....Rick, the usual setup puts the rudder on the right stick. You don't have to pretend the action is like ailerons: it IS, at least on models with dihedral. The reason for that is that as soon as rudder deflection causes the airplane's tail to move sideways, its wings meet the air at different angles of attack.

For example, with a right rudder deflection, the tail of the air- craft yaws to the left. Since the plane is still moving forward, that motion in conjunction with the wing's dihedral makes the left wing meet the air at a greater angle than that of the right wing. The resulting action is exactly like ailerons provide: the left wing rises; the right wing drops; and a banked right turn results.

With proper wing dihedral, every turn of an aileron-less R/C model in flight will be correctly "coordinated": no slipping; no skidding. That's one big reason for the popularity of 3-channel R/C for training beginners. \[JW\]

</div>

<div class="Joe">

....Some discussion of CO2 motor operation arose from infor- mation furnished in a recent Posting. Here's more on that subject:

As I tried to convey in my reply to Affonso, a CO2 motor is just another kind of "steam engine". It operates on the pressure developed when heat expands a fluid into gas.

The reason a CO2 motor doesn't work as well in cold weather as it does when it's hot is the reduced pressure developed in the "expansion chamber" above the motor's piston. Tank pressure has comparatively little effect. The QUANTITY of CO2 (preferably in liquid form!) IS important; but the pressure developed in the tank hardly affects what happens in the motor, because it's the expansion of the gas above the piston that does the work.

The fins on a CO2 motor's cylinder are for WARMING, not cooling, as they are for internal combustion piston-pushing power providers.

In humid weather frost condenses on these fins. That acts some- what like an insulator, keeping the cylinder temperature low, and thus reducing the gas expansion.

CO2's run best and longest when:

1.  \<;i\> The motor's tank is COLD, and filled with liquid CO2;

    \<;i\> The connecting tubing and motor cylinder receive plenty of warm, non-humid airflow during operation.

    \<;i\> The propeller provides PLENTY of torque load! (That maximizes the time between piston strokes, and thus allows maximum "warming" to occur above the piston -- which is the only place that added heat will augment the NET power output of the "system".)

It goes without saying that the 800 psig pressure value for CO2 capsules/bulk tanks is a "nominal number". The actual value varies, especially in a small capsule that's been partially depleted.

It is POSSIBLE to pressurize a "bulk tank" much higher than 800 psig. A few years ago Steve Staples had his bulk CO2 tank mistakenly over-pressurized to 3000 psig! The eventual results of that were mighty unpleasant. However, the overcharge was an accident, and the only one of its kind I've ever heard of in 50+ years of off-and-on CO2 power activity.

There's a new and reportedly extremely efficient CO2 motor on the market, which uses a dual tank arrangement. One tank is filled with liquid CO2; the outflow from that passes into a second tank which acts as a "gasifier"; and ITS output powers the motor. \[JW\]

</div>
