In response to Richard Ranney's experience with the Mini Sukhoi from MAN plans, I had the same problems he encountered.

First, re-doing the plan was more work that building the model. I had to re-plot every rib, because as shown, not one was right. And yes, it did need washout in the wing tips. It was a great flyer once sorted out, and met its ultimate fate knocking over a pop can with the rudder -- inverted...

I have been playing around with my new FMA Super Nova charger, and all I can say is, it's the greatest model product I have owned! It is a far more valuable tool toward enjoying the hobby than a computer radio. The only problem I found was the instructions.

If you have one of these units, put the instructions in file 13, set it up on your bench and play with it. Beep and boop around in the programs and you will have it down in 10 minutes and never have to look at the instructions again.

A recent posting noted that the smaller Enya engines are not available in the USA. This is indeed a pity, as Enya's small range is quite wide, with .049, .06, .06 diesel, .08 , .09, .11 and .15 sizes. Ideal for SMALLsters.

The good news is that these engines are certainly still available in Japan and the Far East. For instance Hobby Shack in Singapore \< 

 \> supplies them. You just have to hunt to find them.

The smaller 4 engines are rather quaint-looking, looking like OK Cubs on steroids. However, in spite of their vintage looks, they come with effective throttles and silencers, and are extremely robust and quite powerful.

They produce their peak torque at lower rpm and are more suited to relaxed and vintage flying than most of the 1/2A engines currently available which are rather more high-revving.

The only Enya Diesel in the range is of particular interest. My Enya .06 R/C diesel compares very favourably with my PAW 06 R/C, especially on larger prop sizes. In my opinion, my Enya throttles better. The PAW definitely out-performs it on smaller, faster planes. But the Enya lugs the larger ones more realistically.

The .09, .11 and .15 are more conventional-looking, and are powerful and very consistent. The .11CX in particular must be now the hottest in its class, as the Webra 11 Speed and Super Tigre 11 are no longer available. This one is a real screamer.

Enyas sometimes require extended run-in for peak performance and easy starting. Especially if you get one that's been set up a bit tight. But once the running-in is complete, I have found the small Enyas to be extremely consistent and reliable-performing, behaving just like easy-starting, larger-capacity quality glow engines.

My 4 small Enyas have had many seasons use, almost always being started by hand with just 2 or 3 flicks.

Raj, Singapore

Sam Brauer \<

I thought I'd write about some of the new electric motors I've been playing with. I've picked up two of the new Mega brushless "Speed 400 sized" motors -- the 5-turn motor (16/15/5) and a motor which is labeled as a 7-turn motor (16/15/7) but really seems to be closer to the 8-turn motor. The number of turns is confusing, but it's very important to figure out what to use for your particular application.

Low turns equates to high rpms and high amperages: i.e. "perfor- mance motors". Higher turns equates to lower amperages, somewhat higher cell counts, and good efficiency swinging a large prop at lower power levels: i.e. "cruising motors".

Dimensionally and weight-wise, these motors are drop-in replace- ments for a Speed 400. (The number of turns affects the internals only.) The new motors are lighter than and less demanding in amperage than previous "400-sized" brushless or cobalt motors. They are of much better construction than the standard Speed 400 however, with ball bearings and hardened shafts.

I've mounted both motors in an airplane that I've been working at to get fly the way I've wanted: a Hobby Hangar Edge 540. This is a versatile airframe, robust, very maneuverable, with an excellent speed range.

Previously I've had Promax 4014 motors and a Mega 22/10/6 in this airplane, driven by a 7-cell CP 1300 mAh nickel cadmium battery pack. Both motors weighed the same: about 3.5 ounces; and the airplane weighed in at 24 ounces even after I took some of the structure out. Performance was quite good: strong vertical (several vertical rolls); high roll rate; nice tumbles. Slow flight was OK, but the airplane did feel a bit heavy.

The first new motor I tried in the Edge was the Mega 16/15/8, powered by an 8-cell, 1100 AAU nicad pack turning an APC 9 x 4.5 E prop at about 8200 rpm to start. Rpm quickly fell to 7500 though. This combination lightened the airplane by about 3 ounces, to 20.5 ounces total.

Needless to say, with less power and weight, the airplane flew very differently. It was much slower, more susceptible to gusts, and lost some vertical performance. I had trouble pulling through a vertical 8, for example. (It was a bit easier to pull this maneuver with the other power system, but I still had trouble making it look pretty.) On the other hand, slow flight was much improved, the airplane felt "lighter". Also, it didn't pick up speed in a dive as readily.

Note the maneuver I had trouble with: a vertical 8. Many of the so-called "aerobatic" park flyers sold as ARFs can't even loop from level flight using geared Speed 280 BBs or Speed 400s.

I also bench-tested this motor with an 8-cell 600 AE nicad pack, similar to what is used in the ARFs. Rpm are down some, to about 7200 on the 9 x 4.5 inch E prop after 30 seconds -- but that still blows away any of the ferrite motor/gearbox combinations that I know of. Since the 16/15/8 motor reaches its maximum efficiency at 4.5 amps, there's no reason you couldn't run smaller, lighter battery packs as well.

However, I was looking for more performance, so I dropped a 16/15/5 motor in place of the 8-turn motor, and changed to a 6-cell CP 1300 nicad pack. These changes added about half an ounce. Now I had a combination that delivered 8300 rpm on the same 9 x 4.5 E prop off the bat, but still delivered 8100 rpm after a minute of run time. In short, much less power drop-off compared to the previous system. The airplane noticed this: effectively the vertical performance is very close to the higher-power, higher-weight sytems; but the speed is a bit lower (and more scale-like). Slow speed characteristics remain improved as well.

Summary: I'm very pleased with this motor/battery/prop combination. This is a Mega 16/15/5 (no gearbox!) an APC 9 x 4.5 inch prop, and a 6-cell CP 1300 nicad pack, which all together probably weighs about 10 ounces. By using this large prop, the airplane flies in a more scale-like manner than a glow-powered ship (with its tiny, high revving, annoying-sound-output prop), yet probably has even better vertical performance (or at least comparable).

Historically, brushless motors have been very pricey; but the new Mega's are about 2/3rds the cost of an Astro 020 with controller. Essentially, these motors are cost-competitive with an expensive geared ferrite setup, such as the MTM S-300. I think performance- wise they are far superior to the Speed 400s with their annoying gearboxes, and they are far more flexible.

As an example, the 16/15/5 turn motor is a drop-in replacement for a 6V Speed 400 -- or close to it; swings a somewhat larger prop; yet it's also a good replacement for a Multiplex Turbo 450 -- a plain-bearing ferrite motor (now unobtainable) which weighs 2 ounces more. By drop-in replacement, I mean that it takes the same batteries and the same props, and produces a 300 rpm increase (from 10,000 to 10,300) while taking fewer amps than the Turbo 450. Mind you, a Turbo 450 comfortably flew my 28-ounce Buzzard Bombshell. It's clear that you could use the same 16/15/5 motor in a 15-ounce airplane, to a 30-ounce airplane; just change the batteries and the props.

Sam Brauer Stamford, Connecticut

Jim Rundle \< 

 \> contributed:

For transferring plan drawings to balsa I use a spiky wheel with a handle on it from my wife's sewing supplies. I've seen a better version of this, with a swivel head, advertised by Micro Mark as a "pounce wheel". I've seen them in art supply shops too. I don't know what it's called in sewing shops.

You just put the plans over the balsa and run the wheel along the outer edge of the lines; cut along the line of holes; and then touch up with a sanding block. I find this method very quick. The plans wind up perforated, but I've never seen any drawback to that.

For ribs I make a plywood template and cut around it. If there is a flat section of the airfoil, I cut it oversize at that point, and glue a stick to the bottom edge so I can push it flush to the edge of the wood before I cut. It's quick and accurate for constant-chord wings.

The one thing I haven't worked out well is accurate, repeatable spar notches. I've heard of people making up sticks with strips of sandpaper cut to the right width and glued on, and a wider stick on top as a stop to get the depth right. Then they use that to sand in the notches. I haven't tried that yet.

If someone out there has a good method that doesn't require a bandsaw I would like to hear it.

Jim Rundle

Dean Owen \< 

 \> has a different method:

After reading recent SMALLnet postings in which the process of making patterns for model parts from plans was discussed, I thought I'd pass along a technique that I've used for years... and with great success. The process involves the use of old X-Ray film. The only part of the process that involves some preparation is making friends with someone in the health care community...an X-Ray tech is ideal....

I am told that in many hospitals and clinics a couple of blank X-Ray films are run each day to insure that the developing system is functioning properly. These films are then discarded. These are best for pattern-making, because they can used "as is".

If old films with images on them can be obtained, the emulsion can be washed off with bleach. At any rate, once some blank X-Ray film is obtained, the process is one of placing the film over the plan and tracing the part, using a fine "Pilot" brand Photographic marker. This produces an outline on the film in permanent black ink which will not smudge.

The tracing can then be cut out with a pair of scissors and used as a pattern by placing it directly on the balsa or plywood and drawing around the perimeter. The technique is rather nice because:

\(1\) the plans are not destroyed;\
(2) the patterns can be placed in an envelope and saved for later use (repairs or a second model); and\
(3) the X-Ray film is usually free for the asking.....\

Cheers, Dean Owen
