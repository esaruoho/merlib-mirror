<td data-bgcolor="#FFF9F4"><strong>On November 18, 2003, the Massachusetts Supreme Court ruled that same-sex couples are legally entitled to civil marriage under the Massachusetts Constitution. The 4-3 ruling did not order licenses to be issued to the couples who challenged the law. Instead, the court ordered the Legislature to change the civil marriage statutes within 180 days — on May 17, 2004.</strong>

<td data-bgcolor="#F4F5F7"><strong>Massachusetts Court Finding Excerpt</strong></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td data-bgcolor="#FAFAFA">“The question before us is whether, consistent with the Massachusetts Constitution, the commonwealth may deny the protections, benefits and obligations conferred by civil marriage to two individuals of the same sex who wish to marry. We conclude that it may not. The Massachusetts Constitution affirms the dignity and equality of all individuals. It forbids the creation of second-class citizens. In reaching our conclusion we have given full deference to the arguments made by the commonwealth. But it has failed to identify any constitutionally adequate reason for denying civil marriage to same-sex couples.”<br />

The Legislature has no legal authority to legislate away the court’s ruling that it is a constitutional right to marry the person of one’s choice. It may not attempt to re-write discrimination into the marriage laws.
<p>Nor can the Massachusetts state constitution be amended in that length of time. An amendment would need to be approved by the Constitutional Convention (the combined House and Senate) in two successive legislative sessions and then ratified by the public. The soonest an amendment could be brought to the public for a vote would be November 2006.</p>
<p>Further, recent polling suggests that the public is not interested in undermining the Court’s ruling:</p>

Massachusetts is the fourth U.S. state to have a high court rule in favor of legal marriage for same-sex couples. Following the court findings, Hawaii and Alaska altered their constitutions to prevent equal treatment of same-sex couples. And Vermont created a separate-but-not-equal domestic partner status called “Civil Unions.”
<p>In spite of the public sentiment in favor of legal marriage, the Massachusetts Senate gave its initial approval to a bill on December 11, 2003, that would allow same-sex couples to receive the same benefits and rights as married couples without labeling their unions as “marriages.”</p>
<p>Senators also adopted an order asking the Supreme Judicial Court if Civil Unions would satisfy its ruling that a ban on same-sex marriage is unconstitutional.</p>
<p>The House also prepared an anti-marriage bill, however, put it on hold until hearing from the Court. The House version goes further than the Senate’s in that it also contain a provision that explicitly defines marriage as the union of one man and one woman, basically, yet another anti-gay measure.</p>
<p>The Massachusetts high court ruled in their advisory opinion on February 4, 2004, that only full, equal marriage for same-sex couples — <em>not</em> Civil Unions — would meet the edict of its November decision. It should be clear to even the Massachusetts legislature that same-sex marriages will take place, as originally stated by the court, in May 2004.</p>
<p>“The history of our nation has demonstrated that separate is seldom, if ever, equal,” wrote the four justices who ruled in favor of marriage. Offering civil unions rather than full, legal marriage makes for an “unconstitutional, inferior, and discriminatory status for same-sex couples,” the court said.</p>
<p>On February 9, 2004, AFL-CIO of Massachusetts president Robert J. Haynes sent a letter to the legislature urging lawmakers to defeat the proposed amendment banning gay marriage. The union has 400,000 members.</p>
<p>The House and Senate met in a Constitutional Convention on February 11, 2004, to consider two proposed state constitutional amendments that would ban same-sex marriage. One of them contained a proposal for Civil Unions. While some lawmakers hoped to write marriage discrimination into the constitution, and overturn the court ruling, both amendments narrowly failed.</p>
<p>In arguing against the state constitutional marriage ban, Senator Dianne Wilkerson (D-Boston) related her experience as a black woman raised in Arkansas, where the hospital did not allow her mother to deliver her children. Sen. Wilkerson said:</p>
<ul>
<li>“I know the pain of being less than equal and I cannot and will not impose that status on anyone else. I was but one generation removed from an existence in slavery. I could not in good conscience ever vote to send anyone to that place from which my family fled.”</li>
</ul>
<br />
On February 12, 2004, after two days of tense negotiations, three amendments met a slim defeat, and the Massachusetts Legislature suspended debate on the proposed ban.
<p>Meeting again to debate placing prejudice into the Massachusetts constitution, lawmakers met on March 11 and 12, 2004. This time, they voted three times to ban same-sex marriage and establish Civil Unions with “entirely the same benefits, protections, rights, and responsibilities” of marriage.</p>
<p>The House and Senate resumed their Constitutional Convention on March 29, 2004. The Legislature adopted a new version of a state constitutional amendment that would ban gay marriage and legalize civil unions, eliminating consideration of any other proposed changes. The revised version would ask voters to simultaneously ban same-sex marriage and legalize Civil Unions, rather than taking those steps separately. It clarifies that civil unions would not grant federal benefits to same-sex couples.</p>
<p>The Legislature must still take two more votes in the 2005-2006 legislative session for further consideration before going to the voters in the fall of 2006.</p>
<p>Massachusetts Governor Mitt Romney told reporters he would ask the state’s highest court to block the pending same-sex marriages until the amendment process has run its lengthy course. However, Attorney General Tom Reilly, whose job it is to represent the state in court, said he would not seek the delay on Romney’s behalf.</p>

Four of the couples have young children; others have faced health crises. All are concerned about providing the security for each other and their families that automatically comes with a marriage license.</p>

The Massachusetts trial court had ruled against the seven same-sex couples on May 2002, concluding that having and raising children is central to the purpose of marriage, despite acknowledging that four of the seven couples in the case have children, and that Massachusetts law allows same-sex couples to jointly adopt.
<p>Litigant Gina Smith:</p>
<ul>
<li>“The decision is particularly troubling because Heidi and I, like many of our gay and lesbian neighbors, have children. We feel strongly that our sons deserve the same level of protection that other children in the state enjoy.”</li>
</ul>

<td data-bgcolor="#F4F5F7"><strong>Massachusetts Marriage License Process</strong></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td data-bgcolor="#FAFAFA"><ul>
<li>Apply in person at any city or town hall for a “Notice of Intention of Marriage”</li>
<li>Offer your Social Security numbers</li>
<li>No residency requirement</li>
<li>Pay the fee ($4-50)</li>
<li>Be 18 or older (or else have a judge’s permission to marry younger)<br />
    (birth certificate may be required )</li>
<li>Not be married to anyone else (a divorce must be final at time of application)</li>
<li>*Not be closely related by blood or kinship to their intended spouse</li>
<li><strong>**May not marry if marriage is forbidden in jurisdiction where applicant resides</strong></li>
<li>Have a blood test showing they do not have communicable syphilis<br />
    (once found non-infectious, a marriage license may be obtained)</li>
<li>Three-day wait period (unless waived) before license granted<br />
    (weekends and holidays included, but the day the application is<br />
    made is not; apply on Friday, license issued on Monday.)</li>
<li>Marriage must be “solemnized” (i.e., have a ceremony)</li>
<li>“Solemnizing” must be done within 60 days of filing the notice of intention of marriage</li>
<li>The notice may be used in any city or town within the Commonwealth</li>
<li>Marriages may be “solemnized” by justices-of-the-peace, or religious officials<br />
    (out-of-state clergy must receive permission from the Secretary of the Commonwealth)</li>
<li>Also, any person may apply to conduct a marriage ceremony in one place, on one day for one couple (a $25 permit)</li>
<li>No witnesses required to “solemnize” license</li>

<p><strong>Federal Recognition</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>None.</strong> The Federal system will <em>not</em> recognize a same-sex marriage because of the <a href="doma.html"><strong>DoMA</strong></a> law. You will <em>not</em> be able to file a joint tax return, sponsor a partner for immigration, receive social security widow benefits, etc. [See our article: <a href="mar-fed.html"><strong>U.S. Federal Laws for the Legally Married</strong></a>.]</li>
</ul>
<strong>Non-Massachusetts Residents</strong>
<ul>
<li><p>Couples who apply for a civil marriage license must complete a “Notice of Intention of Marriage’ form that says in part: “We/I hereby state that there is an absence of any legal impediment to the marriage.” Should a couple reside outside of Massachusetts, the licensing officer may require an affidavit declaring that marriage is <em>not</em> prohibited by the laws where the couple resides. 40 states have laws specifically denying legal marriage to same-sex couples. And supplying proof that you would <em>not</em> be contravening the laws of your state could prove impossible to provide, thereby denying legal marriage to out-of-state hopefuls.</p>
<p>Despite state attorney general Thomas F. Reilly’s view that state law forbids out-of-state couples from marrying in Massachusetts, Provincetown officials plan to issue such marriage licenses. Provincetown’s Board of Selectmen voted in early April to permit marriages for out-of-state residents who complete a form stating that their marriage doesn’t break any laws.</p>
<p>On April 11, 2004, the town manager, Keith Bergman, said that town officials would not investigate to verify the home states, or backgrounds listed for gay couples on marriage license applications. Bergman said: “We’ve never been the marriage police with heterosexual couples, and we’re not about to start with same-sex couples.”</p>
<p>Laurence Pizer, the town clerk in Plymouth for the last 12 years, and a past president of the Massachusetts Town Clerks Association, said that he plans to accept the signed “Notice of Intent” from same-sex couples, just as he accepts the word of opposite-sex couples.</p></li>
</ul>
<strong>Married in Canada or Civil Unions in Vermont</strong>
<ul>
<li>The legally status is not yet clear for same-sex partners to marry in Massachusetts if they already have a Vermont “Civil Union.”</li>
<li>It is clear that same-sex partners may <em>not</em> marry in Massachusetts if they already have a Vermont “Civil Union” with <em>different</em> partners. Civil Unions are a marriage-like, legal status under Vermont state law.</li>
<li>If you have a Civil Union and the relationship is over, you will need to have a dissolution procedure. Courts outside of Vermont have usually not been willing to dissolve a Union. The only clearly established way to dissolve a Union is by living in Vermont for one year and filing for dissolution. Failure to do so before marrying in Massachusetts could make you guilty of the crime of bigamy.</li>
<li>If you were married in Canada, you are legally married no matter where you go. It may be possible to marry the same person again, although it is uncertain what the legal effect would be.</li>
</ul>
<strong>Massachusetts Divorce</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>At least one partner must live in Massachusetts for one year before filing.</strong> If, however, the court believes that the party has moved there simply in order to get divorced, it may not grant the divorce.</li>
<li>It takes at least six months for a divorce to become final, but generally takes longer. Trying to obtain a divorce in other states may be more difficult. If you live in a state that does not honor your marriage — which may not be determined until requesting something usually triggered by a marriage license — the state courts will also be unlikely to grant you a divorce.</li>
<li>Even if the state does not recognize your marriage and will not grant you a legal divorce, you may still be held responsible for the obligations of marriage, such as providing financial support or being held responsible for your former partner’s debts.</li>
</ul>

<td><strong>Warning</strong></td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>We <strong>cannot</strong> recommend that <em>non-</em>resident couples get married in Massachusetts, primarily because of the year residency that is required to divorce.</p>
<p>Because many states refuse to recognize a same-sex marriage license, should a couple have a falling-out, divorce would likely be impossible unless one of the partners moved to Massachusetts.</p>
<p>Besides the emotional reasons to dissolve a no-longer-functioning union, there are legal entanglements to consider. For instance, should one of the partners form a new relationship, they would not be able to sign up their new partner for workplace benefits. Most employers require an affidavit that stipulates that the partners are not married to anyone else or have another domestic partner.</p>
<p>Another complication that may occur stems from the likelihood that Massachusetts licensing officers <em>may</em> require an affidavit declaring that marriage is <em>not</em> prohibited by the state laws where the couple resides. As of April 13, 2004, the towns of Provincetown and Plymouth have gone on record saying that they will honor out-of-state marriage requests</p>
<p><em>First:</em> All states explicitly or implicitly forbid legal marriage for same-sex couples. That means that it is impossible to get an affidavit saying that it was legal.</p>
<p><em>Second:</em> Even if a state’s laws did not explicitly exclude same-sex couples, it is highly unlikely any judge or state agency would be authorized, or feel empowered to issue, an affidavit declaring that a same-sex marriage was actually legal in their state.</p>
<p>It is also worth considering that because many state laws forbid recognition of another state’s same-sex legal marriage license, a civil license would have little or no legal meaning.</p>
<p><em><strong>Further warning:</strong></em></p>
<p>It is possible that a Massachusetts couple would get married, then move elsewhere. While it is certain that the Federal system would refuse to recognize the license, it is impossible to predict whether a state would also refuse.</p>
<p>Suing states for recognition is a highly expensive process, often taking years. Also, it should not be attempted without consulting national gay organizations with experience and knowledge of individual state law. The possibility of putting more “bad” law — that is, negative rulings — in place is extremely high, which could further entrench anti-marriage, anti-gay laws.</p>
