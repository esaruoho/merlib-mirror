### How do you describe yourself politically?

</div>

<div class="content">

Most tests of ideology identify me either as a leftist libertarian or as a libertarian leftist. These scores tend to come about from my strong support for social freedom and civil liberties, combined with my values in economic policy, which support mutual aid and an end to exploitative economic structures, while opposing government economic interventions such as protectionistic trade tariffs and corporate welfare. The counterbalancing aspects of my beliefs on economic issues put me on the libertarian/leftist border. In reality, my overall ideology in politics could be described as *radical feminist and anti-authoritarian Leftist*. I favor both community structures based on mutual aid and workers' control of their own labor, and also a massive roll-back of the State and its regulation. There are many needs which are best served by the community as a whole, and I strongly oppose the exploitative structures set up in our current society by the class of managers, landlords, financiers, and others who profiteer off of the impoverishment of working women and men. Furthermore, in the current situation where the government is one of the primary forms of communal organization that we have, I will provisionally support social welfare measures such as TANF, food stamps, Section VIII housing, and the like, as a stop-gap measure to stand in for what should be accomplished through community-based mutual aid. However, I want to work towards a world where the solutions to these situations take place between the people themselves in a spirit of mutuality, rather than through the intervention of a hegemonic government.

In terms of national politics, I most strongly identify with the [Green Party](http://www.greenpartyus.org/) and the [Libertarian Party](http://www.lp.org/). I support the independent party movement as a whole and am very interested in [ballot reforms](http://www.igc.org/cvd/ "Ballot reform resources at Center for Voting and Democracy") such as loosening ballot access restrictions and Instant Runoff Voting, which would increase independent party influence in American politics and allow independent parties to work in more constructive alliances with the two major parties. Within the Republocratic Party, I tend to sympathize with Democrats more than Republicans, and on certain key issues (abortion access, gay liberation, etc.) will vote for Democrats over Greens or Libertarians as an emergency measure against the Republicans.

Some of my hot buttons are violence against women, gay rights, and religious liberties. On these topics I am respectively a radical feminist, strongly gay-positive, and unabashedly an [atheist](atheism/index.html) and an advocate of the complete freedom of religious practice - or non-practice - from government control. However, at the same time I am in many ways more concerned with the cultural politics of these issues rather than in legislation. In the first place, with the exception of gay rights, most of the overt problems in legislation have been solved; the disenfranchisement of and violence against women, in particular, is no longer endorsed, but rather merely ignored. Moreover, I look seriously askance at the claim that our present government has - or will have in the near future - the best interests of women or gays at heart.

<tr>
<th scope="row">Gun control</th>
<td><p>I am deeply troubled by most Leftists' complacent acceptance of gun control legislation. The way that gun control legislation works is by removing guns, and the access to guns, from the hands of everyone but those licensed by the government - the police, the FBI, the BATF, and so on. This quickly creates a pattern in which people become more and more dependent on the government for everyday keeping of the peace, and also less and less able to defend themselves against abuses of governmental power (which have become more and more frequent through the corrosive effects of the <a href="#drugs">war on drugs</a>). As opponents of unjust power, we should be very concerned when the powerful have all the weapons and those over whom they have power have none to defend themselves. Further, the rhetoric about evil gun-toting criminals shooting up our kids is rather clearly a form of coded racism, stigmatizing young Black men once again in order to push through restrictive government policies (which is very reminiscent of the <a href="#drugs">War on Drugs</a>).</p>
<p>I very much understand the role that widespread availablity of guns has in the tragic number of deadly gun accidents and the use of guns in violence against women. These are pressing issues to be addressed. However, abridging the ability of citizens to defend themselves is not the way to address it.</p>
<p>Finally, states which provide concealed-carry permits for handguns should immediately switch from <strong>may-issue</strong> to <strong>shall-issue</strong> provisions, if they have not already. "May-issue," under which the decision to issue the permit is at the discretion of local law enforcement rather than specified according to law, simply grants arbitrary power to the local government to give or take away the ability to defend oneself, as they see fit. There are many fine men and women in local law enforcement; however, I do not trust the institution with this potentially corrosive power.</p></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th scope="row">Prosecution of rape cases</th>
<td>A woman's sexual history should never be brought up, on pain of a mistrail and contempt of court for the offending attorney, unless that history in some way establishes the likelihood of the woman to falsify charges (e.g., repeated charges of rape which were later found to be false). The prospect of humiliating--and irrelevant--examination regarding sexual history is one of the most preventable factors that can stop women from seeking legal solutions for a rape.</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th scope="row">Punishment for rapists</th>
<td><p>By "rape" I mean all forms of sexual assault, whether they are governed by laws concerning "rape," "sexual battery," "aggravated sodomy," <a href="#ageofconsent">"child molestation,"</a> or what have you. Rape is a form of torture and must be treated as a serious crime, for the safety of all women and girls, and for the security of the survivor, whether this comes through the civil or the criminal process. I support the Violence Against Women Act's provision allowing rape survivors to sue their attackers for reparations, even if the criminal courts have failed to give them justice. Since the Supreme Court unconscionably struck down that clause of the law, I support implementing equivalent versions of it at the state level as long as SCOTUS refuses to recognize the proprietary of federalizing this as a civil rights issue. If the case is pressed through the criminal process, rape should be punished to the maximum allowable under the law. Since I <a href="#deathpenalty">oppose the death penalty,</a> I do not call for rapists to be executed, but anyone convicted of rape should be imprisoned for the rest of their lives without the possibility of parole.</p>
<p>Whenever I propose this, there are a few major objections that come up.</p>
<p>Some object that we are facing a severe the prison overcrowding crisis (both here in Alabama and across the nation), and we simply don't have the room to keep rapists in prison. However, the only reason we face the prison overcrowding crisis is <a href="#drugs">the insane "War on Drugs"</a>, and the government stops wasting prison space to lock up thousands of harmless drug "offenders," there will be no problem with finding room for real criminals. Some object that too many innocent men will be convicted of rape based on "he said, she said" allegations, and we will be dealing out the harshest punishment other than execution to men who have done nothing wrong. The truth, however, is that evidentiary standards in rape trials are not any different from those in any other criminal trial, and in fact the false report rates for sexual assault, which research shows to be about 2%, are the same as the false report rates for any other form of violent crime. Furthermore, punishment for a conviction is based on the principle of what should be done to someone who <em>has</em> committed the offense, and is not weighted by the possibility of innocence. Are innocent people convicted of murder? Yes, but we don't go around saying that convicted murderers should receive only a few years in the pen. Rather, we establish an appeals process to vindicate those who have been wrongly convicted.</p>
<p>Finally, some object that some object that draconian punishment for sexual offenses can be cruel and unusual punishment for certain acts which could come under the laws governing sexual assault (for example, cases in which parents take revenge on a slightly older intimate partner for the fact that their son or daughter is sexually active). My response to this is twofold.</p>
<p>First, there are certain "crimes" classed as "sexual assault" today which are not assaults and should not be crimes at all. Specifically, <a href="#ageofconsent">poorly designed laws concerning child sexual abuse</a>, criminalize some non-coercive sex by framing the matter in terms of some magical "age of consent" rather than in terms of coercive power difference based on age. These laws should be redesigned on the basis of age differential so that they include all cases of child rape but exclude consensual sex between adolescents with neglible age differences. Once this field of law has been cleared out of the way, some will still object that real sexual offenses which are nevertheless minor are being punished in an inhumane manner - for example, there was a recent case in which a boy was convicted and listed as a registered sex offender for having grabbed a young woman's breast on a bet. Surely, they argue, he should not be thrown in prison for the rest of his life for an adolescent prank?</p>
<p>Well, here's the thing. I am not now, nor do I intend to be, in the business of distinguishing which types of sexual assault constitute "real" trauma and which ones the State can safely push to one side. That's a bullshit notion that's been used constantly against women and girls to delegitimize their suffering and let rapists off the hook (rape is rape, regardless of which body parts were involved). Is every rape equally traumatizing? Certainly not, but I am not about to stand for laws which pass judgment on how "deserving" a survivor is of State protection. Another popular bullshit notion is the idea that there's nothing really wrong with going around forcibly groping young women--no matter how much we may chant the mantra "boys will be boys," that <em>is</em> an act of aggressive sexual assault. It <em>is</em> a crime which should not be tolerated. If we excuse the "little" acts of sexual assault then what kind of message are we sending about what acceptable male behavior is towards women and sexuality? What kind of message are we sending about the "big" acts of sexual assault?</p>
<p>We should not be afraid of the prisons filling up with high schoolers and frat boys. If we create an environment in which it is clear that invasion of another person's body will <em>not</em> be tolerated, then I expect that we boys, being rational creatures with brains, will be able to adapt to the situation and choose not to act as though being born with a dick gave us an entitlement to force people into sexual contact without their consent. If you're really all that worried, then I'll be glad to accept a solution where, in the case of a conviction, the <em>survivor</em> is given the choice whether to ameliorate the punishment or carry it to its fullest extent. I have no problem with empowering survivors to say how much they were traumatized and what kind of action is needed to deal with the situation.</p></td>
</tr>
<tr id="ageofconsent">
<th scope="row">Age of consent</th>
<td><p>There is no teenager in the world who, at midnight on their 16th or 18th birthday, magically went from ignorance and irresponsibility, to complete maturity in the decision to have sex. Laws which simply draw an arbitrary line in time as the "age of consent" are deeply flawed. In practice, such poorly designed laws are sometimes abused by parents to take revenge on a slightly older intimate partner for the fact that their son or daughter is sexually active. However, child sexual abuse is rampant in this society and we must take forceful action to fight back against it. Child rape is and must be recognized as a serious crime.</p>
<p>The best way to deal with this is to realize that the issue is one of power differential based on age, not some fixed point where a child magically transforms into an adult and becomes capable of consenting to sex. Therefore, an age of full consent should be set at the age of majority (generally 18 around here), and when at least one of the people involved in the sexual act is below that age, the question of whether it constitutes sexual coercion or not should be based on the age differences between them. Alabama actually already kind of does this, by creating a window between age 12 and age 16 where the question of statutory rape is based on whether the older person is at least 4 years older than the younger. Thus, for example, it would not be a crime in itself for a couple to have consensual sex when one is 15 and the other 16, but it would be a crime for a 25 year old to have sex with a 13 year old. As it happens, I think that four years is a bit too wide of a window (for example, it not should be legal for a 17 year old to have sex with a 13 year old - there is too much space for coercion). However, the basic structure of the law is good.</p></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th scope="row">Sexual education in schools</th>
<td><p>Should be more responsible, earlier, and more extensive. Basic sex ed should start as early as fifth grade. Contraception, abortion, disease risks, masturbation, homosexuality, and other issues should be covered in an objective, descriptive manner; common misconceptions should be explicitly raised and deflated, and the course should be devoid of ethical advisement. The information is the school's department. The guidance is the parents' department. Abstinence-only sex ed is irresponsible, useless, and theocratic nonsense.</p></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<th scope="row">Same-sex marriages</th>
<td><p>Lately I've become more and more suspicious of both sides of the gay marriage debate. Those against, of course, are by and large bigots and never had my consideration. However, many of those in support of gay marriage - including folks like Jonathan Rauch and, <a href="marriage.html" title="Charles W. Johnson, &quot;Civil Rights and Family Values: Defending Homosexual (and Heterosexual) Marriage&quot;
An old essay of mine on gay marriage, which is quite assimilationist and glorifies marriage way too much.">at one time, myself</a> - are arguing for it on the basis of a really assimilationist and subtly heterosexist ethic.</p>
<p>The whole enterprise becomes very distasteful when the debate falls into something like "Let us marry, so that we can act like a bunch of straights! Let us have the chance to be Soccer Mom Queens!" As I've become more of a radical feminist, my sympathy for marriage as an institution has also been undermined sharply, and I've come to believe much less than I once did that a wholesale broadening of those who can marry would really be such a good thing after all. I do still think that as long as heterosexual couples are given the privileges of marriage, it is a basic point of civil rights and justice that gays and lesbians be allowed to marry, and be extended all the legal benefits of married couples. And such a move could very well change the institution of marriage for the better, since women marrying women and men marrying men could seriously undermine the traditional understanding of marriage as a highly structured, patriarchal relationship in which a man acquires power over a woman. But, fundamentally, neither the government, nor an insurance company, nor anyone else has <strong>any</strong> moral right to extend or deny privileges to me on the basis of who I'm sleeping with - or not sleeping with. People can have bonding ceremonies all they want, but <strong>the legal privileges of marriage must be abolished.</strong></p>

<th scope="row">The Military</th>
<td><p>The military machine in the United States is out of control and must be slashed as drastically as possible as quickly as conceivable. Military spending consumes the largest portion of our budget by far with the exception of Social Security and debt repayments - and the majority of the national debt was racked up on military spending. Our raging militarism and imperialistic warmongering abroad is a manifestation of the same system of violent domination of women, children, and minorities that pervades our daily life at home. Not surprisingly, the institution of the military also produces huge numbers of complaints of brutalizing women - both foreign civilians and women within its own ranks.</p>
<p>It is certainly true that we need an organized, trained force to defend citizens from aggression by foreign nations. But we don't need an imperial war machine - especially not in a country with as strategic a geographical location as the United States (with two borders, both of them friendly, and extensive oceans between us and most major states). We need a lightweight border guard, a strong but much smaller Navy and Air Force, and a strong system of open defensive alliances with other countries. But more than anything we need a new approach to international relations which isn't organized around the threat of massive violence. And we need to address the cultural politics of violence and subjugation at home which enable the politics of militarism and imperialism abroad.</p></td>

<th scope="row">Public education</th>
<td><p>Government-sponsored education has done a lot of good in spreading the goal of a humane education to all citizens, regardless of their socioeconomic background, and both the basic education of children and the ongoing education of adults is a legitimate communal need. However, government schooling has often done a great deal of harm. For example, through history classes, it has served as a source for indoctrination into the elites' view of history, its conflicts, and its driving forces; this alone has often paralyzed our citizens' ability to understand the nature of class divisions, sexual politics, and other important issues in our modern-day scene. Through government-written civics classes, the true nature of our governmental system and the citizens' role in it has been distorted so as to glorify our government and encourage people to only work within a narrowly defined set of options for change (find a single issue, write some letters, vote for the whichever of the two major party candidates comes closest to your opinion), and has failed to provide us with the necessary skills for serious participation in our own community governance.</p>
<p>As with some other programs which attempt to serve legitimate communal needs, I provisionally support the government sponsorship of education, but I want to move towards a system in which education is provided by the co-operative efforts of the community, including co-operative learning workgroups, one-on-one tutoring by parents, peers, or experts in the community, individual study, and any number of other options.</p>
<p>One immediate reform that must be taken with regard to education is to make established public education institutions more autonomous from professional politicians and create a space for greater participation by teachers, students, and other direct stakeholders. For example, in Alabama, the school is often micromanaged and mismanaged by the Board of Trustees, which is composed more or less entirely of politically-connected Good Old Boys, including a member of the state Senate and a member of the state House of Representatives. Other members are generally good friends, and sometimes have direct financial connections with one another, creating rampant cronyism on the Board. The Board is appointed by the state legislature, and the members routinely draw on their political cronies in Montgomery to gain and secure their position on the Board. In place of this, we need to enact a system in which faculty, student, and staff representatives are given a major role in the governance of the University, and in which Board members are banned from simultaneously serving on the Board of Trustees <em>and</em> in elected office. By giving those who work in the University, rather than the professional politicians in Montgomery, more control over the University, we can begin to work towards a more open, autonomous, and democratic model of education.</p>
