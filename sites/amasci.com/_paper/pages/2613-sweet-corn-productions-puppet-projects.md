<p><strong>Demian’s Puppet Work</strong></p>
<p>My use of puppets started — when I was about eight years old — with a Jerry Mahoney plaster head doll that had a movable mouth, and a Howdy Doody marionette. I also periodically strung up dolls, as well as made my own hand and rod figures.</p>
<p>In 1969, I toured with the Niccolo Marionettes out of New York City. The show was Hiawatha, based ever so loosly on the Longfellow poem. The figures were Sicilian-style, one-third life-size, made by Nick.</p>
<p>Racing from town-to-town, and from state-to-state, three of us ran the show. John Cunningham and Olga (forgot her last name) were excellent actors and puppeteers.</p>
<p>John drove the rattletrap van to all our engagements with the radio, full-blast, to keep awake on the long drives. I learned to catch up on sleep during these times — lying between John and Olga — with my butt on the motor, my back lying on the storage trunks and flats, and my feet hanging behind the clutch. Not exactly a “Magic Fingers”-type of bed.</p>
<p>Here is a photo of “Little One,” from the show <a href="sw-d08.html"><strong>The Voice of an Angel: A Castrato Remembers</strong>.</a> It was shot at the combined ASIFA-NW (animator’s association) and Puppeteers of Puget Sound meeting, May 21, 2004.</p>

<p><strong>Demian’s Puppet Heros</strong></p>
<p>My interest in puppets began with 50s TV children’s programming. I was infected by such shows as “Howdy Doody,” which always struck me as being noisy and rude. Other shows had greater use of imagination, such as “Time for Beany” (by Looney Toon animator Bob Clampett), “Kukla, Fran and Ollie,” “Foodini the Great” (and Pinhead), “Johnny Jupiter,” and “Rootie Kazootie” (actually another rude one).</p>

In 1969, my friend Bill Skirski (another puppet aficionado) and I met one of the Bunin brothers (creators of Foodini). It was either Morey or Lou, I am not sure, but think it was Morey. He graciously showed us Foodini, who “does nothing but hang in the closet all day.”
<p>Thrilled to find fans who still remembered (and cherished) the show, we got the grand tour of their office and workshop. We not only saw the original hand puppets, we saw the secret workings of a video process the Bunins had created. “Aniforms” allowed a TV cartoon line image to respond directly, and personally, to an audience.</p>
<p>We were told that they intended to take this as a show to shopping malls and the like. I read later that it was used on various children’s TV shows such as “Captain Kangaroo,” and “The Surprise Show.”</p>
<p>According to Rhett Bryson, “Morey Bunin collaborated with David Seville to produce the puppets of Alvin and the Chipmunks. … The Bunins also made puppets who appear on the opening credits of the Ziegfeld Follies (MGM 1946). Bunin did many characters for early television commercials such as ‘Bucky Beaver’ for Ipana toothpaste.”</p>
<p>Almost all puppet work I saw in my youth was through TV and film. I recall only one, live, professional puppet show, sometime in the 50s in Massachusetts, that featured a wonderful interpretation of Dukas’ “The Sorcerer’s Apprentice.” It utilized black light and bubbles (for underwater), which are effects I have since used in many of my own shows.</p>
<p>My favorite vents were Shari Lewis (with Lambchop and Charlie Horse), and the incomparable Paul Winchell (with Jerry Mahoney).</p>
<p>In the world of marionettes, the top-of-the-line was Bil and Cora Baird. Their work graced various TV specials, and were used in many of the Bell Telephone Science Film Series shown in many public schools.</p>
<p>Decades later, the work of Jim Henson, Frank Oz and company, captured my attention.</p>
<p>The most sublime puppeteer’s work I ever saw was done by Kukla and Ollie’s creator and operator Burr Tilstrum. In the 60s on “That Was the Week that Was,” a satirical news review, he did a silent portrayal of the brief lowering of the Berlin Wall, all performed with just his hands. Burr depicted the pain of relatives being ripped apart, the ecstasy of re-uniting, and the horror of being forcibly separated. By the end of the piece I was sobbing.</p>
