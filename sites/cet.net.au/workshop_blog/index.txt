 time travel &#8211; the future 

 Posted on August 12, 2010 by Rob 

 We have all had the experience of looking forward to a holiday or special event and being transported forward to that time. This is just one more example of approaching hypnotic experiences as if they are extensions of everyday experiences, so they become more accessible and easier to connect with. 
 Looking forward and becoming absorbed in it sounds to me much preferable to &#8220;age progression &#8220;! 
 And again, we are more interested to assist a client to some useful resource from their future if the missing experience doesn&#8217;t seem readily accessible from the present or past. 
 Some clients will be so stuck in resignation about their dilemma that there seems to be no possibility of resolution, so why would they bother? I have heard &#8220;I can&#8217;t imagine getting over this&#8221; or &#8220;I&#8217;ve always been like this, and expect I always will&#8221; or &#8220;I&#8217;ve tried before, and I don&#8217;t expect this will work either. 
 In situations like these, the future can be a magical place to visit, experience what&#8217;s missing, and in the experience of experiencing, the resource becomes instantly accessible, to everyone&#8217;s delight.

 time travel &#8211; the past 

 Posted on July 28, 2010 by Rob 

 We have all had the experience of looking at a school photo or some holiday snaps and being transported back to that time. When we approach hypnotic experiences as if they are extensions of everyday experiences, they become more accessible and easier to connect with. 
 Visiting a memory and becoming absorbed in it sounds to me much preferable to &#8220;age regression&#8221;! 
 Also, in this solution approach, we are more interested to assist a client to some useful resource from their past rather than exploring some supposedly causal trauma. To assist someone to re-experience an ability to survive some traumatic event is likely to be much more useful than simply reliving it, with its possibility of re-traumatising them. 
 How to revisit any past experience will be guided by the question &#8220;What&#8217;s missing?&#8221; for this client. 
 If there is an overwhelming emotion associated with a trauma, returning to a time before the even can allow for an opportunity to gather up some resources, which may involve additional people, perhaps even their adult self, so they can then weather the situation and come out stronger. It can also be helpful to allow the client to be a disconnected observer, so there is sufficient emotional disconnection. This can avoid the paralysis of the overwhelming emotion, and create an experience of completion &#8211; &#8220;I survived it!!&#8221;. 
 If there is an emotion of helplessness, help can be imagined; if injustice, then justice; if unfairness, some sort of revenge. Whatever is missing for any individual client can be  imagined, pretended or enacted, and this &#8220;unreal&#8221; experience is experienced as if it were to be real, and so assists in healing.

 connections and disconnections 

 Posted on July 22, 2010 by Rob 

 I find it helpful to think of all problems as containing disconnections from resources or connections with unwanted experiences. 
 This allows us to ask what connections are missing that we can help the client to connect with, and what connections are unhelpful that would be useful to disconnect from. 
 For me, this is way preferable than exploring &#8220;dissociative phenomena&#8221;. Simpler, more likely to be useful for a client, and adding to our effectiveness &#8211; something we all want, specially our clients! 
 To paraphrase Erickson &#8220;What we experience depends on how we direct our attention&#8221;. 
 We can then assist our clients to direct their attention away from problem experiences by disconnection or dissociation, and towards preferred, solution experiences through connections or associations.

 experiences in hypnosis 

 Posted on July 14, 2010 by Rob 

 Just as we can think of hypnosis as an extension of the common everyday trance anyone can experience by focusing and becoming absorbed in reading a book, watching a movie, looking out a window &#8230; we can appreciate some of the complex hypnotic experiences as an extension of common everyday happenings. 
 When I first came across catalepsy I thought it was very weird until I began to notice this apparent automatic pausing half way through a variety of events. A person can say &#8220;What was that word?&#8221; and as they search, there is a natural immobility, a pause in breathing and blinking &#8230; and then life continues, often with no recognition that the &#8220;pause button&#8221; had been pressed then released. 
 Other so-called complex and advanced hypnotic &#8220;phenomena&#8221; like age regression and progression, anaesthesia and analgesia, amnesia &#8211; so many complex Greek-derived jargon words which can confuse and overwhelm &#8211; can be viewed as extension of ordinary experiences &#8211; more soon.

 early learning 

 Posted on July 5, 2010 by Rob 

 Seeing what&#8217;s missing can help; experiencing what&#8217;s missing can help; stories to enhance the experiencing of what&#8217;s missing can help; and &#8230; sometimes, even that seems like too big a leap of faith. 
 In this situation, stories about early learning can bridge the chasm, and make a small beginning possible, getting past the paralysis that overwhelm produces. 
 When we know what a client likes, we can safely explore explicitly or implicitly, how they learnt that. 
 &#8220;When you first learnt to [do what you like] you [had the problem] and as you learnt, you overcame that difficulty, and [found the missing resource]&#8220;. 
 This can allow such a respectful, and gentle beginning to the process of moving from problem to solution.

 stories can help 

 Posted on July 1, 2010 by Rob 

 Sometimes a client can see the missing resource, and recognising it is enough. Sometimes it helps to let someone &#8220;sit in&#8221; the experience of it, to &#8220;soak it up&#8221;, which is where hypnosis comes into its own. 
 Other people can see what&#8217;s missing, and not see the possibility for them. 
 Stories can help here. 
 If we say &#8220;I had a client, not unlike yourself, who solved their problem&#8221;, our client has the chance to discover that they are not a freak &#8211; there are others! Also, if someone else got over their problem, the possibility for this client to get over their problem is based on a shared reality, and moves beyond being a mere hope. 
 Other stories about life, pets, children, nature etc. can also help to make this possibility more real. These stories are likely to be most relevant if they relate to this client&#8217;s individual area of likes. If they like fishing, stories about solving the problem that brought them to us in a fishing context will make sense. If they like cycling, then stories about cycling will be easiest to connect with. 
 If you&#8217;re interested, next time you&#8217;re with a client, find a story from your clinical experience that parallels the client&#8217;s experience, making sure that the story ends with a good resolution, and then see if you can create another parallel story related to the client&#8217;s likes.

 connecting with the missing resource 

 Posted on June 26, 2010 by Rob 

The question compels … “How can we assist a client to connect with this resource?”. Some people when they see the possibility of a connection make the connection and have a direct experience of their solution, and that’s all that’s needed. And, some see the possibility of a connection and we can assist them by making the connection for them … “Speaking in front of a crowd is just the same as watching your garden grow” 
 Sometimes the connection can be unclear for us, so we can off the idea “I wonder what connections you can make, are already making, have already made&#8221;. 
 The resource is always there, in my experience, and connecting is an expected outcome, although of course sometimes it’s not obvious, or even seen as a possibility. 
 Our mood of expectancy can be so pivotal in assisting the client to make their connection.

 experiencing the missing resource 

 Posted on June 24, 2010 by Rob 

 When we know what we’re looking for, the next question concerns where we might find it. 
 If we invite a client to become focused and absorbed in an experience they like – walking in nature, reading, gardening – and then comment on the physiological changes we can observe – blinking, breathing, stillness, etc. – we can explore, with the client, the experiences they are now absorbed in, and look for the missing resource or experience. 
 My experience is that we will always find that missing resource or experience in their likes, and because the experience comes from the client, it is readily available, readily accessible, easily useable.

 what&#039;s missing? 

 Posted on June 20, 2010 by Rob 

 “If you don’t know where you’re going, you may end up in a different place” is an old Irish saying, so if we have the question “What’s missing?”, we are more likely to find it. 
 What’s missing for someone to have a problem is predictably present in their “likes” so “likes” provide a place to explore and expect to find “what’s missing” so that when we assist the client to connect with this, a solution can be created by making this connection.

 accessing resources 

 Posted on June 18, 2010 by Rob 

 We also saw that exploring “likes” is a delightful way of predictably accessing resources! So … we can ask a client to recall or imaging doing the activity that they like, and then … invite them to focus, become absorbed and we can comment on those bodily changes that we can observe to enhance them. 
 This not only provides a way into the hypnotic experience, but also generates an experience of experiencing their likes, their enjoyment, their resourcefulness …
